First, the shape of the game, because it was not clean and nobody’s pretending otherwise.

“The Raiders scored on their first two drives. The Seahawks needed a while to settle, and a couple of mistakes kept the scoreboard closer than the play deserved.”

And his one-word verdict on the whole thing: GOOD.

“It was not clean and it still ended in a win with a lot to like.”

Twelves, that’s the right word, and here’s what makes it remarkable — I want you to hear the full list of who was missing on offense.

“With BOTH STARTING TACKLES OUT and the STARTING LEFT GUARD OUT, the team ran the football well. That is a line in tatters producing a rushing attack that opened up the rest of the offense.”

Both tackles. And the left guard.

Charles Cross out. Abe Lucas out. Quenton Nelson out after knee surgery.

And the analyst’s conclusion: “when a depleted group does that, the result says more about the SCHEME and the EFFORT than about the names on the depth chart.”

That’s exactly right. And it matches what Mike Macdonald said about the man who filled in — Bobby Hart: “the best comment I can say is, I DIDN’T REALIZE HE WAS OUT THERE.”

For an offensive lineman, that’s the highest compliment in the sport. You only notice a tackle when something goes wrong.

NOW — the third quarter, and let me put real context on what happened.

Over one hundred twenty yards in a single quarter. Over one hundred seventy on the day.

And the analyst made the case it should have been bigger: “more than thirty, maybe more than forty yards came off the board because of what I consider HORRIFIC calls — penalties against defenders down the field, and an OFFENSIVE PASS INTERFERENCE call on him that I thought was EGREGIOUS. He should have had two or three more catches in this game, and he almost had a 200-yard day.”

Twelves, I’ll be careful here, because I don’t love building a case on officiating. But I will point out one thing that’s genuinely funny.

Three days before this game, Mike Macdonald was asked about covering tight ends and said this about physicality at the top of routes: “we won’t call them push-offs, but I HAVE MY OWN OPINION ABOUT IT.”

And then his own receiver gets flagged for offensive pass interference.

So Seattle’s head coach thinks it’s never called when it’s done to him, and a national analyst thinks it was wrongly called when his receiver did it. Welcome to football.

But the analyst’s bigger claim is the one worth sitting with: “I think he is the BEST WIDE RECEIVER IN THE LEAGUE, and the gap is NOT CLOSE in my eyes.”

And twelves, look at who’s been saying this.

Cooper Kupp — a Super Bowl MVP — said “I think he’s one of, if not the best receiver in the league right now,” while being invited to complain about his own targets.

Mike Macdonald after this game: “he’s a BEAST,” and then listed every phase — “run solutions, quick game, catch-and-runs, the intermediate and the NINE BALLS, and then on extended plays he’s able to UNCOVER.”

And Drew Lock’s version, which is still my favorite line of the season: “Jax would tell you that if he’s doubled, HE’S STILL OPEN. That’s kind of been the funny line this year.”

He’s over twelve hundred yards. And he crossed a thousand in nine games — which, for context, is pace for one of the great receiving seasons in league history.

And the strategic point the analyst made is the one that actually matters for Seattle’s future: “when a team has a player who can take over a quarter, the defense has to ACCOUNT FOR HIM ON EVERY SNAP, and that OPENS SPACE FOR EVERYBODY ELSE. It also gives the quarterback a SAFE, RELIABLE TARGET WHEN PLAYS BREAK DOWN.”

“When plays break down.” Hold that thought, because it’s about to become the story of the two touchdowns.

NOW — the best play of the game, and I’m thrilled he picked the same one I did.

“Cooper Kupp caught it, and the credit goes to Lock. The fact that he knew where the LINE OF SCRIMMAGE was, put on the BRAKES, and got the football out to Kupp was awesome.”

And twelves, Drew Lock explained this himself afterward, and the explanation is the whole thing:

“Kind of just like an INTERNAL FEELING. You’ve taken a million reps, scrambled maybe half a million, kind of have a good sense of where that line of scrimmage is.”

There is no line painted on that grass. If he crosses it and throws, the touchdown is wiped out — illegal forward pass, five yards, loss of down.

And he can’t look down. He’s got men chasing him and his eyes are downfield.

So he hit the brakes based on a million repetitions telling his body where an invisible line was.

And then his decision: “once I stepped out of the tackle, I was like — all right, I’m going to SIT HERE. Because I didn’t think there was anyone really around me, and see if anybody works open.”

“I’m going to sit here.” The hardest thing to do when a play breaks. Everything in your body says run.

And then Cooper Kupp — in Lock’s words — “did a great job of GRABBING MY EYES and running across that back end line.”

Twelves, that’s the scramble drill, and Kupp publicly took responsibility for Seattle failing at it after the Bears loss. His words then: “we’ve got to do a better job of being able to get open and find ways to let him get the ball out of his hands when he does extend like that.”

He named his own failure in October. He scored on it in November.

AND NOW — the play that nobody outside that building understood, and the analyst nailed why it mattered.

“Shout out to Tory Horton, who made an INCREDIBLY SMART FOOTBALL PLAY to knock that ball back in — which let Seattle get the kickoff INSIDE OF THE TWO-MINUTE MARK.”

And his explanation: “in the closing minutes, EVERY SECOND MATTERS. Horton knocked that ball back in so that Seattle could get the kickoff inside of two minutes. And that detail affects HOW MUCH TIME THE OPPONENT HAS. Those are the details that do not show up in a highlight but DECIDE how the end of a game unfolds.”

Twelves, this is the single most underrated play of the entire afternoon, and when you put it next to what Mike Macdonald said, it gets even better.

Macdonald’s version: “Tory’s actually TRYING to catch that ball — albeit with ONE HAND — but an incredible play.” And then the key: “we TOLD them that was a situation going in. There’s a difference in catching it EIGHT YARDS DEEP IN THE END ZONE and making a play like that.”

So Seattle told its returners on Saturday that this exact situation might come up. And then the men who had to execute it weren’t even the regular guys — George Holani got hurt, which forced Seattle to take Jadarian Price off kickoff return. Macdonald: “those are guys that HAVEN’T NECESSARILY PRACTICED A BUNCH IN A WHILE.”

And one of them kept the ball in play, one-handed, deep in his own end zone, because he understood the clock.

And remember what Macdonald told us about kickoff value last week: “if you can get a kick stopped inside the 25 versus a touchback or coming out past the 40 — we’re talking ALMOST LIKE A POINT.”

He taught the value on Wednesday. They cashed it on Sunday.

NOW — the fumbles, and this analyst did the fair thing and separated them.

“Yes, he had a mistake — a FUMBLE when he was trying to SPIKE the football. THAT ONE WAS HIS.”

A spike fumble. Twelves, that’s trying to stop the clock and dropping the ball doing it. There’s no defending it and the analyst didn’t try.

“The SECOND fumble, however, I do not put on him. THE RUNNING BACK RAN RIGHT INTO HIM on the play. That is a MISCOMMUNICATION IN THE BACKFIELD, and Lock should not carry the blame for it.”

And that matches what Mike Macdonald said almost exactly: “we DID turn it over twice today, which — we want to be better.” And then about Lock: “those were REALLY EARLY IN THE PLAY.”

Early in the play. Not desperation throws. Mechanical, operational errors.

And twelves, I want to be straight with you: that ends Seattle’s streak. Drew Lock had gone four straight games without putting the ball on the ground or in the wrong hands. That’s over.

And the analyst named the other frustrations honestly too: “a couple of dumb decisions like HOLDING THE FOOTBALL TOO LONG and taking a sack, or trying to FORCE a ball into a spot. But overall, he played really well.”

NOW — the point in this breakdown I found most interesting, because it reframes the final score.

“There were turnovers that led to a Raiders touchdown. And there were points taken off the board for Seattle when the kicker MISSED A FIELD GOAL. If that kick goes through and Lock delivers that ball on time to Tory Horton, maybe you are talking about a THIRTY-ONE TO THIRTEEN game — and then we have a different conversation about the offense.”

Thirty-one to thirteen.

Twelves, that’s the version of this game that almost happened. A made field goal, one on-time throw to Tory Horton, and no turnover gifting Las Vegas a touchdown — and this is a blowout nobody is nitpicking.

Instead it’s twenty-one to thirteen and everybody’s arguing about the offense.

And look, I’ll be honest: the scoreboard is the scoreboard. You don’t get credit for the game you almost played. But it does tell you the gap between “this offense is broken” and “this offense is fine” is about three plays wide.

NOW — the observation in this whole breakdown that I think is the smartest thing anybody has said about this football team, and I want to give it its own moment.

“It is worth noticing HOW MANY OF THE BEST MOMENTS IN THIS GAME CAME FROM AWARENESS RATHER THAN ATHLETICISM. Lock knowing where the line of scrimmage was. Horton knocking the ball back in. Witherspoon timing his blitz. NONE OF THOSE PLAYS REQUIRED A MIRACLE. They required players to KNOW THE SITUATION AND RESPOND TO IT CORRECTLY. And that is the sign of a coaching staff that has PREPARED ITS PLAYERS WELL.”

Twelves, stop. Read that again.

Seattle’s three best plays in this football game were not athletic plays. They were KNOWLEDGE plays.

A quarterback who knew where an invisible line was. A receiver who knew what the game clock needed. A defensive back who knew when to time a blitz.

That is not talent. That is Tuesday and Wednesday and Thursday in a meeting room.

And it ties directly to what Mike Macdonald told us about how he runs his staff: “a lot of stuff we’re running is NOT STARTING IN MY HEAD — it’s starting in our staff’s head.” And: “you’re not just running one-off plays, it’s all kind of SEAMLESS.”

And Lock said the same thing about the building: “there’s ‘we want you to go be YOU. We believe in you.’ And when you keep getting that pressed upon you — that builds a confidence.”

You want to know why a team missing six starters keeps winning? That’s it. Everybody knows their job, including jobs that aren’t theirs.

NOW — the running game, and twelves, this analyst made a claim I need to handle carefully.

“Zach Charbonnet looked really good. The more I see, the more I think that the decision not to sign Kenneth Walker is looking smart — because Charbonnet has EARNED EVERY PENNY OF AN EXTENSION. I might be getting crazy, but I think HE MAY BE BETTER THAN WALKER.”

Okay. Let me be clear about what that is: that is one man’s opinion, and I’m presenting it as an opinion, not as news.

But I’ll tell you where I agree. The extension argument is real.

Because look at the timeline on Zach Charbonnet. He tore his ACL in January. Mike Macdonald told us before the Bears game that he “hasn’t had a live rep since the injury” — not one, in eight months. He played Monday night and got held under thirty yards, and a national analyst’s word for him was “RUST.”

Then against Arizona, Macdonald said: “definitely felt Zach in his POWER, and the ability to break tackles and own the sideline.”

And Drew Lock’s version is the one I’ll remember: “when CHARBS does his classic ‘YOU CAN’T TACKLE ME’ thing and shoves people to the ground, it’s pretty fun to watch. That’s kind of like a — oh, THERE it is.” And: “the LOOK IN HIS EYE when you come back to the huddle.”

Rusty to “there it is” in two weeks, coming off a torn ACL, behind a line missing both tackles and the left guard.

Yeah. I think that man gets paid.

And the numbers back the whole backfield: “together, the team averaged OVER FOUR YARDS PER CARRY with both of its starting running backs. That is really good, especially considering the line they were playing behind.”

Over four yards a carry with two backup tackles and no Quenton Nelson. His conclusion: “when your backs are both producing without your usual offensive line, THE SYSTEM IS WORKING.”

And Jadarian Price — twelves, this is a lovely detail: “for the first time in a very long time, somebody VULTURED A TOUCHDOWN from Charbonnet, and it was Price.”

That’s Jadarian Price’s FIRST CAREER TOUCHDOWN. And he took it off Zach Charbonnet’s plate to get it.

And Macdonald’s read on Price: “he has VISION, he has BURST.” Plus the key unlock he explained: “having Zach becoming healthier at halfback definitely helps us be able to do that.”

Charbonnet carrying the load is what frees Price to be a weapon. Two backs working, exactly as designed.

NOW — the defense, and twelves, this is where the game was won.

“Las Vegas had a good start. The Raiders scored on their first two drives, and AFTER THAT THEY REALLY DID NOT DO ANYTHING. That second half was ALL SEATTLE. The defense, I would say, ABSOLUTELY ATE FERNANDO MENDOZA ALIVE.”

And he gave the credit where Macdonald wouldn’t give it to himself: “the coaching staff made a couple of really good ADJUSTMENTS and MIKE MACDONALD DESERVES CREDIT for that. THAT WAS THE DIFFERENCE.”

And here’s the nuance I found most interesting, because it’s counterintuitive:

“The TOTAL YARDAGE DID NOT CHANGE MUCH between the halves, and the Raiders looked strong early — but once Seattle GOT PRESSURE on Mendoza, HE WAS A DIFFERENT QUARTERBACK.”

Twelves, that’s a really important observation. The Raiders kept gaining yards. They just stopped SCORING.

That’s a defense that bends and then slams the door — and it connects to the thing Macdonald said still needs fixing: “finishing the RED ZONE.” They did it this week.

And then the analyst’s explanation, which is the whole scouting report on a rookie:

“Veterans change when they are pressured, and ROOKIES CHANGE EVEN MORE. Once the Seahawks SPED HIM UP, the game belonged to them.”

“Sped him up.”

And twelves, this is exactly what the national panel predicted before this game. Their words: “Mendoza HOLDS ONTO THE FOOTBALL A LITTLE BIT TOO LONG. Takes too many sacks. That is not what you want to do against a Seahawks defense.”

And exactly what Macdonald said his plan was: “we’ll focus on us playing our brand of ball and TRY TO APPLY AS MUCH PRESSURE AS WE CAN.”

Predicted. Planned. Executed.

And credit where it’s due — the analyst was gracious about the kid: “if you are a Raiders fan, I think you should be EXCITED, because it was a good start to his career. I thought he looked good UNTIL Seattle got to him.”

Which is almost word for word what Macdonald said: “tough for a rookie quarterback in our league to play, just come in after not playing for nine — but I THOUGHT HE PLAYED WELL. He’s got a great future.”

NOW — the pass rush, and twelves, this list is the best news of the day.

“DeMarcus Lawrence was flying around making plays and PUTTING LICKS ON MENDOZA. He got a sack and had multiple other big hits.”

“DERICK HALL GOT HOME, which was huge. And BYRON MURPHY got home, which was huge too.”

“I was very impressed with DANTE FOWLER JUNIOR, who got a sack for the SECOND GAME IN A ROW.”

“DEVON WITHERSPOON had a couple of big hits on Mendoza, including a sack, and THE BLITZ TIMING WAS GREAT.”

Count that. Lawrence. Hall. Murphy. Fowler. Witherspoon. FIVE different men getting to the quarterback.

And twelves, every one of those names has a story.

DeMarcus Lawrence publicly said the red zone problem was partly his fault — his specific gap — and then made a tackle for loss against Arizona. Macdonald called that accountability “pretty special” and said “we definitely follow his lead.”

Derick Hall showed up on the injury report with a back problem two weeks ago. Now he’s getting home.

Dante Fowler — two straight games with a sack. And the analyst was honest about it: “yes, I understand that he was NOT BLOCKED on the play. He still went out there and made it.”

Fair. But look at what Macdonald said about him: “his skill set hasn’t been able to SHINE as much as we envisioned BECAUSE OF THE TYPE OF GAMES WE’VE BEEN IN. But we’ve been able to create more PASSING SITUATIONS in the last few weeks. This is MORE OF THE ROLE WE ENVISIONED for him.”

A pass rush specialist needs the other team to be passing. The defense got good enough to force that, and now he’s producing.

And Devon Witherspoon’s sack — Macdonald told the full story: “he was HOT. I think we called one earlier, he didn’t get there but the ball was out — but HE WON HIS RUSH. So we were just like, all right, LET’S KEEP SENDING HIM.” And: “that’s our COACHES’ credit. They saw it, they ENCOURAGED ME to dial it up.”

A blitz that produced nothing the first time but won the matchup. So they called it again. That’s judging process over results, and it turned the game.

AND NOW — the take I have to push back on, respectfully.

“I certainly feel that DREW LOCK IS THE QB1. And when he is in there, GOOD THINGS HAPPEN. At the end of the day, good is good. And getting the football to Smith-Njigba through Lock is THE BEST PATH FORWARD for the Seahawks.”

Twelves, I understand the sentiment. Drew Lock is four and oh. He’s thrown two touchdowns in this game and has zero interceptions on the season. He gives this team, in the analyst’s words, “a spark — there is a FIRE and a PASSION there.”

All of that is true and I’ve said it myself.

But “Drew Lock is the QB1” is not what anybody inside that building is saying, and I’m going to keep reporting what they actually say.

Mike Macdonald, in the press conference after THIS game: “I think we have THREE REALLY GOOD QUARTERBACKS. Drew’s proven he’s a great quarterback in our league.” And then — listen to the word — “it’s great that OUR STARTER goes down and really nobody blinks an eye.”

“Our starter.” Singular. Still Sam Darnold.

And Drew Lock himself, in his own press conference, after his own two-touchdown game: “That’s the life of a backup. WE DON’T WANT TO BE OUT THERE. That’s what’s best for the team. OUR STAR NEEDS TO STAY HEALTHY. AND I LOVE SAM.”

The man the analyst is calling QB1 called Sam Darnold “our star” and said he loves him, unprompted, while celebrating his own win.

So here’s where I land, and it’s not a dodge — it’s the standard Macdonald himself set after this game:

“This will be a BIG WEEK. For where we’re at and for where Sam’s at coming back, YOU’RE GOING TO WANT HIM TO PRACTICE A SIGNIFICANT AMOUNT OF TIME FOR HIM TO PLAY. It’ll be clear AFTER THE BYE on where we’re at — and if he can get enough practice to go play.”

The question isn’t who’s better. It’s whether Sam Darnold can get a full practice week in during this bye.

Because Drew Lock has won these games on ONE DAY of throwing — and he confirmed it again: “did you only throw on Thursday again?” “Yeah.” TWO WEEKS IN A ROW.

He can do that because, in his words, “it’s the ONLY way I’ve been able to get ready for games” after eight years as a backup. Sam Darnold is not built that way. Starters need the week.

So the bye is the test. If Darnold practices fully, he plays at San Francisco. If he can’t, Lock keeps going. That’s not fence-sitting, twelves — that’s literally the policy the head coach announced.
