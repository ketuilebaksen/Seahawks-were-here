“I just asked him if I could share this.”

Seahawks Nation, that is Mike Macdonald, standing at a podium on Wednesday, asking permission before telling the world something about his quarterback. And then he told us.

“He cracked his rib on the second play of the game.”

The second play.

Not late. Not in the third quarter. The second snap of a Monday Night Football game against the Chicago Bears — and Sam Darnold played all four quarters and never told anybody.

Macdonald’s words: “So playing the whole game, without even making a name of it, and playing his tail off and competing — I just think it’s worth noting how tough this guy is.”

And then: “I wasn’t aware of the rib throughout the game until after.”

His own head coach didn’t know.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is 4-4. Sam Darnold is out Sunday. Drew Lock is starting. And twelves, this title is accurate — there is a bombshell about Sam Darnold in this press conference, and it makes him look better, not worse. Let’s go.

First, I want you to recalculate Monday night with this new information.

Sam Darnold went seventeen of thirty-four for 188 yards and two touchdowns on a cracked rib.

He threw a seven-yard touchdown to Cooper Kupp in the fourth quarter. He converted the two-point pass to Tory Horton that cut it to 20-17. He was sacked three times against a defense generating a thirty-one percent pressure rate — three times he got hit in the ribs that were already broken.

And then he hurt his quad.

Macdonald on when: “I think it was right at the end. I think his last drive.”

So read the sequence. Cracked rib on play number two. Quad injury on his final drive. And between those two moments, sixty minutes of football that nobody knew was being played by a man in that condition.

And here’s the exchange that tells you everything, and it got a laugh in the room.

A reporter asked which injury is actually keeping him out. Macdonald: “I think we could play through the rib.” Then, correcting himself and laughing: “He could. I’d be fine. I just — doesn’t affect me.”

And then the real answer: “I don’t want to speak for him, but right now it’s the quad that’s holding him out.”

The broken rib isn’t even the thing keeping Sam Darnold off the field on Sunday. He’d play on that.

And asked whether the rib affected his play: “Yeah, I mean, I’m sure it did. Your adrenaline’s going. He’s tough — but it’s hurt.”

Twelves, I have spent two weeks on this channel refusing to make videos about Sam Darnold wanting out of Seattle, being finished, being benched. Four separate times I told you the people inside that building never wavered on him.

This is why.

Macdonald’s summary: “He’s tough as nails.” And: “He’ll be back. He’ll be back sooner than later, I’m sure.”

And on how Darnold has handled a physically brutal season — the glute injury in Week 2, now this:

“It’s tough, you know. But I just got a lot of respect for how he goes through these things, because he goes through it. There’s no blink, there’s no flinch. It’s very matter-of-fact attacking mentality — which I think is reflective of the rest of our team, which I appreciate.”

“There’s no blink. There’s no flinch.”

Now the official announcement, in his own words.

“Sam’s going to be out this week and be week to week for Sam. He’s not going to go on IR. Plan is for Drew to start. Jalen will be ready to back up. Drew’s still working back from his elbow, so we’ll work through that process this week. So we’ll be getting both guys ready to play. Got full confidence in those guys, and we have a plan in place, and we’ll go execute.”

“Not going on IR” means a minimum of four games avoided. They expect him back sooner. And “getting both guys ready to play” is the part that should get your attention — that’s a head coach telling you he might need two quarterbacks on Sunday.

Now Drew Lock’s elbow — and the origin story is wonderfully, perfectly Seattle.

Asked when Lock hurt it, Macdonald said: “He got it — we practiced in the rain, and it got aggravated in a rain practice.”

A rain practice. In Seattle. In October. The starting quarterback for Sunday’s game hurt his throwing elbow practicing in the rain in the Pacific Northwest. You cannot write it better than that.

And the management plan: “He’s able to throw. How much, we’re working through that right now, but that’s the best way to put it. It’s a different injury than Sam had in like the playoffs. He’s going to throw the ball throughout the week, but we’re just going to manage it. He’s not going to take 8,000 throws.”

“He’s not going to take 8,000 throws.”

That’s the whole week in one sentence. Lock throws, but they’re counting. And he was limited in practice Wednesday and limited again Thursday — no progress, but no regression either.

And Macdonald confirmed the plan: “They’ll be splitting reps for a certain amount of time. I think we just got to take it day by day... Jalen will take some practice reps as well.”

And then he said: “We might be adding a quarterback.”

They did. On Wednesday, Seattle signed Adrian Martinez — a UFL Most Valuable Player and championship game MVP with the Birmingham Stallions, who ran for forty-five touchdowns in college — to the practice squad. He spent time with the 49ers, which matters because Seattle’s offense and San Francisco’s offense come from the same coaching family. He already speaks a dialect of the language.

A coach said he might add a quarterback, and two days later there was a quarterback. That’s it working properly.

Now the news nobody is talking about, and it changes Sunday.

Quenton Nelson did not practice on Thursday.

He was limited on Wednesday with a knee. Thursday: did not practice.

That’s a step backward on a six-day week, and it matters enormously — because of a question Macdonald answered forty minutes earlier in this same press conference.

A reporter asked him directly: with Grey Zabel moving to the right side, would it be better to put Anthony Bradford — who has forty career starts at right guard — in there instead, or does Zabel need the live reps?

Macdonald’s answer: “I think we had to make the decision and go, so we’re committed to that. I mean, we’re confident in AB to go in and play good football for us. But also, Grey’s going to continue to get better as well.”

“We’re committed to that.”

So the plan is Zabel at right guard. But if Quenton Nelson can’t go on Sunday, Anthony Bradford is back in the starting lineup.

Twelves — I spent an entire video arguing against trading Anthony Bradford. My reasoning was that Seattle had three new offensive line spots and Bradford was the only guard on the roster with forty starts at right guard. “You do not sell your insurance policy in the same week you create the risk.”

It’s four days later, Nelson’s knee kept him out of practice, and Bradford is limited with a knee of his own. If Seattle had traded him on Tuesday, who is playing guard on Sunday?

That’s not a victory lap. That’s just the reason depth exists.

And Macdonald’s framing on Nelson is the best explanation of the trade anybody has given.

“It’s worth it when you bring in a guy like Quinton. I think he’s one of those guys that’s going to be a force multiplier for our group — not just our offensive line, but really our whole offensive unit and our football team. So it’s worth that initial investment. Obviously you want it to go quicker than not, but you also have to understand there is a process involved.”

“A force multiplier.” Not a replacement. A multiplier — a man who makes the four guys next to him better.

And he was honest about the cost: “we’ve played a lot of games with our offensive line maybe missing a guy here or there... but we had a lot of consistency and continuity at our offensive line, which has been great.” Translation: we had chemistry, we broke it on purpose, and we believe the trade is worth the short-term mess.

And then the tape review, which is genuinely encouraging.

Asked how Nelson looked on film: “I thought — considering the newness — he looked like Quinton Nelson. Which was pretty cool. And a lot of good stuff in the run game.”

“He looked like Quenton Nelson.”

On a night Seattle rushed for eighty-one yards, the new left guard graded out fine. That tells you where the problem wasn’t.

Macdonald: “You think of Charbonnet — he hasn’t had a live rep since the injury, unfortunately.”

Not a single live football rep since he tore his ACL in the divisional round in January.

Monday night was his first actual football in roughly eight months, in prime time, against a defense that gave up two first-round picks for a defensive tackle. And he was held under thirty yards.

Of course he was. And Macdonald explained the handcuffs: “without getting into pitch counts... there are some constraints on how much we can — which is fair, guys coming off injury. There are things we’re working through which are in the best interest of the player, so we can have them for the long run. It’s not as straightforward as a normal situation would be.”

“Pitch counts.” He used a baseball word, and it’s exactly right.

“They’re lessened. Yeah, they’re lessened.”

Said twice. The rookie first-round pick has fewer restrictions on Sunday than he did Monday. That’s a real, concrete upgrade, and it’s the most useful sentence in the press conference for Sunday.

Now Macdonald on the loss itself, and I respect how he threaded this.

“The feeling that I had, that we had after the game — that wasn’t the best version of us. And that’s the most disappointing thing.”

And then: “As a coach you get emotional. However, you do have to go in and identify the positive things that do happen. It’s not all bad. There are good things that we can build off of, that we will build off of, that we’ve been able to generate up to this point. If you want to stay as steady as possible, and you want to keep climbing and keep growing, I think that’s the mentality you have to have.”

“It’s not all bad.” After a seventeen-point home loss. That’s not spin — that’s a man whose job is to get eleven men to run into contact again on Sunday. You cannot do that by burning the tape.

He also said this, which stung: “I think we played probably our best game against Kansas City, and I was really excited for our unit to grow. And we didn’t — from our expectations.”

Now operations, because this is the tactical meat and it’s excellent.

Asked what he saw on film about his offense’s operation:

“Yeah. We need to be better operationally. We’re not just going to go line up and snap the ball on the quick every snap, and we’re not going to go on two every snap, we’re not going to go on one every snap. But how we take the line, how we motion and shift, how we get in and out of plays — I think it has gotten better.”

“How we take the line.” That’s the walk-up, the cadence, the motion, the shift — the fifteen seconds before the snap that Sam Darnold described as carrying two or three plays plus an alert.

And the honest assessment: “There’s a lot of plays where I feel us taking the attack to the defense — that’s how we want to play offense. And there’s plays where that is, and there’s plays where there isn’t. So we’ve got to focus on how we make our identity come to life from play to play, and let’s be consistent over the course of a game.”

“Plays where there isn’t.” That’s the inconsistency, named by the head coach, in public.

And now the single best question anybody asked all season, and the answer is extraordinary.

A reporter pointed out that Seattle was one of the most efficient offenses in the league on its first fifteen plays last year — and isn’t this year. What goes into being good in the opening fifteen?

“It starts with being decisive. We’ve had some good starts. We’ve had several not as good starts as we want. I think it’s kind of a micro — it’s kind of like defensively, we get to the red zone and we don’t execute as well; we get drives extended offensively. There’s kind of a comedy of things that have happened after the drive gets off the ground, for very different reasons.”

“A comedy of things.”

And the fix: “We have to do a better job of understanding what we’re getting to earlier in games, and make sure everybody has a collective understanding about what we’re doing, why we’re doing it — and then the mental capacity to keep reattacking from play to play.” Plus: “We definitely want to start faster, for sure.”

And when asked whether it’s execution or defenses showing unexpected looks: “You like to always keep it in the world of the things that you can control. So we’re going to focus on us executing it better, and calling it better, and game-planning it better.”

Again — calling it and game-planning it. Coaching listed before players. Every single time.

And finally, the answer I’m going to be quoting for the rest of the season.

A reporter laid out a real analytics point: passing on first down is one of the most efficient things an offense can do across the league — but you also want to establish the run. How do you balance that?

Macdonald started honestly: “It’s a great question. There’s sequencing involved... there’s a time and place for both. Having an understanding of when to hit the button is important.” Then he laughed at himself: “I’m having a hard time giving you an answer without letting them know how we think about these situations. So — I don’t know. This is probably my worst answer in a while. Sorry.”

And then he gave the real answer anyway, and it’s brilliant:

“We believe in our offensive line going forward, and having the quarterback play decisively and fast. And a lot of times, in order for that to come to life, you have to minimize the amount of times everybody knows you’re dropping back. So sometimes you need to call pass-run-run-pass. If you alternate... there’s psychos out there that have computers that find out when you’re going to pass the ball and how you’re going to pass it. And now you just gave them the advantage.”

“There’s psychos out there that have computers.”

Twelves, that is the best thing a coach has said in this entire season, and I want to explain why it’s not a joke.

The analytics are right: across the league, passing on first down generates more expected points than running on first down. That’s true.

But that’s an average — and an average assumes you aren’t predictable.

Every single team in the NFL has an analytics department producing tendency reports. They know your pass rate on first-and-ten from the shotgun with eleven personnel in the first quarter. They know it to the decimal. And the moment your tendency becomes a pattern, the efficiency evaporates — because now the defense is calling its best coverage on exactly the play you were about to run.

So “minimize the amount of times everybody knows you’re dropping back” isn’t anti-analytics. It’s a second layer of analytics. The first layer says throw on first down. The second layer says the value of throwing on first down depends on them not knowing.

That’s why the run game matters even when it’s inefficient. You’re not buying yards. You’re buying uncertainty.
