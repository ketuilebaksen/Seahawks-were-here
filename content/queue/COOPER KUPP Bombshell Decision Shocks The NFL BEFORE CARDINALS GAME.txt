“We just weren’t holding up our end.”

That’s Cooper Kupp, standing at a podium after a seventeen-point home loss, taking the blame for the entire offense in eight words.

And twelves, I’ll be straight with you the way I always am: the word “bombshell” in this title is doing a lot of work. Cooper Kupp did not announce anything. But what he did do is diagnose exactly what broke this offense on Monday night, in detail, with no excuses — and then Julian Love walked up and said something about this locker room that I haven’t been able to stop thinking about for four days.

So stay with me, because this is the most honest twenty minutes anybody from this team has given us all season.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is four and four. Arizona is at Lumen Field. Let’s go.

Let’s start with Kupp’s assessment, because he did not reach for a single excuse.

Asked where this offense actually is after looking promising against Kansas City and then falling apart against Chicago:

“Well, I mean, I think we won’t really know until we get up there and watch the film. But obviously — not good enough. Had way too many three and outs, way too many short drives, and just putting our defense back out on the field. Just not holding up our end, I guess. We’re better than that, and we’ve got to find some solutions.”

“Way too many three and outs. Way too many short drives. Just putting our defense back out on the field.”

Twelves, that is the whole game in one sentence, and I want to put a number on it for you.

Seattle’s offense did not simply play badly on Monday night. It abandoned its own defense. Every three and out is a defense that just came off the field going straight back onto it. Every short drive is a defense that gets no rest, no sideline time, no chance to make corrections. And by the fourth quarter the defense that held Kansas City to three points eight days earlier was giving up thirty-four.

That’s not two separate failures. That’s one failure with two symptoms.

And then Kupp did the thing that almost nobody in professional sports does — he credited the team that beat him.

“Kudos to the Bears. That was a very good defense. But we’ve got to be better. We have to be able to execute better, and at the end of the day keep the ball moving forward and make the plays that are there for us.”

“Make the plays that are there for us.”

That’s the key phrase and it’s a brutal admission. He’s not saying Chicago took everything away. He’s saying the plays were available and Seattle didn’t make them. That’s harder to say than “they had a good scheme,” and he said both.

Now the second answer, and this is the football nugget of the week.

A reporter asked about the focus on getting the ball out quickly, and whether there are problems when Sam Darnold has to extend a play.

Kupp’s answer: “Yeah, I don’t know, there’s probably a lot to that. I think he’s been tremendous off schedule over the last couple years. I don’t know what the stats are saying this year, but whatever it is, he’s a pretty dynamic quarterback when he does get out of the pocket.”

And then he explained the receiver’s half of it, and this is the part I want you to hear:

“There’s a lot of things that go into that in terms of us distributing the field, and understanding that the play continues — the play really, the second play starts once he gets out of the pocket. And we’ve got to do a better job of being able to get open and find ways to let him get the ball out of his hands when he does extend like that.”

“The second play starts once he gets out of the pocket.”

Twelves, that single sentence is the most useful thing a receiver has said on this channel all season, and I want to break it down properly because it explains plays you have been yelling at your television about.

Here’s what’s happening on a scramble. The original play is dead. The route combination — the spacing, the levels, the timing, all of it — becomes garbage the instant the quarterback leaves his launch point. Everybody’s assignment changes at once.

And there are rules for that second play. It’s called the scramble drill. Receivers break to the same side the quarterback is running. Deep routes come back downhill toward the ball. Shallow routes climb. Somebody has to work back across his face. The whole point is to give him two targets at two different depths on the side of the field he’s running to, instead of five men standing exactly where the dead play told them to stand.

And Kupp just publicly took responsibility for Seattle not doing that well enough. He didn’t say Darnold held the ball. He said: when our quarterback extends the play, we have to be better at getting open on the second one.

That is a nine-year veteran with a Super Bowl MVP in his trophy case taking the blame for scramble-drill spacing. That’s leadership, and it’s also a correction you can actually watch for on Sunday.

Now the coverage answer, and Kupp got specific in a way players usually don’t.

Asked what Chicago was doing on the back end to cause problems:

“Yeah, I mean, they had a good plan. There’s a mix of some of the stuff we’ve seen, but also playing a mixture of some four-man stuff. A few different two-man looks, doing a good job with the safeties. And I think they played well off each other in terms of understanding the times they can be aggressive at safety, and be able to cut things and play low based on the pressure they were generating — and were expecting to generate. And also being able to take away stuff down the field when they needed to.”

Let me unpack what he just described, because this is a real scheme answer.

“Two-man looks” means Two-Man Under — two deep safeties with man coverage underneath. It’s the coverage you play when you want to take away everything deep and dare the quarterback to throw short against tight man coverage.

And here’s the part Kupp highlighted: “being aggressive at safety... able to cut things and play low based on the pressure they were generating.”

That’s the whole trick. A safety’s default job is to protect the deep part of the field. But if you’re confident your four-man rush is going to get home, your safety doesn’t have to stay deep — he can come down and attack the intermediate throw, because the quarterback won’t have time to test him over the top anyway.

Chicago’s pressure bought Chicago’s safeties permission to be aggressive.

And that’s exactly why “get the ball out quickly” was such a trap. The quick throws were into tight man coverage. The deep throws were covered by two safeties. And the intermediate windows — the ones that should have been open — had a safety crashing into them because the pass rush was winning.

That’s a well-coached defense and Cooper Kupp said so plainly: “He’s got a good plan. We’ve got to do a better job adjusting to some of those ways. And at the end of the day, we’ve got to make plays as players.”

Always back to the players. Every single answer.

Now the short-week answer, and this is the one I’d put on the wall.

Asked what this team needs to do to get back on track with a six-day week:

“Yeah, just get back to work. You’ve got no other option. The show goes on. We’ve got to prepare for another team coming into Lumen, and we’ve got to get back to the grindstone.”

And then — this is the part that matters:

“On a short week, you can’t — the best teams have to continue to grow. You’ve got to be able to watch the film and make the corrections and then move forward. That’s just the name of the game. We can’t gloss over this thing and just move on to Arizona. We have to make the corrections so we can grow, so we can challenge ourselves, so we can be a better team next week.”

“We can’t gloss over this thing and just move on to Arizona.”

Twelves, that’s harder than it sounds, and here’s why.

The easy thing on a short week is amnesia. Burn the tape, say “we’re onto Arizona,” and let everybody feel better by Wednesday. It’s emotionally convenient and it’s how teams lose twice.

Kupp is arguing for the opposite: sit in it, fix it, then move. Six days to install a new game plan and correct a seventeen-point loss. He’s saying do both, because the best teams don’t get to skip the ugly part.

And now the question I knew was coming — and the answer is pure class.

A reporter raised the fact that Jaxon Smith-Njigba leads the entire NFL in target share — and asked what everyone else can do to even the distribution out.

And here’s Cooper Kupp — a man who once caught a hundred and forty-five passes in a season, who has a Super Bowl MVP, who was the best receiver in football — being asked how to get the ball thrown to him more.

His answer:

“Yeah, I mean, look — Jax is a great player. He’s going to command targets. I think he’s one of, if not the best receiver in the league right now. So that’s just going to be part of the deal. We all have a part of it, and if he’s open, the ball’s going to go his way. So I don’t know what that’s going to — that’s going to be what it is.”

And then: “We’ve got to find ways to continue to compete, and you’ve got to compete without the ball as well. And when the ball does come your way, you’ve got to make a play.”

“You’ve got to compete without the ball as well.”

Twelves, I want to sit here for a second.

Cooper Kupp was handed a free opportunity to complain about his usage — a reporter literally built the ramp for him — and he used it to call the man taking his targets one of the best receivers in the league.

And then he described his own job as competing without the ball. Which, if you know anything about football, is the actual job. Blocking on the perimeter. Running the decoy route that pulls the safety. Clearing out the coverage so Jaxon Smith-Njigba gets a one-on-one. None of it shows up on a stat sheet and all of it shows up in the win column.

“If he’s open, the ball’s going to go his way.” That’s a professional. There is no version of this answer that goes better.

Now Julian Love, and twelves, this is where it gets real.

Asked whether the loss was a surprise after the way the Chiefs game went:

“Definitely a disappointing effort. You want to be able to stack plays. You want to be able to play complementary football. We didn’t. There were a few plays that I’m sure a ton of people wish they had back. And versus a good team, a well-coached team — yeah, it’s going to kill you.”

“You want to play complementary football. We didn’t.”

That’s the same diagnosis Kupp gave, from the other side of the ball. The offense says we abandoned the defense. The defense says we weren’t complementary. Both units looked at the same film and reached the same conclusion. That’s a team that agrees on what’s wrong, which is the first requirement for fixing it.

And then Love was asked a question that had to sting: nobody has moved the ball on this defense like that in two years. Was that them making plays, or you not playing well enough?

“A mixture. I think a few good calls. Like the screen there — that forty-yard screen, that was a killer. Good play call. We’ve got to find a way to get that down, especially on third down, to give our offense a shot. But for the most part, I feel like everything was within our control and we’ve got to clean up a lot of things.”

“Everything was within our control.”

He had the escape hatch and he refused it. The question offered him “they just made plays.” He said it was in our control.

And then he gave us the number that should actually scare you, unprompted:

“Especially our red zone efficiency is terrible right now, and so we need to go to work on that.”

“Terrible right now.”

Twelves, that’s the starting safety using the word “terrible” about his own unit in public. And it connects directly to what Mike Macdonald said this week — “defensively we get to the red zone and don’t execute as well.” Coach and player, same complaint, same week. This is a known problem being named out loud, not a hidden one.

And then the honest balance:

“Other than that, I think we did a great job versus the run. The team runs the ball really well. And then just those open field throws — we made it a little too easy for him back there, and so we’ve got to tighten some things up.”

“We made it a little too easy for him.”

Now, on Caleb Williams, and I appreciated how straight Love was about it.

Asked what makes him who he is: “He’s a good quarterback. I think he can make all throws. He’s got a strong arm, he’s confident, he plays with swagger to him. And so yeah — you give him some open lanes, he’s going to make the throw.”

And then the specific confession: “A lot of those deep developing routes in the middle of the field — I mean, the windows are pretty big. And so I think a good quarterback can make those throws. Other than that, we’ve got to correct that for sure.”

“The windows are pretty big.”

That’s a starting safety telling you his own coverage was too soft in the middle of the field. Deep developing routes — the dig, the deep over, the stuff that takes four seconds to come open behind the linebackers and in front of the safeties. That’s the hole in a defense that’s playing honest, and Chicago found it repeatedly.

Now THE sequence — and this is the moment Julian Love says decided the football game.

Asked how critical the delay-penalty-screen sequence was:

“I mean, that’s tough. That’s a tough one. You get a stop. It was a penalty on us — it was a cost-of-doing-business penalty on that kickoff. Get them down to kind of the fifty-ish. And yeah, we feel like we had them pretty stout, and then you see the flag coming in, and they make the play the next play. Just an unfortunate series of events there.”

And then: “We’ve just got to find a way to get off on third and nine. Man, our third down efficiency needs to step up. I think they were probably like forty percent today, which we’ve got to lower that number on defense.”

And later he came back to it unprompted, and this is the quote of his entire press conference:

“We had a great game last week. The city was fired up. To come into another prime time game, we wish we had a better result. I think we had a shot there. The momentum — you kind of could feel it in the stadium, the momentum was swinging. And then that freaking third and nine, that screen play just killed all momentum.”

“That freaking third and nine.”

Twelves, that’s a professional football player swearing — mildly — at a single play four days later. That’s how much it hurt.

Let me paint the picture for you. Seattle gets the stop. A flag comes in. Chicago gets a free set of downs. It’s third and nine — a down Seattle should win — and the Bears call a screen. Forty yards. Momentum gone.

And here’s Love’s analysis, and it’s generous and accurate at the same time:

“I mean, they throw screens hoping one of their playmakers can make a play. And we have to get that leverage. We have to get some bodies over there. They blocked it really well. And yeah — it’s a great play call for what we were presenting there. And it’s a bummer, because we got the stop on the delay the play before. So it’s those little moments. You could pinpoint that moment of — man, we make the stop, and who knows what happens the rest of this game. And yeah, we sort of fizzled out.”

“We make the stop and who knows what happens the rest of this game.”

That’s a man who knows exactly where the game turned, down to the snap. And he’s not blaming the referee. He said it himself — “a cost-of-doing-business penalty.”

Now the answer that’s the actual reason I made this video.

Asked what the frustration level is for a veteran group sitting at four and four:

“I would say a lesser team would be really frustrated, and like — oh, you know, turn, pointing fingers, turning on each other. I think the guys in the room, or at least me, it’s like: man, there’s so much out there for us.”

“A lesser team would be pointing fingers and turning on each other.”

Twelves, that is the single most important sentence from this entire week, and here’s why.

This is the moment it happens. Four and four. A prime time blowout at home. The quarterback hurt. The new guard hurt. Seven men ruled out. This is the exact point in a season where rooms crack — where the defense starts muttering about the offense, where somebody leaks, where a veteran gives an anonymous quote.

And the starting safety stood up and said: that’s what a lesser team does.

And then the honest part — because he did not tell you everything is fine:

“We play a good brand of football. Teams know kind of what we’re about. I think we play tough, like we’re connected. But we’re still chasing efficiency, on both sides of the ball. There’s so much more for us to be — and it’s exciting, because we have a bunch more games to go out and prove that. But I think we’re still in identity-finding mode. We want to get this thing moving and grooving in the way you guys have seen it before, the way we know how it’s supposed to look. Right now, it does not feel how it’s supposed to look.”

“Right now, it does not feel how it’s supposed to look.”

Said out loud, at a podium, by a team captain. Eight games in.

And when a reporter pushed — isn’t there urgency to that, eight games in and still looking for an identity? — Love did not get defensive:

“I know it’s never good. I think it’s one of those things that you work through the process. You attack whoever you have that week. We’ve got a division game coming up, and hopefully that identity can be formed. It’s not something you can force. We’ve got to play better. It’s a results business. We’ve got to win games. Four and four is not a great spot to be.”

“It’s a results business. We’ve got to win games. Four and four is not a great spot to be.”

No spin. No “we’re right where we need to be.” He said four and four is not good enough and that the standard is results.

And then he closed with the thing I actually believe:

“But I’m optimistic that we’re going to get things right. I think we’re going to catch our stride here. And once this thing gets going, it’s going to be tough to stop.”

And earlier: “I’m just happy with the guys playing tough. But I know each guy just wishes they had a play back in some of those critical downs.”

Every man wanting one play back is not a broken locker room, twelves. It’s the opposite. Broken teams want somebody else’s play back.

Let me bring it home.

Here’s what you just heard from two of the most respected veterans on this roster.

The offense says it put its own defense back on the field too many times and didn’t make the plays that were there. The defense says everything was within its control, its red zone work is terrible, and one screen on third and nine killed the whole night. Both of them credited the opponent. Neither of them blamed a teammate. And one of them told you plainly that a lesser team would already be pointing fingers.

And now Arizona comes to Lumen Field with Seattle missing Sam Darnold, Charles Cross, Quenton Nelson, Leonard Williams, Rashid Shaheed and Uchenna Nwosu — with Drew Lock starting and saying his elbow is doing good and he’s taking every rep.

Here’s what I’m watching for on Sunday, and these are the exact three things these two men handed us: third down efficiency on defense, red zone execution, and whether this offense can stay on the field long enough to let its defense breathe.

Those aren’t my talking points. Those are theirs.

I’ll stay where I was: Seahawks twenty-three, Cardinals sixteen.
