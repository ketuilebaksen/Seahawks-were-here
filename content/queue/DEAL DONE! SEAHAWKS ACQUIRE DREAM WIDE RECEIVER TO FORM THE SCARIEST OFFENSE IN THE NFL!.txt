Seattle signed two wide receivers this week. Their names are Montorie Foster Jr. and Malick Meiga. Neither one of them is going to play on Monday night.

And I’m going to spend this entire video on them anyway — because what happened to those two men this month explains more about how an NFL football team actually works than any trade rumor you’ve heard all season.

Seahawks Nation, let me get the obvious out of the way first. Yes, a deal got done this week. Seattle traded a 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick to Indianapolis for Quenton Nelson — an eight-time Pro Bowler, a six-time All-Pro, a man Mike Macdonald called “a surefire Hall of Fame guard.”

That’s the deal. It was a guard, not a receiver. I’ve broken that one down in full already, and I’ll come back to it at the end because it does actually answer the “scariest offense” question — just not the way the headline says.

But the receiver story is real, it’s just smaller and better. This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Let’s go.

First: exactly what Seattle did on Tuesday.

On Tuesday, October twenty-seventh, the Seattle Seahawks made four practice-squad moves.

Signed: wide receiver Montorie Foster Jr. and wide receiver Malick Meiga.
Released: fullback Jackson Acker and linebacker Josh Ross.

That’s the transaction. Four names, one press release, zero highlights. The kind of thing most people scroll past in half a second.

And here’s why I’m not scrolling past it.

Let me explain what a practice squad actually is, because almost nobody does — and once you understand it, this week makes sense.

A practice squad in 2026 is sixteen players, plus a seventeenth if he’s in the International Player Pathway program. They practice with the team, they run the scout team, they take the hits in the Wednesday walkthrough — and they are not on the 53-man roster. They cannot play on Sunday unless something specific happens.

And here’s what they earn. For players with two or fewer accrued seasons, the 2026 rate is $13,750 a week — about $233,750 across the seventeen weeks. For the veteran group, it’s a negotiated range between $18,350 and $22,850 a week, so roughly $312,000 to $388,000.

And practice-squad contracts cannot contain guarantees, bonuses or incentives. Not one dollar. You get paid for the weeks you’re there, and if they release you on a Tuesday, the money stops on Tuesday.

Twelves, I want you to hold that number next to the one from the trade. Quenton Nelson’s prorated cap number for the rest of this season is $9.77 million. Malick Meiga’s entire year, if he survives all seventeen weeks, is less than a quarter of a million dollars — with no guarantee that he gets week two.

Same building. Same practice field. Same Monday night.

Now the part that matters on game day: elevations.

A team can elevate up to two practice-squad players per game. Any individual player can be elevated three times per season. When he’s elevated, he’s active, he plays, he’s a real NFL player on a real NFL Sunday.

And then: at four o’clock Eastern on the first business day after the game, he automatically reverts to the practice squad. No waivers. No ceremony. He played in an NFL game on Sunday and he’s back on the scout team by Monday afternoon.

And once a man has been elevated three times, you can’t do it again — you have to sign him to the 53-man roster to use him a fourth time. Which means somebody else has to come off it.

So when I tell you Montorie Foster Jr. or Malick Meiga “won’t play Monday,” what I actually mean is: they could. Two of them could be elevated. It would just cost the team something to do it.

And now the rule that explains why Seattle brought these two back instead of leaving the spots open.

A practice squad is not safe storage. Any other team in the NFL can sign your practice-squad player — to their active roster.

That’s the rule. You cannot poach a man onto another practice squad, but you absolutely can poach him onto your 53. And it costs the signing team real money: they owe him at least three weeks of pay at the active-roster minimum, even if they cut him sooner, and they have to count him against their own 53-man roster for three games.

There are timing restrictions too — a player can’t sign with his own team’s next opponent after four o’clock Eastern on the sixth day before that game, which stretches to the tenth day if his team is coming off a bye.

So here’s the real translation of Tuesday’s move: Seattle looked at a receiver room with injuries in it, looked at two young men they already knew, and put them back in the building before somebody else promoted them. You don’t hold a developmental receiver by hoping. You hold him by signing him.

Now Montorie Foster Jr. — and twelves, this is the best roster lesson of the entire season.

Montorie Foster Jr. was a 2025 undrafted free agent. He was a standout in camp. He made the initial 53-man roster — which, for an undrafted guy, is already the achievement of a lifetime. He appeared in one game this season.

And then on Saturday, he was waived.

Why? Not because he did anything wrong. Because Seattle needed a roster spot to activate Anthony Bradford from injured reserve.

Read that again.

Seattle cut a wide receiver off the 53-man roster so that Anthony Bradford could come back and play right guard.

And what happened next? Bradford started against the Kansas City Chiefs. Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 rushing yards. They allowed zero sacks. They held the ball for thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds. They won 20-3. And on Wednesday, Mike Macdonald stood at a podium and volunteered, unprompted: “I think our offensive line probably played their best game of the season this past Sunday night.”

The best offensive line performance of Seattle’s season was paid for with a wide receiver’s job.

That’s the whole truth about an NFL roster: it is fifty-three chairs and there are no exceptions. Every activation is a release. Every promotion is somebody’s phone call. There is no version of this where you add a man without subtracting one.

And then — three days later — Anthony Bradford lost his starting job to Quenton Nelson.

Twelves, think about the shape of that. A young receiver lost his roster spot so Bradford could start. Bradford started one game, the best line game of the year, and then got replaced by a Hall of Famer. And now the receiver is back on the practice squad and the guard is a backup.

The same roster that couldn’t protect Montorie Foster couldn’t protect Anthony Bradford either. And Macdonald knew exactly what he was doing to him: “Really no way to sugarcoat it.”

That’s the business. And Seattle, to its credit, brought the kid back three days after cutting him — because, as the guy on that other channel put it pretty well: sometimes it isn’t about talent. It’s about the math.

Now Malick Meiga. And I’m going to be honest with you — this is the most remarkable year I have read about all season.

Malick Meiga was born in the Ivory Coast and raised in Montreal.

He started his college football career at Penn State. Then he transferred to Coastal Carolina for two seasons. He went undrafted in the NFL draft.

And then the Hamilton Tiger-Cats selected him in the second round of this year’s CFL draft.

He signed with the Carolina Panthers as an undrafted free agent and spent training camp there. He didn’t make it. He signed with Seattle’s practice squad in September. Seattle released him in October — to make room for the fullback they just released this week. And on Tuesday, Seattle signed him back.

Let me lay that out in order. Ivory Coast. Montreal. Penn State. Coastal Carolina. Drafted in the second round by a CFL team. Training camp with Carolina. Seattle’s practice squad. Released. Seattle’s practice squad again.

Two countries. Four professional football organizations. One calendar year.

And one honest note, because I promised I always would: the version going around says his pre-draft testing ranked in the 96th percentile of all wide receivers since 1987. I could not verify that number in Seattle’s own release, so I’m giving it to you as what’s circulating, not as confirmed fact. If it’s accurate, it explains everything about why a team keeps dialing his number. If it isn’t, he’s still a man who has been told no by three organizations this year and keeps showing up to work.

He is not the dream wide receiver. He is something better: he is proof that this coaching staff keeps a list.

Macdonald talks constantly about “stay ready.” He said it about Anthony Bradford this week — “he’s going to stay ready, and we know how this league works; you’re a play away from him playing every play again.” Malick Meiga is the bottom rung of that same philosophy. Somebody in that building wrote his name down in September, crossed it off in October, and wrote it again in October. That’s a team that doesn’t forget people.

Now — why receivers specifically? Because two of Seattle’s are hurt, and the timing is not an accident.

Rashid Shaheed hurt his knee against the Chiefs and did not come back. Macdonald’s early read, Monday: “Any time the knee’s involved, you’re holding your breath. So right now, let’s just say it’s positive.” By Wednesday: “Rasheed’s doing well. Practice status today — I don’t think he’s going to practice today. I’ve been wrong before, but I don’t believe he’s going to be out there.”

And Velus Jones Jr. tweaked a hamstring in the same game.

So: Seattle’s deep threat and punt returner is on a knee, another receiver is on a hamstring, and on Tuesday the team added two receivers to the practice squad. Nobody from the Seahawks said those things are connected, and I’m not going to claim they did. But I can read a calendar.

And this is the quiet, boring reason the practice squad exists. It is not a talent pipeline to stardom. It is an insurance policy with a sixteen-man limit, and you buy the coverage on Tuesday because by Friday it’s too late.

But there’s a second job those two men have this week, and it’s the one nobody ever mentions.

A practice-squad receiver’s actual Monday-through-Friday job is to be the other team’s receivers.

Cooper Kupp said something on Sunday night that I keep thinking about: “We get the pleasure of going up against them” — meaning Seattle’s offense practices against Seattle’s defense every week, and it’s the hardest look in football.

Well, it goes the other way too. Somebody has to stand across from Devon Witherspoon and Nick Emmanwori on a Thursday and run Chicago’s routes at Chicago’s speed, hard enough that the look is real. That’s the scout team, and it’s the practice squad’s job.

So this week, Montorie Foster Jr. and Malick Meiga are the Chicago Bears. They’re running Ben Johnson’s concepts at a defense that is first in the NFL in opponent yards per play and allows the fewest explosive passes in football — and the better they do it, the better that defense plays on Monday night.

Twelves, that’s the part of a practice-squad signing that actually shows up on the scoreboard. Not the two guys who might get elevated. The four days of work they put a championship defense through before kickoff.

And when Seattle released a fullback and a linebacker to make room for them, that was a scheme decision too. You keep the bodies that help you simulate this week’s opponent. Chicago throws it with a quarterback completing 71 percent and topping 300 yards two weeks running. You don’t need an extra fullback this week. You need receivers.

Now let me clear up one thing from this week that confused a lot of people, because it’s a genuinely interesting rule.

You’ve probably heard two things that sound contradictory: Drew Lock was inactive against Kansas City — and — Drew Lock was Seattle’s emergency third quarterback against Kansas City.

Both are true. Here’s the rule.

The emergency third quarterback must be on the 53-man roster, and he cannot be one of the two elevated practice-squad players. But — and this is the key — he can be placed in an inactive spot. He does not count against the forty-eight players a team can make active on game day.

He may only enter the game if the quarterbacks on the active roster are injured or disqualified. And if an active quarterback gets medically cleared to come back, the third quarterback has to leave the field.

The league introduced this version for the 2023 season, after San Francisco lost both of its quarterbacks in the 2022 NFC Championship Game — which is why people call it the Brock Purdy Rule.

So what Seattle actually did against the Chiefs was clever. Drew Lock’s elbow was bad enough that they didn’t want him active — but by designating him the emergency third quarterback, they kept a real NFL starter available for a catastrophe without spending an active roster spot on him.

That’s not a sign of chaos. That’s a front office using a rule correctly. And it’s why I said last week that going into a Sunday night game against Kansas City one injury from improvising was nervy but defensible. It was more covered than it looked.

And the rest of the injury picture, quickly.

Charles Cross — ankle, out Monday, Josh Jones at left tackle (“Josh is our third tackle,” Macdonald said). And the real headline: “As of right now, he’s not going on injured reserve.” IR means a minimum of four games. They didn’t do it, which means they think he’s back sooner.

Zach Charbonnet — and here’s the detail that gets me: he tore the ACL in the playoffs. During the championship run. Surgery February twentieth. He practiced fully for the first time since, and he could make his season debut Monday night.

Jadarian Price — the rookie first-rounder, four games on injured reserve with a chest injury, back at practice this week.

Macdonald on both backs: either one could play, and either one could also stay out. No promises.

Now — the “scariest offense in the NFL” part of the headline. Let’s actually test it.

Seattle’s receiver room, honestly, is not scary because of who they signed Tuesday. It’s scary because of who already works there.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba leads the entire NFL with 867 receiving yards. Second place — Chris Olave of New Orleans — is at 636. That’s a 231-yard lead, while teams double-team him. He is the first receiver since 1966 with 850-plus yards and eight touchdowns through seven games. And his 867 are the third-most through seven games in the Super Bowl era, behind only Tyreek Hill’s 902 in 2023 and Lance Alworth’s 871 in 1968.

He is four yards behind Lance Alworth. One catch Monday night and he’s second in fifty-six years of football.

Next to him is Cooper Kupp, who won the triple crown in 2021 and spent his Sunday night press conference explaining to the world how a doubled receiver avoids breaking.

That’s the room. You don’t make that scarier with a practice-squad signing. You make it scarier by giving the quarterback time — which is exactly what a six-time All-Pro guard does.

Cooper Kupp told us some of Seattle’s third-down conversions against Kansas City came on Sam Darnold’s fifth read. The fifth read. You don’t reach a fifth read in two and a half seconds; you reach it because the pocket holds. And Quenton Nelson has allowed zero sacks and one quarterback hit all season.

So in the only sense that matters, Seattle did make a receiver move this week. He just weighs three hundred and twenty pounds and plays left guard.

Now let me be straight about this title, like always.

“DEAL DONE” — completely true. Seattle made a real, confirmed trade for a surefire Hall of Famer. Full credit.

“ACQUIRE DREAM WIDE RECEIVER” — not true. The acquisition was Quenton Nelson, a guard. The wide receivers were two practice-squad signings, one of whom Seattle had released eleven days earlier, and neither is expected to play Monday.

“To form the scariest offense in the NFL” is a projection — Seattle is still 24th in EPA per play on the season and averaging 3.9 yards a carry.

But I’ll say this for the spirit of it: a league-leading receiver, a triple-crown winner beside him, a Hall of Fame guard arriving Monday, and a backfield that might get Charbonnet and Price back in the same week? That’s not the scariest offense in football yet. It’s the most interesting one.

Let me close.

A wide receiver lost his job so a guard could play. The guard played the best game the line had all year. Then a Hall of Famer arrived and the guard lost his job. And the receiver got a phone call and came back to the practice squad for $13,750 a week with no guarantees.

That’s one week in the NFL. And every single man in that story showed up to work on football Wednesday.
