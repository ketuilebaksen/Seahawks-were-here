Mike Macdonald said six words on Sunday night that I haven’t been able to stop thinking about.

“There’s a laundry list of things we need to get better at. And so, you know, there’s no time to waste. So let’s get after it.”

A laundry list.

Not “we’ll clean up some details.” Not “it’s a long season.” A laundry list — from the head coach of the defending Super Bowl champions, standing at a podium after a three-point loss, four days before his next kickoff.

So today, Seahawks Nation, we’re going to take that man completely literally. We’re going to write his laundry list down, item by item, in his own words. And then we’re going to look at Thursday night in Denver and figure out whether that’s the best place on earth to test it — or the worst.

And I’ll give you the number that frames all of it right now: the betting total for Thursday night is forty-one and a half points. That is the lowest number I have seen on a Seattle Seahawks game all season, and it tells you precisely what the entire gambling world thinks of both of these offenses.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Forty-niners thirty-two, Seahawks twenty-nine. Seattle is 3-2. Let’s get the list out.

LAUNDRY LIST, ITEM ONE: TAKE CARE OF THE FOOTBALL.

His words, from the opening ninety seconds: “Definitely taking care of the football. Way too many giveaways at this point in the season.”

The receipts are ugly. Four Seattle turnovers, and San Francisco converted them into twenty-one points.

Renardo Green intercepted Sam Darnold twice. Fred Warner punched out a fumble that Malik Mustapha recovered at the Seattle eleven, which became a Christian McCaffrey touchdown run. And Gracen Halton stripped the football and recovered it in the end zone with eighteen seconds left in the first half.

Seven combined turnovers — the most in any NFL game this season.

But here’s what I genuinely respect, and it’s the part everybody skipped. Macdonald was handed a free pass to blame his quarterback, twice, and he refused it both times.

“It’s everybody. It’s not just Sam. Sam knows — it’s not like he wants turnovers. We need to just play collectively more efficient. I think when that happens, then the ball doesn’t become in jeopardy as much.”

That last sentence is the real football insight of the whole press conference, and it’s subtle, so let me unpack it.

He is saying turnovers are a symptom of inefficiency, not a character flaw.

When you’re sitting in third-and-nine on every possession, the football has to go into the air, into tight windows, under pressure, on time. That’s where interceptions live. When you’re in second-and-four instead, the quarterback gets to throw the easy one. Fix the efficiency and the turnovers fall off by themselves.

That’s a coach declining a narrative that would have protected him personally. And he said the same thing about the start of games: “We need to find ways to start games a little faster on offense.” And later: “Too frequently early in games, we’re not doing that.”

ITEM TWO: COMPLEMENTARY FOOTBALL.

He used that exact phrase three separate times before anybody even asked him a question.

“In terms of playing complementary football the way that we want to play here as Seahawks, and become the team that we want to become — we’re not there yet.”

And then: “We didn’t do it, and the 49ers did that today, so give them credit. They played a good game.”

Now let me actually define it, because “complementary football” is the most-used and least-explained phrase in this sport.

Complementary football means each unit makes the other units’ jobs easier. Your defense takes the ball away, your offense turns it into seven. Your offense grinds out an eight-minute drive, your defense comes back out rested. Your special teams wins forty yards of field position, and your offense starts its drive already in scoring range.

It is not three separate teams in the same jersey. It’s three units feeding each other.

And here’s the brutal illustration from Sunday, because both teams got gifts and only one team cashed them.

Seattle’s scoring drives after San Francisco turnovers went twenty-four and twenty-three yards. San Francisco’s scoring drives after Seattle turnovers went eighteen and eleven yards — plus a fumble they recovered in the end zone and didn’t need a drive at all.

Same opportunity. Twenty-one points to fourteen. That’s the entire game, and that’s what “we’re not there yet” means in numbers.

ITEM THREE: THE RUN GAME. STRAIGHT UP.

He got asked about the end of the second quarter — Seattle at the San Francisco four, eighteen seconds left, San Francisco holding three timeouts, and Seattle calls a stretch run that gets fumbled into a touchdown the other way.

His answer was five words: “We should be able to execute run plays.”

Then he defended the call, correctly: “We want to stay aggressive. He kicks a touchback, we’re two plays away from field goal range and we’re in good shape. So we want to stay aggressive and get points, and the opportunity to get the ball coming out of the second half.”

And then the landing, which is the whole item: “But we got to be able to execute our run game and hold on to the football. Straight up.”

Straight up. He backed the decision and buried the execution. That’s exactly the right split, because handing the ball off from the four-yard line is a normal football call. You just have to hold onto it.

And then came the funniest and saddest exchange of the night. He was asked whether George Holani should have come out of the game after his fumble, the way Jadarian Price did after Washington. And Macdonald said:

“No. I mean — short on backs, you know. I can’t go out there and carry the ball, so nor do we want to.”

That’s a joke at his own expense four minutes after losing a first-place game, and underneath it is a confession: Price is on injured reserve, Charbonnet is on the PUP list, and you cannot bench a third of your position group. There is nobody to put in.

ITEM FOUR: PROTECTION, AND GETTING OUT OF LONG DOWN-AND-DISTANCE.

Asked what San Francisco was doing to get five sacks, he gave the most complete technical answer of the session — and notice he blamed four different things, none of them his quarterback:

“We got to the rush, coverage — like the protection and the routes and the timing of the play, that needs to be better. We can’t be in second-and-long. We can’t be in third-and-long. We got to keep the ball moving forward. Got to make smart decisions with the football. Got to take care of the ball.”

Five sacks. And here’s the detail that should bother you most: two of those five were the first sacks of those men’s entire NFL careers. Sebastian Valdez and Gracen Halton had never done it before in their lives. Add Jaden Dugger, Osa Odighizuwa, and Keion White on third down in the fourth quarter.

And the number that contains this entire item: Seattle went three-for-twelve on third down.

Twenty-five percent. You cannot win in this league converting a quarter of your third downs, and you cannot convert third downs if you keep arriving at third-and-nine.

Alright. Now let me do the other half of the list, because a video that’s only complaints is dishonest — and two items on this team’s to-do list have already been crossed off, in one week, and nobody is talking about it.

ALREADY FIXED, NUMBER ONE: THE PENALTIES.

Seattle committed five penalties for forty-six yards. San Francisco committed eight for seventy.

Seven days earlier it was fourteen penalties for a franchise-record 150 yards, including seven personal fouls. Four captains — Jarran Reed, Leonard Williams, Julian Love and Ernest Jones — called a players-only meeting with no coaches in the room.

Fourteen to five. Seattle out-disciplined the best team in football, seven days later.

ALREADY FIXED, NUMBER TWO: THE COMPOSURE — AND IT GOT TESTED HARDER THAN ANYBODY COULD HAVE SCRIPTED.

Dre Greenlaw head-butted Ernest Jones the Fourth while Jones was not wearing a helmet. No helmet. On the sideline. And Greenlaw got ejected under a brand-new rule that lets the league office disqualify a player for a non-football act even with no flag thrown on the field.

And Ernest Jones — bare-faced, head-butted, surrounded — did not swing back.

Macdonald on it: “Bro, that was crazy. I don’t know. I’m actually proud of our guys. You talk about chasing edges — I thought we played to the line today, and then they took care of it, given all the things that were going on.”

And on Jones specifically: “EJ led the charge. I know it was an emotional thing, but we got to stay in the pocket too, you know, for us to play our best ball. I thought our guys did a great job, honestly — coming off of last week and understanding how we want to operate.”

And then the sentence that is the actual thesis of this entire video:

“It just shows you — if we put our attention to things, our guys get better. We get it. We attack it.”

Twelves, that’s the argument for optimism, and it’s the reason the laundry list doesn’t scare me.

This team just proved — in seven days, under maximum provocation, against the best opponent on its schedule — that when it names a problem out loud, it fixes it. The laundry list isn’t a confession of weakness. It’s a to-do list from a group that has already demonstrated, this month, that it can execute one.

And there was more good than people are admitting. Byron Murphy the Second had the game of his season — Macdonald lit up: “You felt Murf today. Happy for him... Murf played a really good game.” Nehemiah Pritchett intercepted Brock Purdy, and got the warmest words of the night: “Nehemiah played a great game, man. Probably the best play was a special teams play. Guy’s playing double duty — as a gunner, and as a corner for us. Guy should be really proud of himself.” And on how he got there: “The third corner spot was going to be something that needed to be competed for throughout the offseason, and he didn’t shy away from it... He’s earned a role for us.”

And Seattle sacked Brock Purdy twice — Ernest Jones and Byron Murphy — ending a streak that no team had ended in four games and that nobody in football had matched in twenty-one years.

And one more thing I have to praise, because it’s rare. Asked whether his pass rush faded in the fourth quarter, Macdonald refused to invent an answer: “I’m not sure. You got to give them credit. I think they got to some solutions. I thought their quarterback made some plays. Probably a better answer for you tomorrow. But you’re probably right.”

Probably a better answer for you tomorrow. A head coach declining to make something up twenty minutes after a loss. I’ll take that over confident nonsense every single time.

Okay. Thursday. And this is where the list gets tested.

Thursday night, 8:15 Eastern, Empower Field at Mile High, on Prime Video. Both teams are 3-2. Seattle is favored by three. Total of forty-one and a half.

Now let me tell you what Denver actually is right now, because it is not what you’re braced for.

Denver’s pass rush has fallen off a cliff.

They led the entire National Football League with sixty-three sacks in 2024 and sixty-eight in 2025. Sixty-eight. And this season they came into Week 5 with eight sacks — tied for fifteenth in the league.

Nik Bonitto has one sack and five quarterback hits. His own words: “All across the line, I feel like we’re rushing well” — while admitting he’s getting more double teams — and then the honest part: “I have to find more ways to be effective.” And Jonathon Cooper is on the commissioner’s exempt list and has not played this season.

So if you were lying awake worrying that five sacks becomes eight sacks at altitude on a short week — the single scariest unit on Denver’s roster is the one thing that isn’t working in Denver.

And their offense is in worse shape than Seattle’s. They beat the winless Chargers in an ugly football game, Sean Payton took the play-calling duties back himself because the offense was floundering without him, and he still described the result as underwhelming afterward. Bo Nix is averaging about 188 passing yards a game with six touchdowns in five games.

That’s why the total is 41.5. Nobody believes either of these offenses can score.

And Denver’s injury report is the best news Seattle has had in a week. Patrick Surtain the Second did not practice with an ankle — arguably the best cornerback in football. Talanoa Hufanga didn’t practice either. Riley Moss is limited with ribs. That is their entire secondary on the report, in a week where Seattle has the reigning Offensive Player of the Year.

Now Seattle’s side, and there are two names on here nobody is talking about.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba is limited with an ankle injury he picked up Sunday. Reporting suggests it would take a lot for him to miss Thursday — but he is on that report, and that’s new.

And Drew Lock is limited with a right forearm injury. Your backup quarterback. On a short week. Nobody has mentioned it.

Also limited: Leonard Williams (ankle, again), Beau Stephens (shoulder now, not the knee), and Zach Charbonnet (knee). Chazz Surratt (calf) and Drake Thomas (ankle) did not practice — Drake Thomas being the one injury Macdonald volunteered on Sunday, the man who hurt an ankle but finished the game.

And genuine good news: Irv Charles was a full participant and is set to make his Seahawks debut after five weeks on injured reserve, and George Holani was full despite ribs and a knee — which means the man who fumbled is playing, because, as his coach said, they’re short on backs.

On Charbonnet, Macdonald gave the bluntest version yet: “The answer is no, subject to change.”

So he doesn’t play Thursday. Which means the October twenty-second deadline comes down to exactly one week. Activate him to the fifty-three or lose him for the entire season. Jadarian Price is eligible November second against the Bears.

Watch list, four things, straight off the laundry list.

One: Seattle’s first three drives. He said it twice — too frequently early in games, they’re not doing it. Score on the opening possession and this whole conversation changes.

Two: third down. Three-for-twelve last week. Anything under forty percent and the same story repeats.

Three: the football on the ground. Four giveaways is unsurvivable twice in a row. Zero turnovers Thursday and Seattle probably wins by ten.

Four: Patrick Surtain’s name on the final report. If he’s out and JSN is in, that’s the matchup that decides the night.

Let me close.

A laundry list sounds like a bad thing. Coming out of the mouth of this particular coach, I’m not sure it is.

Because seven days ago the list said fourteen penalties, seven personal fouls, franchise record. Four veterans sat in a room and handled it, and on Sunday this team committed five penalties and watched an opponent get ejected for losing his composure while Seattle’s own captain took a head-butt bare-faced and kept his hands down.

They put their attention on a thing, and the thing got fixed. That’s the pattern. Now the list says turnovers, third down, the run game, protection, and starting faster.

Four days. Mile High. A team whose pass rush has vanished and whose head coach just had to take back his own play sheet.

No time to waste. Let’s get after it.
