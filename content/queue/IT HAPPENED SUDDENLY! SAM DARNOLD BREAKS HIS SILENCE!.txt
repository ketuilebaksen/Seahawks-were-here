Sam Darnold invented a phrase on Sunday night that I’m going to be using for the rest of my life.

He was explaining the interception he threw to Elijah Arroyo — the one he says he’d most like back — and he said the ball needed to be:

“A us or nobody ball.”

Us. Or nobody.

Meaning: when I let it go, there are exactly two acceptable outcomes. My guy catches it, or it hits the grass. Those are the only two. Any throw where a third possibility exists — where a defender can get there — is a throw I should not have made.

Seahawks Nation, that is the entire philosophy of playing quarterback compressed into four words, and it came out of the mouth of a man twenty minutes after the worst afternoon of his season.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Forty-niners thirty-two, Seahawks twenty-nine. Seattle is 3-2 and plays Thursday night in Denver. And today we’re doing Sam Darnold’s full postgame — because somebody asked me to make a video about this man being tired and wanting out of Seattle, and I listened to all six minutes of it twice.

Let me tell you up front what I found, and then let me tell you the one real question about his future, which is a completely different question than the one being asked. Let’s go.

There is nothing in there about leaving.

Not a word. Not a hint. Not a sigh. Nobody has reported it — no insider, no beat writer, no anonymous source. And the six minutes of audio point the exact opposite direction, which I’m about to prove to you line by line.

So I’m not going to build a thumbnail on a feeling somebody had. What I’m going to give you instead is better: a quarterback refusing every single exit his own press conference handed him. Because that’s what actually happened, and it’s remarkable.

Let’s start with the interception, because his answer on it is the most technically detailed thing any Seahawk has said all season.

Here’s the whole thing, and read it slowly.

“We had talked about that route. We talked a lot throughout the week about him kind of taking it high, no matter what the coverage was — and so that was the coaching point. And so I knew he was going to do that. And I just... I got to put more air under it and let him go try to get it. Just have it be a us or nobody ball. I think that’s the main thing there — I had a chance to put it over the top for Elijah to make the catch, or for it to be incomplete. And so for me to put it in harm’s way like that, that’s not the way to go out there and play winning football.”

Let me unpack what he just admitted, because there are four separate confessions in there.

One: they had game-planned that route all week. Two: the coaching point was that Elijah Arroyo takes it high regardless of coverage — he climbs the field. Three: Darnold knew that. He says so. “I knew he was going to do that.” Four: he threw it flat anyway instead of putting air under it and letting a big tight end go up and get it.

So this isn’t “the receiver ran the wrong route.” It isn’t “we weren’t on the same page.” A reporter literally offered him that — asked whether maybe they weren’t on the same page — and Darnold shut that door himself. He said no, we talked about it, that was the coaching point, I knew, and I missed it.

That is a quarterback declining an escape hatch that a journalist held open for him.

And then he named his own standard: “not the way to go out there and play winning football.”

Twelves, I have watched a lot of quarterbacks answer a lot of questions about interceptions. The normal answer is “I’ll look at the tape.” This man gave the coaching point, the receiver’s assignment, his own read, and the mechanical fix — more air under it — in one breath.

And it happened again, with a different escape hatch.

A reporter asked whether San Francisco’s coverage did something different than he expected before those throws. That is the easiest out in football — they disguised it, they rotated late, I got fooled. Every quarterback takes it.

His answer: “No. You know, again, you got to tip your cap to those guys. They played a good game. But those are all things that I can control. You know, just can’t be putting the ball in jeopardy like that.”

No. Then credit to the opponent. Then those are all things that I can control.

He even gave credit by name, and not just to the players — on the five sacks he said: “They did a good job rushing and covering some of our concepts. So got to tip your cap to a good defense, and obviously a good coach and Raheem over there.” That’s Raheem Morris, San Francisco’s defensive coordinator. A quarterback who just got sacked five times naming the opposing coordinator respectfully, by first name, an hour later.

And now the one that genuinely got me, because it’s the play this entire fan base is angry about.

He took the blame for Cooper Kupp’s fourth down.

You know the play. Fourth-and-ten, 54 seconds left, Kupp dives for the marker, gets ruled a yard short, replay upholds it, game over. Half the internet is still screaming about the spot.

Here’s what the quarterback said:

“They did a good job covering downfield, really. And Coop had a little bit of separation — if I didn’t have to move in the pocket, I could have gotten the ball a little bit sooner, where he would have had a little bit more grass to get that first down.”

Read that again. He is saying the throw was late because he had to move, and that if he’d gotten it out sooner, Kupp would have had more field to work with.

I made that exact criticism on this channel two days ago — that the fundamental problem on that snap wasn’t the spot, it was that the football was thrown short of the sticks on fourth-and-ten. And I want to be honest: I feel strange about having been right, because the quarterback said it himself before I did, without being asked, on a play where every single person watching had already decided to blame a referee.

And he was still fair to Kupp: “what I saw was them covering our guys, and Coop was really the only guy, and I felt like he had an all right chance if he broke that tackle — which he did — to get the first down.”

He broke the tackle. The quarterback noticed. And still took it on himself.

“That’s anti who we are.” A Super Bowl MVP being asked if his team is splintering and calling the premise un-Seattle.

A receiver with 102 yards and a touchdown saying I let them down, because he dropped one third-down pass at the San Francisco 25 with 54 seconds left.

And then there’s the moment that explains the mood better than any quote, and Darnold was an eyewitness to it.

He was asked about the scrum after the hit on him out of bounds — whether he was surprised that Ernest Jones was one of the first men there to defend him.

“Yeah, that was just craziness on the sideline. And then I actually saw firsthand Dre kind of headbutt EJ there. So yeah, it’s just not a surprise. You know, I know those guys got my back, and I got theirs. So it’s just how we roll as a team.”

He saw it firsthand. Dre Greenlaw head-butting Ernest Jones — who wasn’t wearing a helmet — because Jones had gone over to defend his quarterback. Greenlaw got ejected. Jones never swung back. And Macdonald’s line on it: “It speaks for our whole team.”

So let me be very plain. A team whose captain eats a head-butt bare-faced for its quarterback is not a team that quarterback is trying to leave.

Now. The honest part, because you don’t come here for only one side.

Is there a problem with Sam Darnold’s play right now? Yes. A real one.

Here are the numbers through five games: 63 of 98, 755 yards, seven touchdowns, five interceptions, and a 90.3 passer rating.

Last season — the Super Bowl season — he finished at 99.6. The year before that in Minnesota, 100.8.

So he’s down about ten points of rating from his own two-year baseline. Against San Francisco he was 18 of 29 for 195 yards, one touchdown, two interceptions, and a 64.6 rating while taking five sacks.

And the detail that should actually worry you most: he has thrown an interception on his first pass in each of his last two games. The first pass. Twice in a row.

And the team number that frames all of it: Seattle’s offense currently ranks fourth-worst in the entire NFL in expected points added. Fourth-worst. On a 3-2 team carried by its defense.

Those are facts and I’m not going to soften them.

But here’s the context that nobody chasing a “Darnold is finished” headline will give you.

Go back to the middle of last season. Week 11. Seattle was 3-2 after a home loss to the 49ers — the exact same spot, against the exact same team. And a few weeks later, in a loss to the Rams, Sam Darnold threw four interceptions in one football game. At that point in November he was tied for the NFL lead with fourteen turnovers.

That team was 7-3.

And they won the Super Bowl.

And when it happened, Ernest Jones stood up in front of reporters and said: “Sam’s been balling. If we want to try to define him by this game... Sam’s had us in every game. So for him to sit there and say, ‘oh, that’s my fault’ — no, it’s not.” And then he finished with a sentence about critics that I can’t repeat on this channel.

So when somebody shows you five interceptions in five games and tells you a quarterback is cooked, remember that this exact quarterback led the entire league in turnovers during a championship season.

And Darnold himself was asked directly about the 2025 parallel — whether the locker room talked about having been 3-2 after a 49ers home loss before. His answer was the sharpest thing he said:

“Not really, to be honest. There was no talk of that. I think a lot of guys were just pissed about not winning the game. It’s a whole different year. We’re not in the business of comparing this year to last year. There’s a lot of things that we need to get better at. Plain and simple. And we got to have a sense of urgency to do that.”

“We’re not in the business of comparing this year to last year.” Same phrase his head coach used on Friday about the run game. That’s an organization that talks to itself.
