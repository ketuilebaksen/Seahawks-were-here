Last February, Kyle Shanahan was sitting in a television studio as an analyst, and somebody asked him for his expert opinion on Mike Macdonald’s defense.

Here’s what the head coach of the San Francisco 49ers said:

“I know you guys want my expert opinion. But I haven’t scored a touchdown on these guys the last two times we played them. So I don’t know how good that is.”

Not “they’re tough.” Not “we struggled.” I haven’t scored a touchdown.

Week 18 last season: Seattle 13, San Francisco 3. The divisional round: Seattle 41, San Francisco 6.

And here’s the number that makes it absurd. Over the eight games before they ran into Seattle, San Francisco averaged 34.3 points a game and won seven of them.

Against Seattle: four and a half.

That man brings a 4-0 football team into Lumen Field today.

First, let me be honest about what’s walking into this building, because I’m not going to show you an old score and tell you it’s a prediction.

This San Francisco offense is the most efficient in the National Football League. Not close.

They lead the entire NFL in yards per play at 6.8.

They lead the entire NFL in third-down conversion rate at 57.5 percent.

And they lead the entire NFL in red-zone touchdown percentage at 84.6 percent.

Read those three again, Seahawks Nation. Best in football at gaining yards. Best in football at converting third down. Best in football at finishing drives once they get close.

Eighty-four point six percent in the red zone means that when the San Francisco 49ers reach your twenty-yard line, they score a touchdown roughly five times out of six. They don’t settle for field goals. They don’t stall out.

And on top of all of it: they have not allowed a single sack in four games — the first team to open a season that way in twenty-one years, since the 2005 Indianapolis Colts, and only the fifth team to do it since the league started tracking sacks in 1963.

The line doing it is Trent Williams at left tackle, Jake Brendel, Dominick Puni and Colton McKivitz, plus a newcomer named Connor Colby. And the number underneath the streak is the scary one: Brock Purdy has faced pressure on just thirty-five drop-backs all season — the second-fewest of any team in football. It isn’t that he’s escaping pressure. It’s that pressure isn’t arriving.

And Macdonald on Purdy, refusing to play the underrating game: “I’d have a hard time underrating him. I think he’s a hell of a player.” Then the specifics — he throws it at all three levels, he throws on time, and he throws into windows with a timing that makes it hard to defend. Which is why Macdonald said the rush plan “is kind of not a one-man job for us. It’s an eleven, twelve-man job.”

Now the other side.

Seattle’s defense allows an NFL-low 246.8 yards per game. Fewest in football. Fewest yards per play in football at 4.0. Fifth in points allowed.

So this is the most efficient offense in the sport against the stingiest defense in the sport — in a building where that offense has scored nine total points across its last two visits.

Something is going to break today, and whichever way it breaks tells you who’s winning this division.

Now the part I love, because Shanahan didn’t just admit he can’t score on Seattle. He told us exactly why, out loud, on national television.

His answer for how you beat this defense: “You better be able to run the ball.”

And then he explained the mechanism: Seattle keeps its safeties deep and simply takes away every big play until you prove you can run it. You don’t get a shot downfield until you’ve earned it on the ground.

So let’s check the receipts on that, because they’re brutal and specific.

In the 2025 season opener — a game San Francisco won, 17-13 — they ran the football thirty-six times for 119 yards.

In the two games they lost, they managed 161 rushing yards on thirty-nine attempts combined.

Thirty-six carries and a win. Thirty-nine carries across two games and two blowout losses.

So the single number I’m watching today is not Brock Purdy’s passing yards. It’s San Francisco’s rushing attempts.

If San Francisco runs it thirty-plus times, Shanahan is executing his own stated blueprint, and that means the safeties have to come down and Seattle’s whole structure gets stressed.

If Purdy throws it forty times, Seattle has already won the argument — because that means the run didn’t work, and the only way they’re beating this defense is by asking their quarterback to be perfect for sixty minutes.

And here’s the beautiful part: Macdonald said the same thing from the other direction on Friday. Asked about the sack streak, he barely mentioned the offensive line: “They’re running the ball. They’re staying ahead of schedule. There’s very limited third downs. When they do throw it, they’re playing on time.”

The sack streak, the third-down rate and the run game are not three facts. They’re one fact wearing three hats. You cannot sack a quarterback who is never in third-and-long, and he’s never in third-and-long because he keeps running it on first down.

Win first down and the whole thing collapses. That’s the entire game plan, and both head coaches just described it to us in public.

So it’s Stefon Diggs.

The rule of this channel, first: This is not a report. Nobody has said this. No insider, no source, nothing.

And then two facts that end it in about fifteen seconds.

Fact one: Stefon Diggs signed a one-year, twelve-million-dollar contract with the Washington Commanders on August fifth. He’s been on their roster for over two months. He grew up in Maryland — he went home. He is not a free agent and there is no phone call to make.

Fact two: he’s hurt. As of October eighth he was a did-not-practice with a hamstring injury.

So the proposal is to go sign an injured player who is under contract with another football team.

And twelves, that’s two weeks in a row. Last week the big idea going around was a linebacker under contract with Kansas City through 2028, ranked one spot below the linebacker Seattle already employs. This week it’s an injured receiver on Washington’s roster.

And the irony is almost too neat, because Washington is the team that ended Seattle’s twelve-game winning streak, and Washington is the team Seattle just reached into and poached Robert Henry Jr. from. Seattle already went into that building and took something this month.

It wasn’t the receiver.

But here’s the part that actually matters, and it’s the best thing I found all week, because it doesn’t just say Diggs is unavailable. It says the entire idea is pointed the wrong direction.

Here is Seattle’s offense in heavy personnel — that means zero, one or two wide receivers on the field, with extra tight ends and backs instead:

Used on 62 percent of plays — third-highest rate in the National Football League. And in those groupings Seattle ranks eighth in the league in EPA per play, eighth in success rate at 52.7 percent, and posts an explosive-play rate of 13 percent — behind only Buffalo and San Francisco.

Now here is Seattle’s offense in light personnel — three or more wide receivers:

Twenty-fourth in the league in EPA per play. Twenty-seventh in success rate, at 38.2 percent.

Do you see it?

This offense is elite when it puts fewer receivers on the field and bad when it puts more.

So the big idea going around is: go acquire a fourth wide receiver, so that you can play more snaps of the personnel grouping where you are currently the twenty-seventh-best team in football.

That’s not a bold swing. That’s pouring water into the bucket with the hole in it.

What the data actually says this offense wants is another blocker or another tight end, not another target. And look at what Seattle has actually been doing: a running back signed to the fifty-three, a running back signed to the practice squad, two tight-end screens to AJ Barner against Washington for 32 and 15 yards, and Rashid Shaheed taking carries out of the backfield — a plan Macdonald confirmed has existed since Brian Fleury walked in the door.

Heavy. More bodies, fewer receivers. That’s the identity, and it’s working.

And the argument on the internet is that this group needs an injured thirty-two-year-old from another team’s roster.

Now, the red zone, because that’s the piece’s strongest claim and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal.

Here’s the football logic, and it’s the thing the whole argument misses. A red zone is defined by the absence of space. There is no deep third. There’s no room behind the safety, because the safety is standing on the goal line. Every route gets shorter and every window gets smaller.

So what wins down there? Not separation over forty yards. Bodies, leverage and a run threat that forces eight men to stay honest. Which is exactly the personnel group where Seattle is already eighth in the league — and exactly the thing Seattle has lost, because its first-round running back is on injured reserve and the other one has been on the PUP list all year.

The red-zone answer for this football team isn’t a fourth wide receiver. It’s Zach Charbonnet walking back through that door, and the deadline for that is October twenty-second.

Now let me be fair, because the piece is not wrong about everything, and this is the part I’d actually complain about if I were in that building.

Third down is a genuine problem.

One analysis has Seattle thirty-first in the league, converting roughly 31 percent of its third downs through four games. And for context on how bad that is for this organization: under Ryan Grubb in 2024, Seattle converted 37.6 percent, which ranked 21st. Under Klint Kubiak in 2025, 39.8 percent, 16th. In Washington in Week 3 it was three-for-nine, on an afternoon when they averaged 2.4 yards a carry.

And Macdonald admitted it on Friday with zero spin: “We’ve fallen short right now on our third down run-pass. Okay, well, let’s attack the process.”

So yes. Third down is broken. But now put it next to the heavy-light numbers, because together they explain each other and almost nobody has connected them.

Third down is the down where you are forced into light personnel.

You can’t line up on third-and-eight with two tight ends and a fullback. You need bodies running routes. So Seattle gets pushed out of the grouping where it’s eighth in football and into the grouping where it’s twenty-seventh — and then defenses blitz it, because they know exactly what’s coming.

So the fix isn’t a better receiver. The fix is not being in third-and-eight in the first place.

Which is the same answer as beating the 49ers. Win first down.

That’s the most useful sentence in this entire video, and it applies to both sides of the football today. Seattle wins first down on offense and it never has to live in its worst personnel group. Seattle wins first down on defense and San Francisco’s sack streak and third-down rate die together.

Everything on this football team right now comes back to first down.
