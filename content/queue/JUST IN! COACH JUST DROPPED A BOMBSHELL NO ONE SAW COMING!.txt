It’s done. Both of them.

Zach Charbonnet is on the fifty-three-man roster. Seattle activated him off the physically-unable-to-perform list on Thursday, the last possible day, and he took his second straight full practice.

And Elijah Arroyo’s season is over. ESPN’s Adam Schefter reported it: Arroyo had shoulder surgery on Thursday and went on injured reserve.

Seahawks Nation, here’s the part nobody has connected, and it’s the coldest piece of arithmetic in this sport.

Seattle placed Elijah Arroyo on injured reserve to open the roster spot for Zach Charbonnet.

One man’s season had to end so another man’s could start. Same roster, same day, same fifty-three slots. That is the business, stated as plainly as it ever gets.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. And today I’ve got the part of Mike Macdonald’s Wednesday session that nobody covered — including me, yesterday, because I didn’t have it.

And I’m telling you straight: the best football answer of the entire week is in the last four minutes of that transcript, and it’s about exactly what happens Sunday night. He explained, without being asked directly, why he is not going to load the box against Kenneth Walker. And his reasoning is better than anything you’ll hear on a broadcast.

Quick note on the title before we go. Macdonald hasn’t been silent — he talks to the media several times a week, and this was a routine Wednesday availability. “Breaks his silence” is a phrase that sounds urgent and promises a reveal that isn’t there. But there’s something better in here than silence being broken. Let’s get to it.

Part one: the moves, with the dates and the mechanics.

Charbonnet. His twenty-one-day practice window opened October first and closed Thursday, October twenty-second. Two outcomes only: the fifty-three-man roster, or PUP for the rest of the year. Seattle chose the roster.

He’s coming back from ACL surgery in February — a knee he tore in the divisional-round win over San Francisco. And on Wednesday he was a full participant for the first time since he started practicing on October first, then went full again Thursday.

But keep Macdonald’s exact words, because they haven’t changed: he said there’s a “possibility” Charbonnet plays “in some capacity” Sunday night. Activation is paperwork. Playing is a football decision. Two full practices is extremely encouraging. It is not a promise of twenty carries.

Arroyo. Shoulder, hurt in Denver, surgery Thursday, out for the season. He finishes 2026 with one catch on two targets for 17 yards in six games.

And I want to say something about that, because a stat line that small invites people to shrug. He was a second-round pick, fiftieth overall, out of Miami, and last year as a rookie he had fifteen catches for 179 yards and a touchdown. He was twenty-three years old and the plan was built partly around him. Now he’s had his shoulder rebuilt in October and he’ll watch the rest of it. That’s a rough year for a young player and he deserves a sentence that isn’t about roster math.

What Seattle actually loses is the two-tight-end package — Arroyo next to A.J. Barner, which is the look that forces a linebacker to honor the run and a safety to honor the seam. That’s why Seattle signed Josiah Deguara, a blocking tight end, and Jackson Acker, a fullback, on Monday. They’re not replacing his hands. They’re replacing his job.

Part two — and this is why you’re here. The best answer Macdonald gave all week.

A reporter asked him about the “box” — the number of defenders he puts near the line of scrimmage — and whether that’s determined by the quality of the run game he’s facing or by who’s in the backfield.

Now, remember the context: Kenneth Walker the Third is coming, with 655 rushing yards and eight total touchdowns in five games, the first player since Jim Brown in 1963 to start a season like that. Every instinct in the world says: put more men in the box.

And here’s what Macdonald said:

“I would say, like — not all coverage structures... more people doesn’t equal better run defense.”

More people doesn’t equal better run defense.

Stop and let that land, because it is the opposite of what almost every fan believes, and he’s right.

Then he explained the mechanism: “There’s ways to manipulate who has who, and wherever you put everybody — in here, out here’s lighter. You know, you drain this guy out, it’s the same amount of guys you have in split safety. It’s just not like one-to-one all the time.”

Let me translate that, because it’s important. A defense has eleven men and they all have jobs. Moving one closer to the line doesn’t create a defender — it just takes him from somewhere else. If you drop a safety down into the box, you now have one high safety instead of two, and the entire back half of your defense has thinner coverage. You didn’t add. You moved.

And then he told a story that is the best piece of coaching logic I’ve heard all season:

“We used to think like, hey, we just blitz the run. We know it’s run. Why don’t we just blitz eleven guys? Like — well, a guy gets cut out and the ball’s out the gate, and you thought you were sweet for blitzing it, but somebody... you know, the result doesn’t tell you you’re sweet.”

That’s the whole thing right there.

Picture it. You know it’s a run. So you send everybody. Eleven men crashing forward. And one single offensive lineman cuts one of your defenders — gets low, takes out his legs, removes him from the play — and now there is nobody behind that gap. Nobody. Because you sent them all. The back goes through the hole and there is forty yards of grass and not one defender with leverage.

“The ball’s out the gate.”

And the line that should be taught in every football class: “The result doesn’t tell you you’re sweet.”

Meaning: you can make a terrible decision and get away with it, and you can make the right decision and get burned. If you blitz eleven and the offense happens to run into it, you look like a genius — and you were still wrong. A coach who judges his calls by outcomes instead of by process will eventually ruin his defense.

That is an enormously mature way to think, and it’s the same philosophy he gave in the same press conference about starting fast: “You can’t control starting fast. You can control your mentality, how well you’re on your assignments, how well we’re aligned, what we’re going to call and when.”

Process over result. Both times.

And then he explained why Seattle prefers split safety — two high safeties — even against a great running back:

“If you’re in split safety, you tend to have a little bit more layers to your defense, a little bit more overlap. So yeah, that’s why we tend to go a certain direction.”

“Layers” and “overlap.” That’s the entire argument for a light box, and it connects directly to the other thing he said about run defense on Wednesday:

“You see the ball gets out — it’s either a missed tackle, or multiple missed tackles, or a bad leverage or a bad angle by the third-level player. So it’s got to be team defense.”

Those two answers are the same answer. Runs don’t become touchdowns at the line of scrimmage. They become touchdowns at the third level, when the man who was supposed to be the last layer took a bad angle — or wasn’t there, because you blitzed him.

So here’s the prediction I’ll make from all of this: Seattle is going to play two-high safeties against Kenneth Walker on Sunday night, and some of you are going to be furious about it in the second quarter.

He will give up some four- and five-yard runs on purpose. He’s doing it to make sure that when Walker breaks the line of scrimmage — and he will, a few times — there are two men deep instead of one, and the eight-yard run doesn’t become a sixty-yard run.

Seattle’s thirty-five game streak without allowing a hundred-yard rusher wasn’t built on stuffing people at the line. It was built on never letting anybody out the gate.

Part three: the other answer I missed, on the defensive line.

He was asked how intricate the up-front work has to be to let Seattle play all that two-high safety. And he deflated the premise immediately:

“I’d say it’s not as intricate as you might imagine. It’s just — it’s just a hard ask, you know, and it takes great players to do that. It takes great coaches to get that done and get the guys to buy in to play that way.”

“It’s just a hard ask.” That’s refreshingly honest. Playing light up front isn’t a magic trick — it’s asking four or five men to hold up against five or six blockers and not get moved. No scheme makes that easy. You just need men who can do it.

And then: “We try to take stress off of — believe it or not, we do try to take stress off our defense, our D-linemen, as much as we can.”

“Believe it or not.” He knows how it looks. He knows people watch Seattle play light boxes and think he’s hanging his front four out to dry.

And then the part that tells you about that locker room: “When we’ve played great, our best football, those guys are on fire and they set the tone for the rest of the defense. And I think they’re the first ones to say, like, when we’re not meeting up to our standard, they could play better as well.”

The first ones to say it. That’s Leonard Williams — three years, ninety million dollars, twenty-one sacks since 2024, most of any defensive tackle in football — and Jarran Reed, and DeMarcus Lawrence, putting their own hands up. That’s what a veteran front looks like from the inside.

And there’s one more layer to the light-box answer that I want to give you, because it’s the counter-argument and he addressed it himself.

A reporter framed the question as a choice: is your box size driven by the quality of the run game you’re facing, or by who’s in the backfield? Meaning — do you respect the scheme, or do you respect the man?

Macdonald’s answer refused both options: “I think it’s more of like a what we do well, who we have type of thing.”

He’s not building his defense around Kenneth Walker. He’s building it around his own personnel. Same philosophy he gave about the run game on offense — “keep it within our wheelhouse, then target within the wheelhouse.” Identity first, opponent second.

And then he gave the tell for how he actually decides: “A lot of times the routes are, you know, drives your front, your coverage opportunities as well. You can look at it from like a top-down perspective.”

“Top-down.” That’s a defensive coordinator saying he often starts with the coverage and works forward to the front — not the other way around. Most fans assume a defense is built front-first: stop the run, then figure out coverage. Macdonald is telling you he frequently does the opposite, because the pass is where games are decided and the coverage structure dictates how many bodies are left over.

Which is why you’ll see two safeties deep on Sunday even on second-and-two. It isn’t disrespect for Kenneth Walker. It’s that the coverage comes first and the box gets what’s left.

Part four — the van ride. And I’m going to let him tell it, because it’s the most human thing any coach has said this season.

Somebody asked how it ended with Kenneth Walker. Whether there was a goodbye.

“Well, I think for everybody after the Super Bowl, no one’s really hanging out. But yeah — we drove, we had this big media session. It was just us in the van for like an hour. I mean, we’re talking all sorts of stuff. Really nothing about football.”

An hour in a van, the morning after winning the Super Bowl. The head coach and the MVP, going to the MVP press conference together, talking about anything except the thing they’d just done.

And then somebody pushed: what was that van ride actually like?

“Could it get any better? You just won the Super Bowl. You’re hanging out with the MVP. Life’s good. Yeah — it was awesome.”

“Could it get any better.”

And then, asked if they talked about the game at all: “Of course we did. Yeah. I mean, but you know — we’re not talking about future or anything.”

We’re not talking about future. Eight months later the future arrived, and it’s wearing red on Sunday night.

His closing on Walker: “I have nothing but great memories for him. We appreciate the heck out of him. Guys — Super Bowl MVP, helped us win a championship. These things happen, you know. We have to move forward now. But got nothing but great things and great memories of Ken.”

“These things happen. We have to move forward now.” That’s a man who understands the business and refuses to be bitter about it. And earlier in the same session: “I love Ken. I respect the heck out of him. I’m happy for him.” Followed by: “Obviously we have to do a great job of stopping him this week.”

Both things, at once, honestly. That’s the whole press conference.

Part five: the Thursday injury report — and there’s a lot here, on both sides.

Seattle’s good news is real. Anthony Bradford was upgraded to full participation — he’d been limited — which matters because Macdonald said Monday that if the week went well he’d play “a significant amount, if not the whole game.” The week went well. Charbonnet: full. Rashid Shaheed: full with a hip. Beau Stephens: full. And Tyrice Knight upgraded to full after an oblique — he started in place of Drake Thomas last week.

And the bad news is concentrated in exactly one room: linebacker.

Drake Thomas did not practice for a second straight day with his ankle. Chazz Surratt did not practice for a second straight day with a calf — he’s now missed three games. And Connor O’Toole did not practice with a hamstring.

Three linebackers didn’t practice Thursday. Against the man who’s the first since Jim Brown. And remember what Macdonald said about run defense: “a bad angle by the third-level player.” The third level is exactly where Seattle is thin.

Also limited: DeMarcus Lawrence with a knee — though listed as resting, not injury-related — Uchenna Nwosu with a hip, and Drew Lock with a right elbow, which is new.

Now Kansas City, and they are not healthy either.

Rashee Rice did not practice — hamstring and knee. That’s a major piece of their passing game. Travis Kelce was limited with an ankle. L’Jarius Sneed limited with a knee. Brashard Smith limited with a hamstring — that’s their backup running back, which means more Walker, not less.

And here’s the one that could decide this game: Kansas City’s offensive tackles.

Josh Simmons did not practice — listed as a back issue and a personal matter — and reports say he was placed on injured reserve Thursday. And Jaylon Moore reportedly missed both Wednesday and Thursday with a concussion.

That’s potentially both starting tackles. Against DeMarcus Lawrence and Uchenna Nwosu and a Seattle defense that just held Denver to 168 total yards.

If Kansas City is starting two backups at tackle, that is Seattle’s best path to winning this football game — and it’s a much better path than anything a trade deadline could have offered.

Oh — and Patrick Mahomes was a full participant. Knee. Which is exactly what Macdonald told you on Wednesday when he said the under-center usage “speaks to his health.” The injury report agreed with him two days later.

Let me close.

Two things got decided this week and neither one was a trade.

Elijah Arroyo’s season ended on a surgeon’s table Thursday morning, and the roster spot that opened is the one Zach Charbonnet is standing in. That’s football. It’s not fair and nobody pretends it is.

And a head coach who could have spent his Wednesday complaining about his run game instead spent four minutes explaining why sending more men at a great running back is usually how you get beaten by one.

“More people doesn’t equal better run defense.” And: “The result doesn’t tell you you’re sweet.”

Sunday night, Kenneth Walker the Third comes back to the building where he won a championship, in red, against a defense that hasn’t allowed a hundred-yard rusher in thirty-five straight games — with three linebackers who didn’t practice on Thursday, and a right guard who just went full.

And a coach who, eight months ago, spent an hour in a van with him talking about nothing.

Go through, not around.

Twelves, two questions. First, the one I actually want to argue about: do you trust the light box? Macdonald just told you he’d rather keep two safeties deep and give up four yards a carry than load up and risk one cut block turning into sixty. Against Kenneth Walker — do you back that, or do you want eight in the box and dare him? And second: with three linebackers banged up and both Chiefs tackles in trouble, does this game get decided by Seattle’s front four or by Walker in the second level? I think it’s the tackles.
