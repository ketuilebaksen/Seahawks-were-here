“Uchenna and Shaheed will be back.”

Seahawks Nation, there it is. That’s the change.

Uchenna Nwosu and Rashid Shaheed are playing against the Raiders.

And Mike Macdonald gave us a target date on Charles Cross, confirmed a brand-new quarterback development that nobody saw coming, and had to answer a question about whether this football team can even get on an airplane.

That last one is not a joke. We’ll get there.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four, Las Vegas is the matchup, and this is the final word out of the building before kickoff. Let’s go.

First, the returns — because this is the biggest news of the week.

Uchenna Nwosu is back. Rashid Shaheed is back.

And twelves, let me tell you why those two specifically matter so much.

Start with Nwosu. Seattle’s edge rusher room has been held together with tape. Nwosu out with an ankle. Derick Hall showed up on the report with a back problem. Connor O’Toole has been questionable with a foot. Seattle quietly re-signed outside linebacker Jamie Sheriff to the practice squad the day before Hall landed on that report.

And now you’re playing a rookie quarterback making his first NFL start — a quarterback whose documented flaw is HOLDING THE BALL TOO LONG.

Getting a real edge rusher back for that game is the single most useful thing that could have happened to this defense this week. You don’t need to invent exotic pressure against a rookie. You need guys who can win one-on-one and make him feel the pocket shrinking.

Nwosu being back is worth more today than it would be in almost any other game on the schedule.

Now Rashid Shaheed — and this is the one that changes the offense.

Twelves, I’ve been saying this for weeks: Shaheed is the only true vertical speed threat on this roster. And a national analyst said the quiet part out loud about his absence — his words were, “no Shaheed, and even though he hasn’t been great, AT LEAST THAT SPEED, you have to worry about that a little bit.”

That’s exactly right, and it’s not an insult. Shaheed’s value isn’t his catch total. It’s that a safety has to respect him.

And here’s why that matters RIGHT NOW more than ever.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba just crossed a thousand receiving yards in nine games, and he leads the entire NFL in target share. Every coordinator left on this schedule is planning to bracket him. A national panel called his target share “criminal” compared to everybody else and said flatly: “somebody else on the outside has to step up and make a play.”

You cannot double Jaxon Smith-Njigba with a safety if that safety is terrified of the man running past him on the other side.

Rashid Shaheed coming back is how you make doubling JSN expensive. That’s the whole mechanism.

And remember Drew Lock’s lever from this week: “run the ball, forcing them to get into single high coverages — and that opens up the pass game. Then you start hitting the pass game, they’ve got to play shell, and you come back to the run.”

Shaheed is the reason they have to play shell. He’s the threat that keeps that safety honest, and he’s back.

NOW — Charles Cross, and Macdonald gave us an actual date, which he almost never does.

“Charles got back to practice yesterday for the FIRST TIME in a while.”

And asked whether he was close to being available this week:

“I think it’s fair to say he should be ready for the GAME AFTER THE BYE. He’s done a great job working through all the things. We just couldn’t make this game, unfortunately.”

“He should be ready for the game after the bye.”

Twelves, that is Seattle’s starting left tackle targeting a return at San Francisco on November twenty-ninth.

And that is a genuinely big deal, because look at what that bye week is now carrying.

Charles Cross back at left tackle. Quenton Nelson back at left guard after his knee procedure — remember Macdonald said “music to my ears” two days after the surgery, and “I know he’s going to be back sooner than later if it’s under his control.” And Sam Darnold’s rib and quad getting two weeks instead of one.

Seattle plays today, then gets a bye, then goes to San Francisco. And they could walk into that 49ers game with their left tackle, their Pro Bowl guard, and their starting quarterback all back at once.

That bye week might be the most valuable thing on this entire schedule.

And I’ll say what I’ve been saying all week: Charles Cross getting back is the single best thing you can do for a quarterback managing an elbow and an oblique. Everything gets easier when nobody’s hitting him.

Just not today. Today it’s somebody else at left tackle again.

Now the quarterback, and the message is “steady as she goes.”

Asked how Drew Lock looked this week:

“Good. Good. He’s similar cadence and workload in terms of last week. So it’s good to have done it and gotten through the week well. So I know he’s confident and optimistic. So we’re in good shape.”

“It’s good to have DONE it and gotten through the week well.”

That’s the key phrase, twelves. The twenty-throw week isn’t an experiment anymore. It’s a procedure they’ve executed once and know works.

And a reporter pushed on it — Drew said the oblique is on the back of the muscle so it hasn’t affected throwing, is he AHEAD of where he was last week? Has he done more?

And Macdonald said: “I’d say it’s pretty similar to how we handled his workload last week.”

Same plan. Same rations. No acceleration.

And twelves, that’s the right call even though Lock says he feels better. Because here’s the thing about elbow tendinitis: it’s an overuse injury. The cure is throwing less. The second you reward feeling good with more throws, you’re back where you started.

They’re not chasing comfort. They’re protecting Sunday. Macdonald said it last week: “to make sure he’s feeling his best on Sunday.”

And Lock’s own version, which I still love: “this injury is what it is. It’s going to be ready to go by Sunday, and I’m going to go out and play NORMAL.”

AND NOW — the development I did not expect, and it’s the most interesting thing in this press conference.

Macdonald volunteered this. Nobody asked him:

“And I think it’s appropriate to say Jalen Milroe has done a TREMENDOUS job when he’s been in there with the ones. Just been really, really impressed. He’s worked extremely hard and he’s been very DECISIVE. He’s delivering the ball ACCURATELY and ON TIME. He’s done a great job filling in for Drew when we need him.”

Twelves, stop and count the words in there.

“Tremendous.” “Really, really impressed.” “Decisive.” “Accurately.” “On time.”

That is not a coach being nice to a rookie. That’s a scouting report, and it’s a good one.

And here’s why it matters so much — because of what the pre-draft scouting report said about him.

A national evaluator read us his own draft binder on Jalen Milroe a few weeks ago. He had him as his eighty-sixth player. Just under six foot two, 217 pounds, 4.4 flat at his pro day. And he said — using the baseball twenty-to-eighty scale, where eighty is the absolute ceiling — “he’s an eighty runner. You’re not going to find a better runner as a quarterback prospect. He gets the corner on everyone.”

But then came the criticism: “his eyes and feet, they’re not connected. He’s up on his toes. The ball sails. It is a work in progress as a passer.”

Now look at what Mike Macdonald just said.

“Decisive.” “Accurate.” “On time.”

Those are the exact three things that fix “eyes and feet not connected.”

Because that’s what the flaw IS. When your eyes and feet disconnect, you’re late — you decide, then your body catches up, and the ball comes out behind schedule and off platform. That’s when it sails.

Being decisive, on time, and accurate is literally the description of eyes and feet working together.

That doesn’t mean he’s fixed. One good practice week against your own defense is not an NFL game. But twelves, this is the first real public evidence that the thing people worried about is being worked on and is improving.

And then a reporter asked a brilliant follow-up: even if he doesn’t end up playing over this stretch, how valuable is it for Milroe to get all these reps?

And Macdonald’s answer is my favorite moment of the week:

“Yeah, it’s a great point. I was asking Sam during practice — I was about to say, like, hey, as funky as his situation is, it’s great to see what he’s capable of doing with the STARTERS. Anytime you’re down your starters, those are really valuable reps for anybody, but including the quarterback position. So he’s definitely taking advantage of it.”

“I was asking Sam during practice.”

Twelves, picture that. Sam Darnold — cracked rib, bad quad, can’t throw a football — standing on the practice field next to his head coach, WATCHING Jalen Milroe run the first-team offense, and the two of them are evaluating him together.

That’s the third week in a row we’ve gotten evidence of this. Macdonald told us Darnold is “kind of like a coach on the field right now” and that he’d “talked to him the most during practice I’ve ever had.” Cooper Kupp volunteered, unprompted, “Sam was awesome this week for Drew.”

And now the head coach is standing beside him asking his opinion.

A starting quarterback in a contract year is functioning as an assistant coach for the two men behind him on the depth chart. That is not a normal thing, and I don’t want anybody on this channel to take it for granted.

And the broader point Macdonald made is important: “anytime you’re down your starters, those are really valuable reps for anybody.”

That’s “the power of our seventy” showing up again. When you’re missing six starters, the men underneath them get real repetitions against real first-teamers — and that’s development you cannot buy any other way.

Drew Lock said it himself: “reps for quarterbacks is better than any tape they can watch, better than any footwork drills they can go do on the side.”

NOW — Leonard Williams, and this answer is the most honest thing Macdonald has said in a month.

Asked whether he’s been able to rule anybody out:

“Yeah, this is one of those things where you just have to — it’s kind of NOT GOOD UNTIL IT IS. So, not good yet.”

“It’s not good until it is. So, not good yet.”

Twelves, that’s the perfect description of a stinger and I want to explain it, because it’s different from every other injury on this report.

A stinger is a nerve injury. It’s a burning, numb, weakness sensation that comes from the nerves in the neck and shoulder getting compressed or stretched. And the thing about nerve injuries is that they don’t heal on a schedule. There’s no ninety percent. There’s no “he’s getting closer.” It’s bad, and then one day it isn’t.

And Macdonald described the actual barrier perfectly: “you’re able to move around and do all the things. It’s just — I mean, you know, stingers, it’s just MORE OF A CONTACT THING.”

“More of a contact thing.”

That’s why he was out there doing warm-ups and still can’t play. He can run. He can lift. He can cut. He just can’t take a collision — and he plays defensive tackle, which is nothing BUT collisions, on every single snap.

And then Macdonald said this, and it tells you who Leonard Williams is: “he’s doing a good job staying in shape and staying fresh, and he’s in the meetings. He’s doing all the LEO THINGS. He’s just not playing right now.”

“He’s doing all the Leo things.”

Twelves, Leonard Williams just missed his first football game since Week 4 of the 2024 season. Two full seasons and a playoff run without missing a single one. And the week he can’t play, he’s in every meeting doing everything a healthy Leonard Williams does.

That’s the most reliable player on this roster. And he’s still not playing.

And on Sam Darnold, Macdonald drew a really useful distinction between the two:

“Each are on their own track. I see Sam progressing with his rehab, and the guys are saying he’s doing a great job. We’ll take it a day and a game and a week at a time. And same thing with Leo — I think it’s just different with Leo. It’s just when he becomes able to play. And Sam’s probably more of a TIME issue. It’s more of a LINEAR PATH, I would say.”

“Sam’s is more of a linear path.”

That’s genuinely important, twelves. A cracked rib and a quad heal on a clock. Tissue knits. Every day is measurably better than the day before. That’s linear.

A stinger is not linear. It’s binary.

So when you’re asking “when does Sam Darnold come back,” the answer is a calendar question. When you’re asking about Leonard Williams, nobody can answer it — including the medical staff.

And with the bye coming after this game, Sam Darnold’s linear path has two weeks of runway. That lines up perfectly with what we’ve been hearing: the signs point to Darnold starting after the bye.

NOW — Jarran Reed, and this is the nicest news of the day.

Asked what’s going on with him:

“He had a personal issue that I’ll just leave as a GOOD personal issue — that he was excused from the team for a couple of days. Great to have him back. But he’ll be able to play in the game.”

“A good personal issue.”

Twelves, I’m not going to speculate about a man’s private life, and neither should anybody else. All we need to know is that something happy happened for Jarran Reed, Seattle let him go handle it, and he’s back and playing.

And I want to point out what that says about this building. A veteran defensive lineman gets excused for a couple of days in the middle of a game week — during a stretch where this team is missing six starters — because something good was happening in his family.

They let him go. And he came back and he’s playing.

That’s “we hired you to be Seahawks.” It cuts both ways.

NOW — the strangest question Mike Macdonald has been asked all season.

A reporter told him there was a GROUND STOP on air travel in the United States right then, and asked how that affects the team if there are delays.

And Macdonald said: “Yeah, I just heard about it walking in here from Dave. So I don’t know. I don’t know. We’ll play it by ear, and obviously we’ll adjust to how we need to adjust. So we have a plan for tomorrow and we’re leaving tomorrow, which helps obviously. So — or we’re TRYING to leave tomorrow.”

“Or we’re trying to leave tomorrow.”

Twelves, that got a laugh, and it’s also a real problem. This is an NFL football team that needs to get to Las Vegas, finding out mid-press conference that air travel in this country just got stopped.

But notice the response, because it’s the same response he gives to everything: “we’ll play it by ear and we’ll adjust to how we need to adjust.”

That’s the identical phrasing he used about the rookie quarterback this week — “make the best adjustments we possibly can as the game starts to unfold.” And about the offensive line changing three times in two weeks. And about six starters being out.

A ground stop. A quarterback with two injuries. A guard having surgery. A coordinator who’s now the other team’s head coach. “We’ll adjust.”

And the good news he flagged: they’re leaving SATURDAY, not the morning of. That gives them a cushion. A team that flies in the day before has room to absorb a delay. A team that flies in on game day does not.

NOW — the football question I actually care about, and Macdonald’s answer was carefully optimistic.

Asked how he’s seen the offense stacking plays, especially the run game:

“Well, I think having the CONSISTENCY — having the offensive line INTACT for another week — will help. Feels like we’ve had a really productive week. I feel like guys have been on the details. Practice has been good.”

“Having the offensive line intact for another week.”

Twelves, that is the whole thing, and I don’t think people appreciate how rare it’s been.

Count the configurations. Seattle traded for Quenton Nelson and moved Grey Zabel to right guard. Then Nelson needed knee surgery. Then Zabel went back to left guard and Anthony Bradford came back to right guard. All without Charles Cross at left tackle.

Three different interiors in two weeks.

And THIS week, for the first time in a month, nothing changed. Same five men, two weeks in a row.

And here’s the honest coaching note he added, which I appreciated: “I think it’s also appropriate to say, hey, it’s FRIDAY. We still have ample time between now and Sunday to feel really confident and have CLARITY, so we can go be DECISIVE to start the game.”

“Clarity, so we can be decisive to start the game.”

That’s the slow-start problem again, and he will not let it go. Three weeks running he’s brought it up unprompted. His words before the Arizona game: “it starts with being decisive.” And after it: “we want to be on our game to START games, for sure.”

He keeps naming it. That’s how you fix a thing.

And then the forward-looking part, which is the most optimistic he’s been all season:

“As we start to stack some reps here with the same crew, and our halfbacks gain more experience, and Charbonnet is kind of getting back in the flow of coming back from his injury — I think we should be OPTIMISTIC and we should EXPECT BETTER RESULTS as we go here.”

“We should expect better results.”

Twelves, Mike Macdonald does not talk like that. This is the man who after a seventeen to three win said “I thought we took a step today” and refused to say anything bigger.

“Expect better results” is as close to a promise as he’s come all year.

And one last football nugget, on Grey Zabel moving back to the left side.

Asked if he noticed anything different:

“Well, it’s probably TOUGHER THAN ONE MIGHT JUST ASSUME going from one side to another. So for both our guard positions — really our offensive line as a whole — we expect to take a step this week.”

“Tougher than one might just assume.”

And twelves, this is worth understanding, because it’s the thing I argued about when the Nelson trade happened.

Moving a lineman from left guard to right guard is not like switching hands on a steering wheel. Every single thing reverses. Your stance flips — which foot is back. Your first step goes the other direction. Your pull goes the other way. The teammate you’re combo-blocking with is now on your opposite shoulder. The hand you punch with changes.

Years of muscle memory, mirrored.

And now the head coach is confirming it: it’s harder than people assume.

Which is exactly why putting Zabel back on the left and Bradford back on the right — in Macdonald’s words, “where they banked all those reps” — was the right call. And they haven’t allowed a sack since.

And one final small thing I enjoyed, because it ties back to the whole season.

Asked how they decide whether to practice outside in the rain:

“We want to be out there as much as possible, and as long as the FOOTING’S okay. Our ground crew do a phenomenal job of keeping the fields in great shape. I can’t think of all the amount of times that we’ve actually had to be inside.”

Twelves, Drew Lock’s elbow injury happened in a rain practice. Macdonald told us so himself — “we practiced in the rain and he got it.”

And they’re still going outside, because footing is a safety issue and rain isn’t. That’s the right call, and it’s also a little bit funny given how this whole quarterback saga started.

Let me bring it home.

Here’s the final word before kickoff.

Uchenna Nwosu is back. Rashid Shaheed is back. Jarran Reed is back, after something good happened in his life. Drew Lock is on the same managed plan that produced seventeen to three, confident and optimistic. Jalen Milroe has been decisive, accurate and on time with the first team, and his head coach is standing next to Sam Darnold evaluating him. The offensive line is intact for a second straight week. Charles Cross targets the game after the bye. Sam Darnold’s recovery is a linear path with two weeks of runway. And Leonard Williams is doing all the Leo things except playing.

And across the field: a number one overall pick making his first NFL start, with a documented habit of holding the ball too long, against the number one pass defense in football — coached by the man who ran Seattle’s offense last year.

I’ll stay where I was: Seahawks twenty-four, Raiders thirteen. And with Nwosu and Shaheed back, I feel better about it than I did yesterday.

Two questions for you. First: which return matters more today — Uchenna Nwosu or Rashid Shaheed? I say Shaheed, and it’s because of what he does when he doesn’t touch the ball. He’s how you stop teams from doubling Jaxon Smith-Njigba for free.

And second: how do you feel about “tremendous, decisive, accurate, on time” being said about Jalen Milroe? Because I feel really good about it. The pre-draft knock on him was eyes and feet not connected. Decisive, accurate and on time is the cure. That’s progress you can hear.
