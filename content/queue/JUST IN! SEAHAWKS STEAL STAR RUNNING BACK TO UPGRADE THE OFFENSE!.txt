A 3-2 football team with the fourth-worst offense in the National Football League is supposed to change something.

Fire somebody. Change the play-caller. Rip up the scheme. That’s what everybody expects, and that’s what a reporter was circling when he asked Mike Macdonald whether the offense’s problems are just the growing pains of a new coordinator.

And Macdonald answered with the most important sentence of his season:

“Sometimes you grow best by doubling down on your core competencies and the things you believe in.”

That’s the decision.

Not an overhaul. Not a new voice. Double down.

And then, Seahawks Nation, he did something I have never heard him do in public. He spent the next ten minutes explaining exactly what he’s doubling down on — and he defined all of it, out loud, in three words.

Decisive. Relentless. Shocking.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. It’s Thursday, Seattle is 3-2, and they play in Denver tonight. Today we’re doing the full press conference — the three words and what each one actually means, the sentence about coaching that made me respect this man more than anything he’s said all year, the technical diagnosis of what’s broken in the pass game, and the fact that he disagrees with the Cooper Kupp spot. Let’s go.

First, where those three words come from, because they were not improvised in a press conference.

If you watched Hard Knocks in August, you’ve already seen them.

Seattle has a culture diagram — an actual drawn arrow, a spear, that this coaching staff built. And on it:

“12 As One” sits at the center in the biggest letters, circled by twelve green feathers off the jersey. And the “12” is deliberate — that diagram includes you, on your couch, as part of the unit.

“Chasing Edges” is written very large. That’s his oldest saying — the belief that you hunt improvement wherever it lives, however small it is.

“Running it forward” is the direction the arrow points. That’s his newest one, about never standing still.

And around the border, in smaller letters: “Thru, Not Around.” “Stack W.I.N.S.” “Old School Principles, New School Methods.” And my favorite: “A Style Nobody Wants To Play.”

And right at the tip of the arrow — the point of the spear, the sharpest part — three words:

“Decisive, Relentless and Shocking.”

So when a reporter asked him what a team needs in order to play decisive, relentless and shocking, he wasn’t being handed a soundbite. He was being handed his own diagram. And he unpacked it.

DECISIVE. His words:

“The decisiveness is — it’s mind, body and spirit, right? It’s clarity. It’s a sense of connectedness. It’s a confidence. It’s a feeling of: I’m letting go of the result, because I’m very confident in the things I’m going to do process-wise on the inputs. That’s decisiveness.”

“Letting go of the result because I’m confident in the inputs.”

Twelves, that isn’t a football cliché. That’s a performance philosophy, and it is the exact opposite of how a struggling team behaves.

A pressing team thinks about the scoreboard. A pressing quarterback thinks about the last interception. A decisive team thinks about its footwork — the inputs — and lets the outcome arrive on its own.

And look how precisely that lands on this specific problem. Sam Darnold’s own words Sunday night: “got to continue to trust myself, trust my feet, trust what I’m seeing.” Feet and eyes are inputs. The interception is a result. And his phrase for the standard he missed — “a us or nobody ball” — is an input too. It’s a rule about the throw, not a wish about the catch.

The head coach and the quarterback are describing the same fix in different vocabulary. That’s not an accident in a well-run building.

SHOCKING. And this is the one I got completely wrong before I heard it.

I assumed “shocking” meant explosive plays. Eighty-yard touchdowns. Making a stadium gasp.

Here’s what he actually said:

“Shocking is the level of detail. It’s all the things that go into what makes running a great slant route. It’s what makes E-A-T come to life. You know, people talk about silent tape all the time. What pops off a silent tape.”

Shocking means detail.

Not explosions. Detail. And he picked, as his example, a slant route — three steps and a cut, the single most basic route in the sport. He chose the most boring thing in football to illustrate the word shocking. That tells you everything about how this man’s brain works.

And then he gave away a scouting concept that every coach uses and nobody explains: silent tape.

You watch the film with the sound off. No commentary. No crowd. No score on the screen. Nothing but twenty-two bodies moving.

And then you ask one question: who pops?

Who is moving differently than everybody else? Who finishes the play? Who is still sprinting when the whistle has already gone? Who gets off a block like it personally offended him?

You cannot fake it on silent tape, because there’s no story to hide inside. No announcer telling you he’s a star. No scoreboard explaining why the effort mattered. Just a man, or not.

That’s the standard he’s asking for. Be the guy who pops with the sound off. And “E.A.T.” — one of this team’s internal sayings — is the shorthand he reached for about the daily, unglamorous version of it.

RELENTLESS. The shortest definition and the best one:

“Relentless is the spirit we have of playing without circumstance. We just go until the job’s done.”

“Playing without circumstance.”

Think about every excuse available to this football team right now. Four turnovers? Circumstance. A fourth-down spot you disagree with? Circumstance. A short week, a mile of altitude, a first-round running back on injured reserve and another on the PUP list? All circumstance.

And then came the self-audit, and it was honest in a way that surprised me:

“I think you watched a relentless team yesterday. I think we are those things a lot of the time — but it hasn’t been as connected and as consistent enough to put great Seahawk football out there, to the degree and the caliber that we believe we can get to.”

So his own grade: relentless, yes. Connected, not yet. Decisive, inconsistently. And “connected” is the word that keeps coming back — it’s on the diagram, it was in Cooper Kupp’s mouth Sunday, it’s in his definition of decisiveness.

He also gave credit where it was due: “We’re growing on defense, we’re growing on special teams. I think you saw our special teams take a jump yesterday. So offense is right there with it — but there’s a sense of urgency to get going.”

Now let me give you the sentence that made me respect this man more than anything he’s said all season, and I want you to sit with it.

He was asked to assess Brian Fleury’s work against the things going wrong — the run game, the turnovers. He gave Fleury real credit for “trying to find solutions and working through things that arise.” And he gave the honest frame: “We’re scoring points, but we’re not as efficient, and we’ve turned the ball over at a similar clip that we did early last year.”

And then he said this, completely unprompted:

“I don’t want execution to say it’s like we’re pawning anything off on players. It drives me crazy when coaches say, ‘Hey, we just got to execute better.’ It’s like — yeah, no kidding. Like, that’s our job.”

Stop.

“We just need to execute better” is the most common sentence in professional football. You have heard it a thousand times. And what it almost always means is: the plan was fine, the players failed. It is the most elegant way ever invented for a coach to blame the men who can’t hold a press conference.

And this head coach said out loud that it drives him crazy — and then said “that’s our job.” The coaches’. Not the players’.

Then the version he’s willing to use: “it’s our job as a football team to carry out our responsibilities better, and take care of the football.”

Our job. As a football team. That’s how you earn a locker room that calls its own meetings.

Alright. Now the technical diagnosis, because a reporter asked the sharpest question of the week and almost nobody has reported the answer.

Seattle is seeing both a higher time to throw AND a higher pressure rate than last season.

Now, normally those two numbers move in opposite directions. If your quarterback holds the ball longer, pressure rises because he’s standing there. If pressure rises, time to throw usually falls, because he’s getting rid of it in a panic. Both going up at the same time means something structural is wrong.

Macdonald’s answer: “We haven’t really clicked yet in terms of the timing of our pass game coinciding with the protections. It kind of falls on everybody’s area of responsibility.”

The timing and the protection are out of sync.

That means the route is breaking at a different moment than the pocket was designed to hold. The quarterback’s third step doesn’t match the receiver’s fifth. So he waits — and the pocket, built to last 2.6 seconds, is being asked to last three.

That is not a bad quarterback and it is not a bad offensive line. It’s two systems five games into learning the same language, under a coordinator who arrived in February.

And the contrast is stark. Through nine games last season, Seattle had allowed nine sacks — tied for the fewest in the entire NFL. In one November game, Darnold was pressured on three of twenty-four dropbacks — a 12.5 percent rate, a career low.

On Sunday he was sacked five times in one afternoon.

Same quarterback. Mostly the same line. Different timing.

And there’s a second structural thing happening that he confirmed. A reporter noted that last season Seattle ate up man coverage until teams stopped playing it — and that this year, teams are playing more man against them.

Macdonald’s answer was the most honest competitive admission of the day: “Teams are going to do what they think they can do successfully. So if we’re seeing more man, that means they think they can play more man. So we’re going to have to beat man.”

Translate that. The league has watched the tape and decided Seattle’s receivers can be covered one-on-one. That is a verdict from thirty-one other coaching staffs. And there’s no scheme answer to it — he didn’t offer one. The answer is: go win your matchup.

Which, by the way, is a terrific thing to say two days before playing a Denver secondary that will be missing both of its best players. We’ll get there.

Now the news items, and there are four worth your time.

One: Zach Charbonnet. Asked again whether he’d play: “As of right now, answer is no. Subject to change.” And then he explained the actual decision process, which nobody had laid out before:

“We have to have consensus from all parties that he’s ready to go — he’s the most confident and ready to go, that he’s had enough exposure to go play an NFL game. Those are the variables at play here.” And: “We’re trying to do right by the process right now.”

Consensus from all parties. The player, the medical staff, the trainers, the coaches — everybody has to agree. And the key phrase is “enough exposure”: has he taken enough full-speed reps to survive a real game.

And the clock is loud: Seattle must activate him to the 53-man roster by October twenty-second or he reverts to PUP for the rest of the season. That’s seven days from today.

Two: the Cooper Kupp spot — and this is news. Asked whether watching the film changed his view:

“No. They said the ball didn’t cross the line.” And then: “I could tell them I disagree, and I — but I don’t care what I think.”

He disagrees.

He watched it, he thinks they got it wrong, and in the same breath he said it doesn’t matter what he thinks. That is about as close as a head coach can get to saying the officials blew it while refusing to let anyone use it as an excuse. Playing without circumstance.

Three: Jaxon Smith-Njigba, who spent his own press conference tearing himself apart over one drop. Macdonald:

“I’m not surprised to hear that from JSN. But — is there a player you trust more with the ball thrown to him on the planet? I don’t think so. Doesn’t change one bit the way we see him. It was a really tough catch. I know he wants to come down with it. We obviously have faith that he’ll catch those passes. But there’s nobody we trust more than JSN.”

Is there a player you trust more on the planet? That’s your answer to anybody asking whether that drop costs him anything.

Four: Robert Henry Jr. was inactive Sunday, and here’s why: “We dressed three backs yesterday. He’s coming along. He’s doing a good job. He’s on the team. He could be ready to play if we needed him.” And asked whether he needs the full playbook first: “Does he need to know everything? No.”

So the man they poached off Washington’s practice squad is a healthy scratch for now — ready if needed, not yet needed.

And on Sam Darnold’s leadership, the thing that ties this whole press conference together:

“Sam’s a great representation of everyone we have on offense, and really on our football team... When your quarterback is one of those guys leading the charge saying ‘hey, I got to do X, Y and Z better’ — I think it sets the table for everybody else to have the same mentality. And if you have that mentality, you got a really good chance to improve.”

And then: “I really love our group. I love the people involved. I trust them. So let’s go get better.”

One more, because it’s a free coaching clinic. A reporter asked him to explain, for the fan on the couch, why you can’t just double-team the guy who keeps catching it — meaning Mike Evans, who had seven catches for 113 yards.

“When you double a guy, it just takes away from other areas. And just because he catches the ball doesn’t mean that you don’t have two people allocated to him.”

That second half is the part nobody understands. A completion against a double team is still a double team. Good receivers beat two men sometimes. And then the honesty: “Sometimes you’re right on why you’re in those coverages and sometimes you’re not... I understand it’s probably frustrating to see that, and frankly it’s a fair criticism.”

“It’s a fair criticism.” From a head coach, about his own defense, to a fan’s complaint.

Okay. Tonight — and the short week is its own art.

His framework: “You’re fighting a battle on two fronts. It’s the health-body battle of trying to get your body ready to play the best you can — so how you recover, and how you get reps without going full speed. There’s also something to be said about getting your body moving at some point before the game.”

And front two: “the balance of having a great, clear, concise, aggressive plan that you can go execute at a high level.”

Clear. Concise. Aggressive. On four days’ rest you cut the menu down and you attack. Don’t expect sixty new wrinkles tonight — expect fewer plays, run harder.

And altitude: recovery, nutrition, how they rest going into the week, “more of an awareness, and then maybe some strategy things involved in our front” — meaning rotation up front, which matters when the air is thin.

Kickoff is 8:15 Eastern at Empower Field at Mile High on Prime Video. Both teams are 3-2. Seattle is favored by three, total forty-one and a half.

And the final injury report is a gift. Jaxon Smith-Njigba is a full participant. Leonard Williams has no designation. Nobody on Seattle’s roster is listed as questionable. Out: Drake Thomas with the ankle — Tyrice Knight starts in his place — Chazz Surratt, and Charbonnet.

And on Denver’s side: Patrick Surtain the Second is OUT. Talanoa Hufanga is OUT. Their best cornerback and their best safety. Riley Moss is questionable with ribs.

So go back to Macdonald’s sentence: “we’re going to have to beat man.”

Tonight he gets to try it against a secondary missing its two best players, with the reigning Offensive Player of the Year at full participation.

Watch list, four things.

One: Seattle’s time to throw. If the ball is coming out faster, the timing clicked. That’s the whole diagnosis in one measurable.

Two: JSN against that secondary. Beat man. There will never be a better night for it.

Three: the play menu. Clear, concise, aggressive. If you see Seattle run the same six concepts over and over and win, that’s the doubling-down working in real time.

Four: who pops on silent tape. Watch one quarter with the sound off tonight and tell me who’s moving differently. That’s the whole third word.

Let me close.

The decision everybody was waiting for was a change. The decision Macdonald actually made was the opposite: double down on what you believe in, and get better at it faster.

And then he told us precisely what that means, which almost no coach ever does. Decisive — let go of the result and trust your inputs. Shocking — the level of detail in something as small as a slant route, the thing that pops off a silent tape. Relentless — play without circumstance, go until the job’s done.

Those three words have been sitting on the tip of an arrow on a wall since August. A diagram with “12 As One” in the middle of it, with twelve feathers around it, because he built the fans into the picture on purpose.

Four days of rest, a mile of altitude, and a Denver secondary with its two best players in street clothes.

No time to waste. Let’s get after it.

Go through, not around.
