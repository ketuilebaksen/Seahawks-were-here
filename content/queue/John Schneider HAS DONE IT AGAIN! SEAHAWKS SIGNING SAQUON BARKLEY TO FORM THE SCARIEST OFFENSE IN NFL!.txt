This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. And today I’m going to give you the single most useful idea I’ve found all season, because this document asks two separate questions — does JSN need help? and does the run game need a star? — and it never notices that they have the same answer.

But first, the title, and I have to be direct with you.

Seattle is not signing Saquon Barkley. There is no signing, no trade, no agreement, and no reported contact of any kind between the Seahawks and the Philadelphia Eagles. The idea is a written proposal by Cody Williams of FanSided — which this document correctly credits, and I’ll give it full marks for that.

And I have to say the other thing too: this is the fourth time I’ve been asked to make a Saquon Barkley video. I’m not annoyed, and I understand exactly why that headline keeps coming back. But I’ll make you a deal — I’ll handle Barkley properly in about four minutes with one new number that settles it, and then we’re going to spend the rest of this video on the stuff in this document that’s actually new, plus the real deadline that expires today.

Because there is one. Today is October twenty-second. Today is the Zach Charbonnet deadline. More on that at the end.

Let’s start with the idea that reframes everything.

The document asks whether Jaxon Smith-Njigba needs help, and honestly, it’s a fair question. It says the offense is “JSN, JSN, and more JSN,” and that’s true.

So here’s what that actually looks like in numbers.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba has 717 receiving yards in six games. Forty-nine catches. Seven touchdowns. A hundred and nineteen and a half yards per game.

And the number that stopped me: he’s accounting for roughly forty-nine percent of all of Seattle’s receiving yards — which, per ESPN Insights, is the highest share of any player in the National Football League. The record for that is Ken Burrough’s 1975 season at about fifty point six percent.

His pace projects to about two thousand and thirty-two yards. Calvin Johnson’s all-time single-season record is 1,964. Nobody in NFL history has reached two thousand.

So now hear the question again: should Seattle trade a draft pick for a receiver to take targets away from that?

No. And here’s the part that matters — the reason JSN gets bracketed has nothing to do with the other receivers.

Watch what Denver did last Thursday. They put two men on him and held him to two catches for eleven yards in the first half. Seattle had ninety-one total yards at halftime and trailed 14-3.

Why could Denver afford to do that? Because Seattle’s run game is ranked near the bottom of the league, so a defense can commit a safety to doubling your best receiver and still stop the run with the men it has left.

That’s the whole thing. A bracket isn’t a receiver problem. It’s a run-game problem wearing a receiver problem’s clothes.

You cannot buy your way out of a double team by adding a fourth wideout. You get out of it by making a defense keep a safety honest — which is what a credible run game, two tight ends, and heavy personnel do. Which is exactly what Seattle spent this week buying.

So both halves of this document have the same answer, and it isn’t a star. It’s blocking.

Now — the three receivers, with real numbers. And the document has them in exactly the wrong order.

Jerry Jeudy is the most available of the three, and that’s the problem with him.

Jordan Schultz reported that Cleveland is open to moving him and wants a third- or fourth-round pick — and that interested teams are balking at that price. The expectation is the cost drops closer to November tenth, maybe to a late fifth-rounder or a swap of Day 3 picks in 2027. Adam Schefter has fueled the same story.

So why is a 27-year-old first-round talent that cheap? Here’s why.

683 receiving yards in his last twenty-two games. In 2025 he caught 50 passes on 106 targets for 602 yards across seventeen games — that’s under forty-eight percent of his targets caught. He plays about fifty-eight percent of Cleveland’s offensive snaps.

And the money: a $10.378 million cap hit in 2026 and $13.472 million in 2027.

Read those two facts together. Ten point four million dollars this year for a man producing 683 yards over twenty-two games. Seattle’s entire remaining cap space is roughly twelve point eight million. You’d be spending eighty percent of your flexibility on a receiver who’d be your number three.

He’s available because he isn’t worth what he costs. That’s not a bargain. That’s a clearance rack.

Brian Thomas Jr. is the best player on the list and the least gettable.

The document is right that the talent is real — ten touchdown catches as a rookie is not an accident. But look at the situation. Jacksonville is 4-1. Four and one. Teams at 4-1 do not sell good young receivers in October.

His 2026: nine catches on eighteen targets for 105 yards and a touchdown in five games — including zero catches on three targets in the Week 5 win over Philadelphia. And Parker Washington has become Jacksonville’s top receiver.

The Athletic’s Jonathan Jones reported Thomas’s name keeps coming up around the league, which is real — but Jacksonville is described as unlikely to rush anything.

And now the price, which is the part nobody says out loud. Jones noted that Houston’s willingness to give up a 2027 second-round pick for Marvin Harrison Jr. raises Thomas’s asking price. That’s your floor: a second-round pick. For a 24-year-old with nine catches this season, from a team that’s winning.

That’s not a trade. That’s a bidding war Seattle would lose.

And then Jauan Jennings — where the document finds something genuinely smart and then points it the wrong way.

The connection is real and I love that they found it: Jauan Jennings worked with Brian Fleury at the San Francisco 49ers. Fleury was San Francisco’s tight ends coach before Seattle hired him, and Jennings is a legitimately excellent blocking receiver — which fits a scheme built on motion and heavy personnel better than a pure speed guy would. That’s a sharp observation and it deserves credit.

Now here’s the number.

Jauan Jennings has two catches for nine yards in four games this season. On two hundred and one offensive snaps.

Two hundred and one snaps. Two catches.

He’s with the 5-0 Minnesota Vikings on a one-year deal. And the reporting says he isn’t going anywhere: Tom Pelissero flagged the trade speculation and then predicted Jennings stays in Minnesota — the team still believes he can help, and Jennings wants to be there.

And the document’s best line is backwards. It says: imagine him against San Francisco, facing his former team. But the actual speculation, from Ian Rapoport, is that Jennings returns TO San Francisco — because of the injury to Deebo Samuel.

So the dream scenario isn’t him lining up against the 49ers in Seattle colors. It’s him lining up against Seattle in red and gold.

So there’s your receiver market: one guy nobody wants at his price, one guy whose team is 4-1 and would cost a second, and one guy with two catches on two hundred snaps who’s told people he’s happy where he is. The document’s own verdict was “none of these guys feels like a home run,” and that’s exactly right. Credit where it’s earned.

And before we leave the receivers, let me deal honestly with what the document says about the men already in that room, because it makes three specific criticisms and they deserve three specific answers.

On Cooper Kupp, the document says he’s good but “up there in age” and “not as fast as he used to be.” Fair on both counts — he isn’t a vertical threat anymore. But here’s what he actually is to this team right now.

In overtime in Denver, on third-and-seven, Cooper Kupp was the open man. Darnold’s own words: “I should have just gotten it down to Coop. Coop ran a really good route right under the sticks, and I feel like he would have caught it and got the first down.”

That’s not speed. That’s a man who gets himself exactly as far as you need and turns around — the least glamorous, most valuable job in football. And then, instead of pointing out he was open, Kupp went to a podium and said: “We are not doing a good enough job offensively to allow Sam to play in rhythm.”

You don’t trade for that. You can’t. A receiver who sits at the sticks in overtime and then defends his quarterback in public is not a problem on this roster.

On Rashid Shaheed, the document says he has speed but isn’t a great route runner. Maybe. Here’s his Denver game: he fumbled in the first half, and then he had a 42-yard catch, the two-point conversion that made it 14-11, ten yards on a third-and-six on the final drive of regulation, and a 31-yard punt return with under a minute left that set up the tying field goal.

That’s a man who deleted his worst moment and went back to work four separate times in one night. Route-running critiques are fine. That’s not a player you’re trying to replace.

On Tory Horton, the document’s complaint is the most interesting one: “the team just doesn’t seem to want to throw him the ball very much.” And that’s the right observation with the wrong conclusion.

When one receiver is absorbing forty-nine percent of your team’s receiving yards, everybody else’s target share gets squeezed. It isn’t that Seattle dislikes Horton. It’s that there’s one football, and roughly half of it is going to Smith-Njigba. Adding a fourth receiver to that room doesn’t fix Horton’s usage. It makes it worse.

So the honest version of the receiver conversation is: Seattle doesn’t have a talent problem at receiver. It has a possession problem. Not enough drives, not enough plays, not enough snaps — because the run game is twenty-ninth in the league and the quarterback is getting hit on two of every five dropbacks.

Alright. Barkley. Four minutes, and then we’re done with him for good.

The Cody Williams proposal is a 2027 third-round pick, a 2027 seventh, and a 2028 second — Williams himself called it a “hefty haul,” and he built it off San Francisco’s Christian McCaffrey trade, discounted because Barkley is three years older than McCaffrey was. The document reports all of that accurately.

Here’s the number that ends it, and I want to pair it with its opposite so the lesson sticks.

Barkley’s 2026 base salary is one point three million dollars — tiny, because almost all of his money was paid up front as signing bonus years ago. And the rule is: when you trade a player, every remaining dollar of that spread-out signing bonus accelerates onto your cap at once — and it does not travel with him.

So Philadelphia trading Barkley saves about one million dollars and absorbs roughly twenty-six million in dead cap. The seller can’t afford to sell. They’re also 2-3 and publicly trying to win this year. And Barkley did not practice all week and was ruled out against Carolina with a hamstring.

Now put Tony Pollard next to that. Final year of a three-year, $21.75 million deal, $6.74 million base salary — and Tennessee trading him would save about $7.25 million while eating only about $2 million in dead money.

Pollard: seller saves seven, eats two. Barkley: seller saves one, eats twenty-six.

Same league, same position, completely opposite outcomes. And the reason is one sentence: base salary travels with the player. Signing bonus stays with the old team. Learn that and you’ll never be fooled by a trade rumor again.

And one more thing about that proposal, because the document itself flagged it and then moved on — and it’s the most dangerous part.

The document says, correctly: “a second round pick in 2028 is no small thing.” That’s the smartest line in the whole piece, and I want to explain why.

A 2028 second-round pick is the most deceptive currency in football, because it costs nothing today. You don’t feel it. It isn’t a player leaving your locker room. It’s a line item two drafts away, and the men making the trade might not even be in the building when the bill arrives.

But think about what you’re actually handing over. You don’t know where that pick will land. If Seattle is a championship team in 2028, it’s pick fifty-something and the cost was small. If Seattle is rebuilding in 2028, that’s pick thirty-three or thirty-five — effectively a late first-rounder — and you’ve given away a starter to rent a thirty-year-old running back for eleven games in 2026.

That’s the trap: a future pick is cheapest exactly when you need it least, and most expensive exactly when you need it most. The worse your future turns out, the more that pick was worth. You’re betting against your own bad years.

And there’s a second mechanic worth knowing: comp picks can’t be traded before they’re awarded, and Seattle’s projected future hauls are projections, not assets. So a 2028 second isn’t a surplus pick Seattle has lying around. It’s a real selection out of a real draft.

The deadline is November tenth.

And I’ll give the document credit on the run-game numbers, because they check out: Green Bay is worst in the league at negative point three four EPA per rush, and Seattle is tied with the Raiders for next-to-last at negative point two six. The document also had the right instinct that acquiring a back would let Seattle not rush Charbonnet back — that’s genuinely good thinking.

Which brings us to today, and the thing that actually matters.

Today is Thursday, October twenty-second. Today is the Charbonnet deadline.

His twenty-one-day practice window opened October first. When it closes there are exactly two outcomes: he goes on the fifty-three-man roster, or he reverts to the physically-unable-to-perform list for the rest of the season. No extension. No third option.

And keep this distinction, because it’ll get reported both ways: activation is a paperwork decision. Playing is a football decision. Macdonald on Monday: “Zach is going to have a full week’s worth of reps, so that’s exciting. And there’s a possibility that you could see him on Sunday in some capacity.” Earlier: “He looks tremendous.” And ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler reported a “legitimate chance” he debuts against Kansas City.

There’s also a roster consequence nobody’s mentioning: putting Charbonnet on the fifty-three means somebody comes off it. Watch for that move today or tomorrow — it’ll tell you what Seattle values.

Let me close.

The document’s own conclusion is that this offense is “out of balance,” and that last year’s Super Bowl run was built on running the football. That’s completely right, and it’s the most honest sentence in the whole thing.

But the fix isn’t a receiver and it isn’t a star running back. The fix is that Seattle is the team with the highest single-player share of receiving yards in the National Football League — and that’s a symptom, not a strategy.

Forty-nine percent of your passing offense running through one man means every defensive coordinator knows exactly who to double. And you break a double team by making a safety stay home, not by adding a fourth receiver to the formation.

A right guard does that. A healthy running back does that. A fullback and a blocking tight end do that. A 683-yard receiver on a ten-million-dollar cap hit does not.

And the scariest offense in the NFL? It isn’t one Seattle buys at a deadline. It’s one where the man chasing Calvin Johnson’s record stops seeing two defenders on every snap.

Macdonald, Monday: “I’m excited about where we can go, honestly.” And then, immediately: “We’ve really had one good day’s worth of work up to this point.”

Go through, not around.
