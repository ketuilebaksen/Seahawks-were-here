There’s a rule in the National Football League that lets you walk into a rival’s building and take a player right off their practice squad — and they cannot stop you.

No trade. No draft pick. No compensation. No permission. You don’t even have to ask.

On Tuesday, John Schneider used that rule on the Washington Commanders — the team that ended Seattle’s twelve-game winning streak two weeks ago — and he took their running back.

Robert Henry Jr. is now on the Seattle Seahawks’ fifty-three man active roster.

Washington got nothing.

Seahawks Nation, local coverage of this move didn’t use the word “signing.” It used the word poaching. And once you see what this kid actually did in college, you’ll understand why Schneider didn’t wait around to be polite about it.

Third in the entire country in yards per carry. We’ll get there.

Today: exactly how Seattle stole a running back without giving up a thing, the résumé of the man they took — and it is a lot better than “practice squad guy” suggests — why this is the most John Schneider move of the entire season, and the Thursday injury news out of both buildings that changes Sunday. Let’s go.

Let me start with the mechanic, because almost nobody actually knows how this works and it’s the whole reason the title of this video says “steal.”

A player on another team’s practice squad is not locked up. Under the rules, any team can sign a practice squad player straight to its own fifty-three man active roster, at any point in the season, without owing one dollar or one draft pick to his old team.

Washington could not block it. Washington could not match it. Washington could not trade him first to get something back. They found out and that was the end of it.

Now, there is one catch, and it’s the catch that makes this a real decision instead of a free lunch: you have to put him on your active roster. Not your practice squad — your fifty-three. The league built it that way on purpose, so that you can only poach somebody if you genuinely value him enough to give up a roster spot for him.

And that’s exactly what Seattle paid. To make room for Robert Henry Jr., the Seahawks waived safety AJ Finley — a twenty-five-year-old who three weeks earlier had made the first start of his career in Washington on two days’ notice when Julian Love’s calf tightened up. Five tackles in three games, and Macdonald said the back end was organized that day.

So the price wasn’t a fifth-round pick. The price was a man’s job. That’s the honest version, and it’s why this move tells you Seattle actually believes in the player.

And here’s the human side of that rule, which nobody ever explains.

A practice squad player gets paid by the week. The minimums run roughly thirteen thousand dollars a week for a rookie, about seventeen and a half thousand for a veteran, with a ceiling around twenty-two thousand — paid across the eighteen weeks of the regular season. No signing bonus. No guaranteed salary. You’re a week-to-week employee of a football team.

The fifty-three man active roster is a completely different universe. The league minimum for a player on the active roster is a seven-figure annual salary.

So when Seattle called Robert Henry Jr. on Tuesday, they weren’t just offering him a better depth chart. They were offering him a raise of roughly ten times his paycheck, and Washington could not do a single thing about it. That’s why the rule exists in that shape — the league decided that if a team values a player enough to put him on its actual roster and pay him real money, the player has earned the right to go.

Which is also why the practice squad isn’t a parking lot anymore. It’s a shelf other teams shop from. And it cuts both ways: Seattle has to protect its own shelf too, which is part of why Velus Jones Jr. got signed from Seattle’s practice squad to the fifty-three the Saturday before the Chargers game, and why Dontae McMillan sitting down there matters more than it sounds.

One more mechanic while we’re here, because it decides Sunday: a team can elevate two practice squad players per game, the move has to be made the day before kickoff, and each player can only be elevated three times in a season before you have to actually sign him. The player reverts afterward without clearing waivers. That’s how Trevon Diggs and safety Maxen Hook got on the field against the Chargers — Hook for the second straight week.

So if you see Dontae McMillan’s name Saturday, that’s what you’re looking at.

Alright. So who is the man they stole?

Brady Henderson of ESPN reported it Tuesday, Seattle confirmed it, and the team’s own language was characteristically boring: Henry gives them “another option at running back.”

Let me give you the version that isn’t boring.

Robert Henry Jr. went to Lumberton High School in Mississippi. And in Mississippi, at Class 1A, he was Mr. Football twice. Two-time Mr. Football. He was a four-time all-state selection — four years. As a senior he ran for 1,961 yards and twenty-nine touchdowns.

And then in the 2020 Class 1A state championship game, he was named MVP — a hundred and thirteen yards and two touchdowns with a title on the line.

That’s the first level. Now the second.

He went to Jones College, junior college football in Mississippi. And in 2022 he was named the NJCAA Offensive Player of the Year — the best offensive player in junior college football in America. That same season he won the inaugural Walter Jones Trophy, which goes to the best junior college running back in the country. The first one ever awarded, and he’s got it.

Third level. UTSA.

In 2025 he was first-team All-American Athletic Conference. He ran for 1,045 yards and nine touchdowns.

And here is the number that made me stop and read it twice: 6.9 yards per carry — third in all of FBS.

Third. In the entire country. Out of every running back at every Division One program in America, two men had a better average than Robert Henry Jr.

So let’s be precise about the word “star,” because I’m not going to oversell you.

Is he an NFL star? No. He has zero career NFL carries. Zero. Everything I just told you happened in a Mississippi high school, in junior college, in the American Athletic Conference, and in the preseason — where he ran twenty-six times for a hundred and five yards and a touchdown across three games for Washington.

But has this man been a star at every single level of football he has ever played? Yes. Mr. Football twice. National juco player of the year. The first Walter Jones Trophy ever handed out. First-team all-conference. Third in America in yards per carry.

And nobody drafted him. Two hundred and fifty-seven picks went by in April and not one of them was Robert Henry Jr.

Which brings me to why this is the most John Schneider thing that has happened all season.

Look at what this front office actually did in about ten days, at one position group, and look at the pattern instead of the panic.

Jadarian Price, the first-round rookie, went on injured reserve with a chest injury — the one that started with a clean X-ray in Arizona and got worse for two weeks. Minimum four games. He’s out until November.

Macdonald, at the podium, with zero coach-speak: “We’re down runners. We need running backs.”

Then Seattle brought in Joe Mixon. A two-time Pro Bowler with more than seven thousand four hundred career rushing yards and seventy-four total touchdowns. Jay Glazer reported the meeting. The deal was essentially done — practice squad, pending a physical.

And then on Tuesday, Adam Schefter reported Seattle was not signing him after the physical. A U-turn. Mixon is still a free agent today.

So in the same seven-day stretch, this front office said no to seven thousand four hundred career rushing yards and yes to a junior college kid with no NFL carries.

That is not indecision. That’s a board. Somebody in that building has a ranked list and they are following it even when the famous name is sitting right there in the lobby.

And then they kept going. On October sixth, Seattle signed Dontae McMillan to the practice squad — a kid from Chief Sealth International High School in West Seattle who ran for 2,019 yards and 44 touchdowns as a senior, then put up 1,014 yards at 5.7 a carry at Eastern Michigan with All-MAC second-team honors and his school’s MVP award. Before the Chargers game they’d already signed Velus Jones Jr. off their own practice squad onto the fifty-three for depth. They’ve got Trevon Diggs on the roster, signed back in August, getting elevated on game days.

Four running back moves, one safety waived, one Pro Bowler rejected. In ten days. While sitting at 3-1 and favored over an undefeated team.

And if you want the proof this method works, he’s already on the field: Emanuel Wilson. Undrafted free agent. Seattle just named him a Top Newcomer on its own first-quarter honors list. He took over for Price in Arizona, put up a hundred and twenty scrimmage yards and two touchdowns in that stretch, and then caught a twenty-three-yard screen for a touchdown against the Chargers on top of twenty-one carries.

Three weeks ago nobody outside this fan base knew his name. He’s the lead running back for the defending Super Bowl champions.

That’s Schneider’s whole career in one sentence: he does not care where you came from.

Now let me give you the honest side of all this, because a highlight reel is not a football team.

Here’s the problem Robert Henry Jr. was actually signed to fix, and it isn’t glamorous.

Seattle’s running backs have fumbled three times in four games.

Three. You watched one in Arizona. You watched the next one hand Washington seven points in a game Seattle lost by two.

And then there’s the number I owe you about a man I’ve spent three weeks praising. Emanuel Wilson is averaging 3.6 yards a carry on fifty-three attempts, with a 60.6 PFF rushing grade.

Three point six is below the league average. Sixty-point-six is roughly a C-minus. Everything I said about his effort and his toughness is still true, and he is still not the answer for twelve more games. Both of those sentences can live in the same video.

So the running back room walking into Sunday is: Emanuel Wilson, George Holani — who was limited Thursday with a rib injury — Velus Jones Jr., and Robert Henry Jr., who has been in the building five days. Plus Dontae McMillan on the practice squad, and Jadarian Price on injured reserve.

That’s the context for the poach. Seattle didn’t steal a running back because they’re greedy. They stole one because they were down to the studs.

Now the genuinely good news, and this is the part that got buried this week — because it deserves better.

Zach Charbonnet is back on a practice field.

And I want you to sit with the full timeline, because it’s remarkable.

He tore his ACL in the divisional round, in January — against the San Francisco 49ers. Seattle won that game and lost him in it. He had surgery on February twentieth.

He was back practicing in under seven and a half months.

He started this season on the physically-unable-to-perform list, which meant he had to miss the first four games. And here’s the detail that is almost too poetic: this Sunday, against San Francisco, would have been the very first game he was eligible to play. The team on the other end of his injury, in his comeback game.

It’s not happening. Macdonald confirmed on Brock and Salk Monday that he will not play.

And honestly? Good. That’s the right call and I’d have made the same one.

He was limited at practice Thursday and Friday last week, and this week he’s taking team reps for the first time. Macdonald’s words: “I think we’re off to a great start” and “he looks pretty dang good” — specifically about the cutting ability and the change of direction, which is exactly the thing an ACL takes away from you and exactly the thing you need back before you play.

And the praise I liked most: Macdonald pointed out that plenty of guys don’t look like that coming off a knee injury, and Charbonnet got there in a short amount of time. That’s not luck. That’s seven months of work nobody watched.

One date: October twenty-second. That’s Seattle’s deadline to put him on the fifty-three man roster. If they don’t, he stays on PUP for the rest of the season. Everything we’re hearing says he’s trending the right way, but the clock is real and it’s loud.

Okay. Sunday. And I have to report something that went the wrong way, because I called it two days ago and I’m not going to quietly skip past it.

On Wednesday I told you Leonard Williams was a limited participant, that the sideline pass-rush drills suggested a short-term injury, and that if Thursday went backwards, you sit him.

Thursday went backwards. Leonard Williams did not practice at all.

Brady Henderson of ESPN says it’s unclear whether that’s an actual setback or the team being cautious. Either way, here’s who didn’t take a practice snap two days before a first-place game: a three-time Pro Bowler in his twelfth NFL season, Seattle’s own first-quarter Defensive MVP, with three sacks, five tackles for loss and six quarterback hits in four games — all team highs — and more sacks than any defensive tackle in football since the start of 2024.

The rest of Seattle’s Thursday: Chazz Surratt did not practice with a calf. Limited: Nehemiah Pritchett (ankle), George Holani (ribs), Julian Neal (hamstring), Ernest Jones the Fourth (rest, non-injury), plus Charbonnet and Irv Charles off their reserve lists.

And real good news: full participation for Ty Okada, Beau Stephens, Tyrice Knight and Patrick O’Connell — and Jaxon Smith-Njigba is back to full after Wednesday’s veteran rest day. Seattle just named JSN its first-quarter Offensive MVP: thirty-two catches, 481 yards, and a league-leading six touchdowns.

San Francisco’s Thursday went the other direction, and you need to know this. Christian McCaffrey, Trent Williams and Dre Greenlaw were all full participants after resting Wednesday — those three are playing. Nick Bosa still did not practice with a knee and a calf. Mike Evans got downgraded to did-not-practice with ribs. Juszczyk, Marques Sigle and James Thompson also sat out. Upton Stout and Mykel Williams were limited.

So Friday morning the picture is: their stars got healthier, your best defender got worse, and Seattle is still favored by three points over the only undefeated team in the conference. Total of forty-seven and a half.

Watch list, four things.

One: Leonard Williams’ Friday designation. Doubtful means out. Questionable after a Thursday DNP means game-time, and I’d hold him. Byron Murphy the Second and Jarran Reed can carry one afternoon — they cannot carry eight games, and this franchise’s season gets decided in January.

Two: does Robert Henry Jr. touch the football? Five days in the building, third in America in yards per carry, zero NFL snaps. If Brian Fleury gives him eight carries in a first-place game, that tells you everything about how the staff graded that tape.

Three: Nick Bosa on the inactive list ninety minutes before kickoff. Two straight DNPs with two listed injuries is not the profile of a man who plays.

Four: the penalty count. Fourteen for a franchise-record hundred and fifty yards against the Chargers, seven personal fouls. Macdonald: “To have seven personal foul penalties is unacceptable on any level, however you slice it. So that’s not us, and that’s not going to be how we play football around here.” Hand a 4-0 team a hundred and fifty free yards and they will bury you with them.

Let me close.

Two weeks ago the Washington Commanders beat the Seattle Seahawks 33-31 and ended the longest winning streak in the history of this franchise. Twelve games, gone, in a stadium where Marcus Mariota ran for a first down on third-and-seven to finish it.

And on Tuesday, John Schneider reached into that same organization’s practice squad and took their running back — a two-time Mississippi Mr. Football, the first man ever to win the Walter Jones Trophy, the national junior college offensive player of the year, first-team all-conference, third in the United States of America in yards per carry — and put him straight onto the active roster of the defending Super Bowl champions.

Washington got nothing. Not a pick, not a dollar, not a say.

He turned down seven thousand four hundred career rushing yards on Tuesday morning and signed a juco kid with 6.9 a carry on Tuesday afternoon. In ten days he’s made four moves at one position, waived a starting safety to do it, and signed a kid from Chief Sealth High School who ran for two thousand yards in a West Seattle uniform.

That’s not a front office panicking. That’s a front office with a list.

John Schneider has done it again. He just did it quietly, for free, out of the back pocket of the team that beat us.
