Part two. Now here’s what Schneider actually did since Monday morning — and this is why the title works.

Move one: Anthony Bradford was designated to return from injured reserve.

He went on IR September twenty-third with knee and hip injuries and has missed five games. On Monday he returned to practice, which opens his twenty-one day window.

Keep the mechanic straight, because you’ll see it both ways this week: a return designation is not an activation. It lets him practice without taking a roster spot. Seattle still has to formally add him to the fifty-three before he can play.

And then Macdonald did something he almost never does. He said: “I know normally I’m pretty coy on this one, but it’s pretty straightforward. If he has a good week, he’s going to play a significant amount, if not the whole game.”

And on what Seattle gets: “He’s a physical presence for us at right guard, both in run and pass.”

Move two and three: Seattle signed fullback Jackson Acker and tight end Josiah Deguara to the practice squad.

Deguara’s a real veteran — a third-round pick out of Cincinnati, now twenty-nine, 78 career games with 12 starts, 53 catches for 471 yards and three touchdowns. He was released by Philadelphia after Week 5 and had appeared in one game for them this year. He is not a receiving weapon. He’s a hybrid blocker who can line up in the backfield.

And Macdonald told you exactly what the plan is. Asked whether this was about adding a different type of fullback — Seattle lost Robbie Ouzts earlier in the year and Brady Russell has been filling in — he said: “It gives us some more opportunity to be in two-back sets.”

Move four: the releases that paid for them — McMillan and Meiga, as we covered.

And the fifth thing, which isn’t a move yet but is the biggest one: Zach Charbonnet’s deadline is tomorrow. October twenty-second.

His twenty-one-day practice window opened October first. Tomorrow it closes, and there are exactly two outcomes: on the fifty-three-man roster, or back on PUP for the rest of the season. No extension. No third option.

And on Monday, Macdonald finally moved off the vague answers: “Zach is going to have a full week’s worth of reps, so that’s exciting. And there’s a possibility that you could see him on Sunday in some capacity.”

He’s also called a Week 7 debut “a realistic target.” And Gregg Bell of Seattle Sports has reported that when Charbonnet does play, he’d likely take a large share of the carries.

Now put all five of those together and look at what they spell.

A fullback. A blocking tight end. A physical right guard. A running back. And releases of a third-string back and a receiver to pay for it.

Every single move Seattle made this week was about the run game and heavy personnel. That is not a scattershot week of paperwork. That’s a front office with one idea, executing it in five transactions.

And the idea makes sense, because here’s the problem it’s aimed at. Seattle’s rushing offense has fallen from 123.3 yards a game last season to 107.2. Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks in Denver and went 6-of-19 for 47 yards under pressure. He’s taken fourteen sacks this year — five against Denver, five the week before against San Francisco. And in Bradford’s absence, Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens both allowed multiple pressures on Thursday.

One problem, stated two ways. And Schneider just spent a week buying the answer instead of a headline.

Let me teach you the roster mechanic, because it decides who actually plays Sunday.

Acker and Deguara are on the practice squad, not the fifty-three. Practice squad players cannot simply suit up. Seattle has two ways to get them into a game:

One — sign them to the active roster, which costs a fifty-three-man spot and makes them permanent.

Two — elevate them, which is temporary and free of a roster spot. But the rules are strict: two elevations per game, maximum, and three per player per season. After the game, an elevated player reverts to the practice squad without going through waivers.

So count the competition for those two Sunday spots: Acker, Deguara, Ciarlo if the linebackers are still hurt, Irv Charles for special teams. Two tickets, four plausible names. That’s the decision Seattle makes Saturday, and it’ll tell you exactly what Macdonald thinks he needs against Kenneth Walker.

And let me show you why a fullback signing is secretly a defensive move, because this is the part that’ll make you sound smart on Sunday.

Kenneth Walker needs carries to reach a hundred yards. And a running back gets carries when his team is ahead and wants to shorten the game. His 177-yard night against Las Vegas came on 22 carries in a win.

So flip it. If Seattle is leading in the fourth quarter, Kansas City has to throw, and Walker finishes with sixteen touches and seventy yards. If Seattle is trailing, Kansas City hands it to him until the clock dies.

Now: what does a fullback, a blocking tight end and a healthy right guard actually produce? Longer drives. More third downs converted on the ground. More minutes where Patrick Mahomes and Kenneth Walker are standing on the sideline in headsets.

Every possession Seattle keeps is a possession Kansas City doesn’t get. And every carry Walker doesn’t get is a carry that can’t go for eight yards.

That’s what complementary football actually means, and it’s not a slogan — it’s arithmetic. Seattle’s offensive line is part of Seattle’s run defense this week. A 35-game streak isn’t defended only by tackling. It’s defended by score and clock.

And there’s a second layer, which is the personnel chess match.

Heavy personnel — two backs, two tight ends — forces the defense to put bigger bodies on the field. Kansas City has to answer with linebackers instead of nickel corners. And the moment they do, your pass game gets easier, because now Jaxon Smith-Njigba is running routes against a defense built to stop a fullback.

That’s the whole purpose of what Schneider bought this week: not to run it forty times, but to make Kansas City prepare for it, and then throw over the top of their answer.

Now, one more thing about Robert Henry Jr., because his situation just changed and nobody’s mentioned it.

With McMillan released and Charbonnet coming back, look at Seattle’s backfield: Emanuel Wilson, George Holani, Charbonnet, Velus Jones Jr., and Henry — with Price returning in two weeks. That’s crowded.

He hasn’t carried the ball in an NFL game yet. But the man ahead of him just got activated off a torn ACL and may only play “in some capacity.” Somebody is getting carries Sunday that nobody has planned for.

Denver held JSN to two catches for eleven yards in the first half by taking exactly that away.
