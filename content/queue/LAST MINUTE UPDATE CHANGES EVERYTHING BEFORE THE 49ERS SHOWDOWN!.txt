A reporter told Mike Macdonald on Friday that one of his rookies had said out loud that he didn’t expect to play at all this season.

And Macdonald’s answer was the best thing he said all week:

“I haven’t heard him say that, but we want everybody getting ready all the time. And so it’s probably a great lesson learned for him — you need to stay ready to go, and you’re competing to play and to start. So let’s go. No rest for the weary. Let’s rock and roll. We need you.”

We need you.

A fifth-round rookie who figured he’d spend the year holding a clipboard, and his head coach’s response is: no rest for the weary, we need you.

That rookie plays right guard today, against the only undefeated team in the National Football Conference.

And five days before that, four veterans on this defense walked into a room and closed the door on their own coaches.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. It’s game day. Today: the players-only meeting nobody called for, the penalty problem and why Macdonald is refusing to panic about it, an officiating detail that could decide this afternoon, and the whole Friday press conference. Let’s go.

Monday, October fifth. Players only.

Jarran Reed. Leonard Williams. Julian Love. Ernest Jones the Fourth.

Those four men got Seattle’s defense into a room with no coaches in it. And here’s the part that makes this remarkable, Seahawks Nation, the part I want you to sit with:

They had just won the football game.

You call a players-only meeting after a loss. That’s when they happen. Seattle beat the Chargers 30-23 and then its four most respected defenders called one anyway.

Reed’s description: “We just had a real, honest meeting with each other about what we expect and what we want to go through.”

And the subject: “We hit it head on, because that was way too many penalties for us as a team, especially the personal foul penalties.”

So let me show you the numbers they were looking at, because they’re worse than you remember.

Fourteen accepted penalties for 150 yards. A franchise single-game record for penalty yardage. And the thirty combined penalties in that football game tied for the second-most in the history of this franchise — out of 797 games Seattle has ever played.

But the raw number isn’t the problem. This is the problem:

Seattle led that game 27 to 6. And the Chargers cut it to 30-23, partly on four second-half personal fouls.

Four fifteen-yard gifts, on two Chargers touchdown drives. Roughing the passer on Derick Hall. Roughing the passer on DeMarcus Lawrence. Unnecessary roughness on Devon Witherspoon. A facemask on Nick Emmanwori.

Plus Emmanwori’s dead-ball head butt at the end of the first half, which set up a Chargers field goal attempt. And two roughing-the-passer calls on DeMarcus Lawrence that erased his own sacks — he beat the man in front of him twice and got nothing for it both times.

And across four games: thirty-five accepted penalties, tied for seventh-most in the league. Five unnecessary-roughness penalties — the most in the entire National Football League. Two roughing-the-passer calls, more than all but three teams.

Now here’s why I respect Jarran Reed so much, because the framework he gave is the most mature thing a player has said all season:

“Some of the stuff is just the cost of doing business. But the ones that can be avoided — we need to avoid those penalties.”

That’s it. That’s the distinction. If you play defense the way this team plays defense, some flags are the receipt. You are going to hit people hard and occasionally the whistle goes. Fine. But a head butt after the play is over isn’t physicality. That’s temper.

And he put it in context perfectly: “It wasn’t a panic moment, but a moment of realization.”

Then he got specific about where it hurts most: “We can’t hurt ourselves, especially in the third-and-long distance where we can get off the field.”

Think about that. Third-and-twelve is the moment this defense has spent two years winning. You force a punt, your offense gets the ball. And a fifteen-yard penalty on third-and-twelve doesn’t just erase the stop — it hands them a first down and keeps the drive alive. One moment of frustration undoes three plays of brilliant football.

He also named the dead-ball stuff plainly: “anytime the player is running out of bounds, or the quarterback hands the ball off.” And then the honest ending: “The guys play hard and we like to be a physical group — but once you get to the situation, if you think it’s going to be a certain type of flag situation, we’ve got to be smart.”

And Macdonald’s answer about why he lets his players run that meeting instead of running it himself tells you everything about this building:

“It wasn’t a long meeting. The guys just mentioned they wanted to do it, and let’s rock and roll. We have a lot of ownership from our guys. They take a lot of pride in our way of life here, how we play football — a really great group that we trust, and they keep delivering.”

“We have a lot of ownership from our guys.” That’s the whole culture in seven words.

Now here’s the part that surprised me, and it’s a genuine tension I want to lay out honestly.

Mike Macdonald is not banging the table about penalties.

His actual public position: “We’re going to have penalties.” And: “I don’t think every team that led the league in penalties won the most games every year.” And: “We’re going to be somewhere in the middle, and what matters is us playing great football.” And the kicker: “The penalty stuff will take care of itself.”

So the players held an emergency meeting about it and the head coach is telling everyone to breathe.

Which one is right?

Both. And here’s why, because I think this is the smartest thing happening inside this team right now.

Macdonald is protecting aggression. The instant a defense starts playing scared of flags, it stops finishing tackles. It stops closing on the quarterback. It arrives a half-second late because it’s thinking instead of hunting. The thing that makes the Dark Side great is that nobody hesitates — and a head coach who stands up and screams about penalties for a week creates exactly that hesitation.

He cannot be the man who makes his players flinch.

So the players handled the part the coach isn’t allowed to say. That is precisely what a players-only meeting is for. The leadership group can demand discipline from the inside without the head coach ever putting fear into the room from the outside.

That’s not a contradiction. That’s a well-run football team dividing a job between two voices.

And the league context backs Macdonald up in a way almost nobody is reporting.

There were 963 accepted penalties across the National Football League through four weeks — the most for that span since 1978.

Nineteen seventy-eight. And 271 in a single week, which is the second-most for any week since 1978.

So this is not a Seattle problem. The entire sport is being flagged at a forty-eight-year high. Seattle’s seventh-place ranking in a season like this one means something different than it would in a normal year.

And now the detail I found that I haven’t heard anybody mention, and it might matter this afternoon.

The crew that worked the Chargers game was Scott Novak’s. That crew has thrown 102 flags in four games — twenty-two more than any other crew in football — for 860 yards, which is 220 more than any other crew in football.

Today’s crew is Land Clark’s. They’ve thrown 69 flags this season.

A hundred and two versus sixty-nine. That is a thirty-three flag gap between the crew that buried Seattle last Sunday and the crew calling this game.

And Macdonald is doing the homework on it — his line was “understanding how they’re calling certain things.”

Now, don’t take that as a promise. A crew that’s been letting things go can absolutely swallow the whistle for a quarter and then throw five in a row. But if you were looking for a reason to believe last week’s flag storm was partly circumstance and not purely a Seattle character flaw, that’s a real one.

Alright. Now let me get back to that opening thesis, because the rest of Friday was a parade of men who stayed ready.

Beau Stephens. A fifth-round rookie and a first-team All-American at Iowa. He played twenty-seven snaps in his NFL debut in Arizona. And he’s in the right guard rotation today because Anthony Bradford — who started every single game at right guard last season — went on injured reserve with knee and hip injuries.

Macdonald’s full answer, and I love it: “We want everybody getting ready all the time... it’s probably a great lesson learned for him — you need to stay ready to go, and you’re competing to play and to start. So let’s go. No rest for the weary. Let’s rock and roll. We need you.” And then: “He’s doing a good job though. He’s playing good football.”

The other half of that rotation is Christian Haynes, a 2024 third-round pick who made his first career start in Arizona — forty-three snaps, a 58.6 overall PFF grade and a 76.6 pass-blocking grade, which against this 49ers front is the number that matters. Brian Fleury said the two will keep rotating based on “the state of the game.” Macdonald: “With the way those guys played, we have great competition right there.”

And real good news on the man they’re replacing: Macdonald on Anthony Bradford — “He’s doing great. He’s working really hard. He should be ready soon.”

Rodney Thomas the Second. Started the year as a practice squad call-up and has held down a safety job for a month. Macdonald: “Rodney’s done a tremendous job filling in for Ty... coming in and filling in on short notice and playing high level football.”

And Ty Okada plays today. Four weeks out with the hamstring, and he’s active. Macdonald: “Ty’s going to play. He’s ready to go.” And listen to what he actually praised: “He’s making plays in the back end. He’s getting us lined up. He’s getting us in and out of plays. He’s not rusty at all on the mental side, and seems like he’s moving around great.”

Will he use both Okada and Thomas? “We could. We could.”

Robert Henry Jr., five days into being a Seahawk: “He’s done a nice job. He’s an impressive guy. I’m glad we have him.” And the scouting: “He’s really quick. He’s been good in the pass protection stuff with the limited amount of time he’s been here. Caught the ball out of the backfield. Seems really fast. I haven’t seen him in a game yet, but seems fast to me.”

And the single best observation of the whole press conference came from a reporter, not the coach: Robert Henry has been practicing every single day since the start of training camp on Washington’s practice squad — so he is already in football shape. No conditioning ramp. Macdonald agreed; the only question is how much of the game plan he’s absorbed.

That’s the hidden value of an October practice-squad poach that nobody explains. You’re not signing a man off his couch. You’re signing a man who has taken a thousand full-speed reps that nobody in the world watched.

Now the most revealing answer of the week, and it’s an admission.

Asked about Okada being a coach on the field, Macdonald gave up something real: “We basically took off every one rep throughout the offseason, through training camp, through the spring. So there’s a chemistry there with our guys that we’re trying to kind of rebuild.”

Read that again. The back end of this defense has barely played together — and he said the same thing happened a little last year.

Then the balanced version, which is why I trust this guy: “the moving pieces we’ve had in our secondary — I wouldn’t say it’s adversity, but it’s something that when you work through it provides depth, which is great. But there’s something to be said for having consistency in who’s back there, so you can build the chemistry of being ahead of plays.”

Being ahead of plays. That’s the phrase of the week and it’s the whole secret of great secondary play. A great secondary isn’t fast. A great secondary is early. It’s already standing where the ball is going, because four men have seen that formation together a thousand times and they all know what’s coming.

And “early” is built out of reps together — which is exactly what Seattle hasn’t had, between Love’s calf, Okada’s hamstring, Emmanwori’s hamstring, Finley’s start, Rodney Thomas filling in, and a safety getting waived and re-signed inside twenty-four hours.

So Okada coming back isn’t a depth note. It’s a chemistry deposit. And Macdonald closed it generously: “everybody that’s played safety for us this year has done a great job.”

Two more things from Friday.

Zach Charbonnet, on how the week of team reps went: “He’s done great, and yes — we’re on schedule here.” Then the honest caveat: “Next week will be a little tricky with the speed and tempo of practice, but I think we’re in a great spot going into next week.”

Translate that: this week was team reps at controlled speed. Next week is full tempo — and that’s the real test for a man ten months off a torn ACL. October twenty-second is the activation deadline. It’s going to be close and it’s going to come down to how he moves at full speed on Wednesday.

And then my favorite exchange of the entire session. A reporter asked whether having Brian Fleury — hired out of San Francisco in February — gave Macdonald a better read on Shanahan’s offense.

He mostly declined the premise. They talk about how Seattle is going to do it, not how San Francisco does it. He mentioned learning “targeting in the run game,” and noted that across his coordinators the common denominator has been his own offensive line coach.

And then he said this: “This is a football guy that likes football. Like, how do you not like learning more football?”

That’s the same man who told us he spent twenty-four months chasing an edge in run blocking by learning from his own assistants. He doesn’t hoard knowledge and he doesn’t pretend to have it. He collects it, out loud, from whoever walks in the building.

Okay. Today.

Leonard Williams has no injury designation. Macdonald on Friday: “Leonard was held out yesterday. He’s got no designation. He’s good to go.” And he downplayed it hard: “I don’t want to take anything away from Leo — it’s not a major... we had a pretty good feel throughout the week that he was going to be good. We just wanted to do right by him and make sure he was good to go.”

So Thursday’s DNP was never a setback. They knew all week.

Seattle’s out: Julian Neal with a hamstring, Chazz Surratt with a calf. Questionable: George Holani with ribs, Irv Charles with a knee — Macdonald confirmed that one himself to open the session.

San Francisco’s out: Nick Bosa, Kyle Juszczyk, Marques Sigle, James Thompson. Mike Evans is not on the report and plays. Mykel Williams is being activated off PUP.

And the context: San Francisco hasn’t allowed a sack in four games, the first team to open that way in twenty-one years. Seattle is favored by three over the only undefeated team in the conference. And the last time these two met in this building, Seattle won 41-6 in the divisional round.

Watch list, four things.

One: the penalty count. Land Clark’s crew has thrown thirty-three fewer flags than last week’s crew. If Seattle still ends up in double digits, that’s on Seattle.

Two: third-and-long discipline specifically. That’s the exact situation Jarran Reed named in that meeting. Watch what happens the first time this defense forces third-and-twelve.

Three: Robert Henry Jr.’s first touch. Already in football shape, zero NFL carries.

Four: Okada and Rodney Thomas on the field together. Macdonald said “we could.” If you see both, Seattle is playing the chemistry card early.

Let me close.

Everything about this week came down to one idea, and it showed up in three different places.

Four veterans called a meeting after a win, because the standard isn’t the scoreboard. A rookie who didn’t expect to play all year is starting at right guard against the best team in football. And a running back nobody drafted has been taking full-speed practice reps since July on a team that cut him, which is the only reason he can help somebody today.

Stay ready. That’s it. That’s the whole organization in two words, and the head coach said it out loud: no rest for the weary, let’s rock and roll, we need you.

Four-and-oh San Francisco is in our building. Leonard’s playing. Bosa isn’t. Okada’s back.
