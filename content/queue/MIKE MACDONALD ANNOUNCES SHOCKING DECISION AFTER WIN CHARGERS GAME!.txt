Let me start with the quote, because I want to give the man credit for saying something risky.

The Monday after the Washington loss — streak over at twelve, three turnovers, forty-four rushing yards, the whole fan base on fire — Mike Macdonald stood at a podium and said this:

"You can feel the resolve. You can feel the eagerness to get back at it on Wednesday."

Now, think about how that sentence usually ages. Every coach in America says a version of it after a loss. It's the most forgettable thing in sports — "we'll bounce back, I love this group, we'll get to work." Nine times out of ten it means nothing and nobody remembers it by Thursday.

Six days later his team ran the ball thirty-three times for a hundred and thirty-eight yards, forced four turnovers, sacked the quarterback four times, and won a football game.

He wasn't doing public relations. He was reporting what he actually saw in the building on a Monday morning. And that's the difference between a coach whose words are noise and a coach whose words are information.

Which brings me to the concept at the center of all of this, and I want to take my time with it because it's the single most useful idea for understanding this team.

After the loss, Macdonald gave us his definition of how football games are won. His exact words: "In the NFL, what wins games is great situational football, and taking care of the rock, and playing complementary football, and stacking plays. We didn't do those things today, and they did."

Complementary football. That's the phrase.

Here's what it actually means, because it gets thrown around and rarely explained. Complementary football is when your three units hand each other good situations instead of bad ones. The defense gets a takeaway and gives the offense a short field. The offense finishes a drive and lets the defense play with a lead. Special teams flips the field so the defense starts the other team at their own fifteen instead of their own forty.

It is the opposite of what happened in Washington. There, the offense gained four hundred and thirty-seven yards and simultaneously handed the defense three sudden-change situations — three times where the defense sprinted onto the field with the other team already in scoring range. Washington turned those into twenty-one points, something they hadn't done in a single game since 2005.

Four hundred and thirty-seven yards. And the units were working against each other.

Now look at Sunday.

The game's very first play from scrimmage: Justin Herbert throws it and Seattle linebacker Drake Thomas intercepts it. Perfect.

And then Seattle's very first offensive play: Darnold throws one right back.

Two plays, two interceptions. The Chargers cashed it into a Cameron Dicker field goal from forty yards and led 3-0.

So the day started exactly the way last week went. The offense undid the defense's work before anybody had sat down.

And then — this is the part I want you to see — special teams fixed it.

Los Angeles muffed a punt return. Seattle recovered the football. And on the very next snap, Darnold found Emanuel Wilson for a twenty-three yard touchdown.

That right there is complementary football in about forty seconds of real time. Special teams created the takeaway, the offense needed exactly one play to cash it, and the scoreboard flipped from 3-0 down to 7-3 up.

You want to know why Seattle won with a hundred and sixty-eight passing yards? That's why. The units stopped working against each other.

Personal fouls are not timing. They're composure. A personal foul is a choice a grown man makes after the whistle. You don't commit seven of those because the play clock ran down. You commit seven of those when a team loses its temper.
