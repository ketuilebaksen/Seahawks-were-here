“I’m assuming we’re probably going to have to make a decision on who we’re going to get ready.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s Mike Macdonald — forty minutes after a seventeen to three win — telling you he does not know who his quarterback is.

And then he added this: “Drew’s situation, we’ll have to evaluate that as well. So it seems like it’s kind of up in the air right now with Drew.”

Up in the air.

Both of them. Sam Darnold on a cracked rib and a quad. Drew Lock, who just won this football game, being evaluated for an oblique.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four. Las Vegas is Sunday. And this title is right about one thing — Mike Macdonald absolutely sent a message about Sam Darnold in this press conference. It just isn’t the message you’d expect after a backup quarterback wins by fourteen. Let’s get into it.

First, the message itself — because it’s four words and it changes how you read this whole week.

Asked about Drew Lock playing through the elbow, Macdonald praised him — “he’s a beast” — and then, unprompted, said this:

“Really, both quarterbacks that have played — I mean, talk about, I don’t know how you get any tougher. BOTH guys tough as nails.”

“Both guys.”

Twelves, that is the message, and I want you to understand what just happened there.

A reporter asked exclusively about Drew Lock. Nobody mentioned Sam Darnold. Nobody brought him up.

And Mike Macdonald pulled him into the answer anyway.

This is the third press conference in a row where he has volunteered something flattering about Sam Darnold without being asked to. Last week it was the cracked rib revelation and “he’s tough as nails.” Then it was “he’s kind of like a coach on the field right now... it’s been fun.” And now, in a press conference celebrating his replacement, he said “both guys.”

That is not an accident. Nothing a head coach says in the third sentence of an answer is an accident.

Here’s what he’s doing: he is refusing to let a quarterback competition narrative get started in his own building. Drew Lock won. Drew Lock was great. And in the same breath, the starter who is hurt is “tough as nails” and belongs in the same category.

“I don’t know how you get any tougher” — about both of them, together, in one sentence.

And he’s got the evidence. Darnold played four quarters on a cracked rib without telling his head coach. Lock played this game on a bad elbow and came off the field being checked for an oblique. Macdonald’s dry line about it: “apparently something with the midsection of our quarterbacks is an issue right now.”

That joke got a laugh. But what it actually tells you is that Seattle’s quarterback room is now two men hurt in the same place — and their coach refuses to rank them.

Now, the actual decision — and I want to lay out the real situation, because it’s more complicated than people realize.

Here’s the full answer on Sam Darnold’s chances this week:

“I don’t — I think we’ll probably re-evaluate earlier in the week. But you know, I’m assuming we’re probably going to have to make a decision on who we’re going to get ready and how we’re going to do that. Drew’s situation, we’ll have to evaluate that as well. So it seems like it’s kind of up in the air right now with Drew.”

Pay attention to the exact words: “a decision on who we’re going to get READY.”

Not “who starts.” Who they get ready.

And twelves, that’s the actual problem, and it comes straight out of what Drew Lock told us about his own week.

Lock revealed that he threw a football on exactly ONE day this week. Thursday. That’s it. No throwing Wednesday, no throwing Friday, no throwing Saturday — and he didn’t even throw in pregame until he had the pads and helmet on. They spent all of Saturday building that plan.

So here is Seattle’s dilemma going into Las Vegas: you cannot run that plan for two quarterbacks at once.

A throw budget that tight means somebody has to get the walkthrough reps, the tape time, the install, the week. You have to pick. And if you pick wrong — if you build the week around Darnold and his rib doesn’t clear Friday, or you build it around Lock and the oblique tightens up — you walk into Las Vegas with a quarterback who hasn’t been prepared.

That’s why “who we’re going to get ready” is the exact right phrase. The decision isn’t who plays. It’s who gets the week.

And Macdonald’s instinct on this is already on the record, from last week: he named Drew Lock as the starter on Wednesday instead of keeping it a mystery. He chose certainty for his locker room over a competitive edge. Expect him to do it again. He’ll tell us early.

And now the third name, because people keep forgetting he exists.

“And Jalen Milroe too — like, operating our offense when he took the snap that he took, and being ready to play in case Drew couldn’t go or couldn’t finish. So really a great team effort.”

Jalen Milroe took a live snap in this football game.

Twelves, that matters more than it sounds, and the reason comes from Drew Lock’s own mouth last week: “reps for quarterbacks is better than any tape they can watch, better than any footwork drills they can go do on the side.”

Jalen Milroe just banked his first one. He has still never thrown a pass in an NFL game — but he has now operated this offense, in a real game, in a division win. And Macdonald specifically credited him for being ready “in case Drew couldn’t go or couldn’t finish” — which, given the oblique, was a live possibility.

If both of the men in front of him are hurt going into Las Vegas, that snap stops being a footnote very quickly.

Now let me tell you what Mike Macdonald actually led with — because it wasn’t the quarterbacks.

“Proud of our team. A lot of noise this week with injuries and other things going on. So just the poise that we showed — the twelve was one. We said this game was like the power of our seventy, with the depth we were going to lean on, with the bottom of our roster. And guys did a great job of stepping in. Not flinching.”

“The power of our seventy.”

That’s fifty-three on the active roster, sixteen on the practice squad, plus the international pathway player. And Macdonald has been pounding that number all season — “I’m on the seventy” — because the point is that nobody on it is decoration.

And Sunday was the day he collected. Six starters out. He walked into a division game and told his team that the bottom of the roster was going to decide it.

He named them: “Drew Lock, AB coming back, Dante Fowler, eager to see what Riley did — Riley had more of an opportunity, that was cool. Mike Morris.”

Anthony Bradford. Back at right guard. The man I spent an entire video telling you not to trade.

And Drew Lock used the exact same phrase in his own press conference, unprompted: “Mike preaches it. The ready squad — it’s seventy guys ready to roll at any time.”

When the coach’s phrase comes out of a player’s mouth with nobody feeding it to him, the message has landed. That’s culture actually working instead of being talked about.

Now the stat Macdonald led with, and it’s the most important one of the day.

“Got a couple red zone stops, which is a first for us. And then a couple red zone scores. So that was great.”

“Which is a FIRST for us.”

Twelves, stop and appreciate the timeline here.

Six days earlier, Julian Love stood at a podium and said — his word — that Seattle’s red zone efficiency was “terrible right now.” Macdonald said the same thing himself: “defensively we get to the red zone and we don’t execute as well.”

They named the problem on Wednesday. They fixed it on Sunday. First red zone stops of the entire season — plus red zone touchdowns.

That’s not luck. That’s a team that can identify a flaw in public and correct it inside a week.

And then: “to be able to close the game out in a tough situation there at the goal line, and then our offense putting a four-minute drive together. Some really good situational football.”

A goal line stand and a four-minute drive. Those are the two hardest situations in football and Seattle won both of them on the same afternoon.

Now the leadership story of the day, and honestly it’s the best thing in this press conference after the quarterback stuff.

A reporter reminded Macdonald that DeMarcus Lawrence had said during the week that he felt like he was part of the red zone problem — specifically his C gap — and then he made a tackle for loss at the end of this game.

“Yeah, I didn’t realize that Daw had said that until Dave filled me in later on in the week. But he’s just like a great example of the guys on our team. And for him to be a leader and to show the example of — look, where we fall short doesn’t fall on Daw’s shoulders. Everybody knows that. But for someone of that kind of pedigree to take that type of responsibility and accountability is pretty special. So he’s awesome. Played great today, and we definitely follow his lead.”

Follow the chain, twelves.

Seattle has a red zone problem. A thirteen-year veteran with over sixty career sacks publicly says it’s partly his fault — and names his own gap. His head coach says plainly it wasn’t on him and everybody knows that.

And then Seattle gets its first red zone stops of the year, and DeMarcus Lawrence makes a tackle for loss at the end of the game.

“We definitely follow his lead.”

And remember Julian Love’s line: “a lesser team would be pointing fingers, turning on each other.” This is the proof. Their most decorated defender pointed it at himself.

Now the coaching decision that actually won the game, and nobody’s talking about it.

Asked about Nick Emmanwori covering Trey McBride on fourth down:

“Yeah, it was a big-time play. We put him on McBride and felt confidently he could cover him. And he did. So that was pretty cool.”

And then a reporter noted Emmanwori was on McBride on a lot of third downs too, when the ball didn’t go that way.

And here’s the reveal: “Yeah, we didn’t rush Nick as much this week. Different reasons for why we did that, but it just shows you — hey, wherever you put your focus.”

“We didn’t rush Nick as much this week.”

Twelves, that’s a real tactical change and I want to explain it.

Nick Emmanwori has been used as a blitzer — a six-foot-three, 227-pound safety coming off the edge. And this week Macdonald took that away and used him as a cover man on Arizona’s single best offensive player instead.

Trey McBride is one of the two or three best tight ends in football. And Seattle’s answer was: put the rookie safety on him and erase him. Third down after third down, the ball didn’t go there. And on fourth down, Emmanwori won the play.

That’s a defensive mind identifying the one man who can beat him and assigning somebody to delete him.

And the praise was about process, not talent: “he really had a good week of focusing in on details, and playing his style of ball but also being detail oriented. So I’m glad he had the production to back that up.”

Now the running backs, and this is the development we’ve waited eight months for.

“Yeah, it’s great. Really happy for Zach. Happy for JD. Definitely felt Zach in his power, and the ability to break tackles and own the sideline there — even though we got the penalty to move us back. And I think they should continue to get better with more reps and more experience.”

“Definitely felt Zach in his power.”

Remember where we were eight days earlier. Macdonald told us Zach Charbonnet “hasn’t had a live rep since the injury” — not one since he tore his ACL in January. He played Monday night for the first time in eight months and got held under thirty yards. A national analyst watched that tape and his word was “rust.”

And on Sunday he closed out a division game with his power.

“Own the sideline” means a back lowering his shoulder and refusing to go down in bounds. That’s the Zach Charbonnet we remember, and it took one week.

And on Jadarian Price, Macdonald gave the single best football explanation of the press conference.

“Yeah, I felt him out there. I thought he was DECISIVE with his runs. Sometimes he’s the primary in some of those pass concepts, sometimes he’s kind of at the end of the progression — and when the ball finds him, if you can make a guy miss, that’s a great example. It doesn’t have to be some extraordinary play to create explosives. Let’s just be efficient with our decision-making and get it in our players’ hands and let them do the rest.”

“It doesn’t have to be some extraordinary play to create explosives.”

Twelves, that’s the thesis of this entire offense.

Remember what Sam Darnold said: “you’re not ever really seeking out explosive plays.” Macdonald just said it from the coaching chair. The explosive play isn’t a sixty-yard bomb. It’s a five-yard checkdown to a running back who makes one man miss.

You don’t hunt explosives. You create them by being efficient and letting good players be good.

And remember — the analyst’s word for Price was “hesitant.” Macdonald’s word was “decisive.” One week apart. And the reason: “now that he’s stacking weeks and he’s getting healthier.”

Now the offensive line, and this is quietly remarkable.

“I thought — we still ended up in some longer situations, where those are inevitable to some degree, but we want to minimize those. But I thought the guys did a good job. Be fun to watch the tape. Always room for improvement, but with the movement that we’ve had across our line in the last two weeks, to kind of come together and especially finish the game the way they did was really cool.”

“With the movement that we’ve had across our line in the last two weeks.”

Count it. Two weeks ago Seattle trades for Quenton Nelson and slides Grey Zabel to right guard. Nelson needs a knee procedure. Zabel goes back to left guard and Anthony Bradford comes back to right guard. No Charles Cross at left tackle through all of it.

Three interior configurations in fourteen days, without the starting left tackle.

And zero sacks allowed.

Which connects to the pressure question — Arizona pressured early and finished with no sacks. Why?

“Yeah, kind of both. A lot of times teams give you a look you might not have seen, but our rules should be able to handle those things. And sometimes there will be free runners and we have to get rid of the ball. Speaks to our team just making the adjustments. But we want to be on our game to START games, for sure. So we’ll look at that and try to come up with a solution.”

“Our rules should be able to handle those things.” That’s pass protection honestly described. You don’t have a call for every blitz. You have rules, and the rules have to hold. They bent early and held all afternoon.

But notice he still wouldn’t let the slow starts go. Even in a win by fourteen, that’s still on his list.

Now the defense, and this answer is about character.

“We were just talking to Leo about it. Just the composure of our team, the togetherness. There’s several things that didn’t go our way today — I felt like we got the ball out several times. There’s some penalties against us to put us in not-great situations. But just no flinch from the fellas. I thought our coaches did a great job of finding solutions and staying calm in the moment, even though it gets kind of crazy sometimes. But you could feel it from our group that they were determined to get that stop at the end of the game, and we were confident we could pull it off.”

“No flinch from the fellas.”

And he was discussing it with Leonard Williams — the man who just missed his first game since Week 4 of 2024. He was there. He just couldn’t play.

Now AJ Barner, and this answer contains the sentence that kills a national narrative.

“Yeah, proud of AJ. We love him and we trust him and we understand what type of player he is. So we can kind of feel the frustration with him, of what he expects from himself. But just no flinch, keep attacking it. He’s a leader on our offense and he provides so much great energy.”

And then the tactical part: “to get into some of those short-yardage situations where we can run the sneak is kind of galvanizing, energizes the group — and then to put the ball away in the red zone WHEN THEY TAKE JAX AWAY, to have another option down there, is really helpful.”

“When they take Jax away.”

Twelves, a national analyst called this offense a “one-man band” last week. His exact game plan against Seattle was: “if we can limit Jaxon Smith-Njigba to under two hundred yards, we’ll have a chance.”

Arizona took Jax away in the red zone. Exactly as predicted.

And AJ Barner caught two touchdowns. While playing through a hand injury.

Cooper Kupp called it in advance: “you’ve got to compete without the ball as well. And when the ball does come your way, you’ve got to make a play.”

They took Jax away. Somebody else beat them. That’s the answer to “one-man band,” delivered in one afternoon.

Now the grade — and this is the line that ties the whole season together.

Asked how this game graded for playing Seahawks football:

“Yeah, I thought we took a step today. A lot of positive things. Being able to close games out like that was great. I thought we had some great situational stuff at the end of the half, where you’re continually putting pressure on our opponent. And there’s some things we fell short at, that of course we’re going to go back and attack. But really, just like I told the guys — I don’t care about the score. I just want us to go let it loose and go be us and feel like us. And a lot of times we felt that way.”

“I don’t care about the score. I just want us to feel like us.”

Twelves, that is the direct answer to Julian Love’s confession six days earlier.

Love said: “we’re still in identity-finding mode... right now it does not feel how it’s supposed to look.”

And Sunday his head coach said: “a lot of times we felt that way.”

Not finished. Not fixed. But present. And he didn’t claim more than that, which is why I believe it.

And finally, the penalties — because he handled this like a grown-up.

Asked about three calls that went against Seattle — the sideline catch he challenged, the late hit called on Jalen Sundell, and the forward progress ruling on the fumble:

“Well, yeah, I think those just didn’t go our way. They saw Jalen as a late hit. I think they went to New York on the challenge — I think they said it was part of like a toe drag, so that can continue into — and so, not enough to overturn. And then forward progress, I think, is not reviewable to my knowledge. So we kind of drew the short end of the stick on those.”

“Kind of drew the short end of the stick.”

That’s it. That’s the entire complaint. No tirade, no conspiracy, and he actually explained the rules properly — including the correct note that forward progress rulings are not reviewable. You cannot challenge a referee’s judgment about where a runner’s momentum stopped. He knew that and said so.

And remember: he’d already said the penalties put Seattle in “not-great situations” and his team didn’t flinch anyway. The calls went wrong and they won by fourteen.
