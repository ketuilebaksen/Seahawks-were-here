“I know normally I’m pretty coy on this one, but it’s pretty straightforward.”

Seahawks Nation, stop right there. That is Mike Macdonald telling you, out loud, that he’s about to break his own rule.

And here’s what came next: “If he has a good week, he’s going to play a significant amount, if not the whole game.”

He’s talking about Anthony Bradford. The starting right guard. The man whose absence has been strangling this entire offense for five weeks.

A head coach who hedges every single question in that room just told you his starting right guard might play the whole game against Kansas City. That’s not a hint. That’s a declaration.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here, and I have been waiting to make this video for three weeks. Because after the worst ten days of Seattle’s season, this football team is about to get substantially stronger in three different places at once — and the man in charge said something on Monday that I don’t think he’s said all year.

He said: “I think we’re definitely more aligned as a team than we were a few days ago.”

Let’s go through all of it. The reinforcements, the one real loss, and then why I think this Sunday night is the most important game of Seattle’s season — and why I think they’re genuinely built to win it.

First, what actually happened over the break, because this part matters more than any stat.

Macdonald came out of Denver frustrated — and he was specific about why. Not about turnovers. About identity. His words: “Where my frustration lie was just the identity of us and what it feels like, you know, Seahawk football — and that feeling wasn’t there.”

And then he said something I thought was remarkably self-aware for a head coach: “You’re frustrated, but also without the facts, and like without the objectivity behind everything. So I think that’s what makes the problem-solving process interesting.”

Read that again. A head coach admitting out loud that right after a loss, you have the anger but not the information. That’s why the ten-day break mattered.

So what did he do with it? He had conversations. Real ones. With Sam Darnold, with Jake Peetz, with offensive coordinator Brian Fleury, and with Cooper Kupp.

And on Kupp specifically: “Coop’s a tremendous asset for us. So we want all those guys to feel like we’re all part of the solution and how we’re going to move forward. Coop’s been great.”

“All part of the solution.” That’s the second time in a week Macdonald has used that exact word about a player — he used it about Darnold on Friday too. That’s not an accident. That’s a head coach deliberately refusing to let anybody in that building become the villain.

And his verdict: “I’m proud of the couple days of work we put in. I think we had some honest conversations, an honest process. I think we’re definitely more aligned as a team than we were a few days ago. And I’m excited about where we can go, honestly.”

But he immediately put the brake on it, and this is why I trust him: “We’ve really had one good day’s worth of work up to this point. So it’s a matter of following through and making sure we’re hitting the checkpoints on a daily basis.”

One day. He’s not selling you a turnaround. He’s telling you the first day was good.

Now — reinforcement number one, and it’s the biggest. Anthony Bradford.

Let me give you the full picture, because the details make it better.

Bradford went on injured reserve on September twenty-third with knee and hip injuries. He has missed five games. And on Monday, he returned to practice — Seattle designated him to return, which opens his practice window.

Small but important mechanic, because you’ll see it reported both ways this week: he is not on the fifty-three-man roster yet. A designation to return lets him practice without taking a roster spot. Seattle has to formally activate him before he can play. Macdonald himself said “activated” casually on Monday — the paperwork is a return designation.

But the football answer is the one that matters, and Macdonald gave it as plainly as he’s ever given anything: “If he has a good week, he’s going to play a significant amount, if not the whole game.”

And then he described what Seattle gets back: “AB’s played really good football for us. He’s a physical presence for us at right guard, both in run and pass.”

Both in run and pass. Hold onto that phrase, because it’s the entire answer to Seattle’s season.

Here’s why. In Denver, Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks, and under pressure he went 6-of-19 for 47 yards. He’s taken fourteen sacks this season — five against Denver, five the week before against San Francisco. In Bradford’s absence, Seattle rotated a second-year player and a rookie at right guard — Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens — and against Denver, both allowed multiple pressures.

And on the ground: Seattle’s rushing offense has fallen from 123.3 yards a game last season to 107.2.

One position. Both problems. Every single thing people have been yelling about for two weeks — the interceptions, the sacks, the dead run game — runs through a right guard who has been in street clothes since September.

And Macdonald added the part that genuinely surprised me: “I think he’s a better version of himself than he was before he got injured.”

Asked what he meant: “Normally a guy is forced to go on IR and it’s a bummer... and you could sense there’s frustration there because he wants to be healthy, he wants to be playing great football. But there’s a way that you can approach that too. And the way he’s approached it with positivity and energy — I think he’s been kind of a sense of strength for the guys.”

A man on injured reserve becoming a source of strength for the players who are playing. That’s a locker room.

And Macdonald gave credit where it belonged, to John Benton, Seattle’s senior offensive assistant and offensive line coach: “John deserves a ton of credit with that, and so does Anthony — about just a daily approach of chipping away at becoming a better version of yourself.”

Then he turned it into a coaching confession that I thought was the most interesting thirty seconds of the press conference: “We’ve learned our lesson on that front... you don’t just coach your starters. You coach everybody, man.”

And he explained where that lesson came from: “You show up, you don’t know a player, you make judgment about a player — it’s like, what are you doing? Just coach a guy. It’s April. Go coach the heck out of them and see where you can go.”

That’s a head coach telling you how a fourth-round guard became a starter he’d now play for sixty minutes.

Reinforcement number two. Zach Charbonnet — and the news here moved.

For three weeks the answer has been vague. On Monday it wasn’t. Macdonald: “Zach is going to have a full week’s worth of reps, so that’s exciting. And there’s a possibility that you could see him on Sunday in some capacity.”

A full week of reps. And a possibility you see him Sunday. That is the most optimistic thing anybody in that building has said about Charbonnet since he got hurt.

And now I can tell you exactly when he got hurt, which I couldn’t before: Charbonnet tore his ACL in the divisional-round win over the San Francisco 49ers, and had surgery in February.

So think about the arc. He blew out his knee in a playoff game, had surgery in February, and he’s being discussed for a game in October. Macdonald on that: “It’s remarkable. I know he’s had a certain date in mind, which is a really ambitious date. And for those who have tried to come back from a significant knee surgery, understand the undertaking that takes.”

And then, about the player: “Zach is — I mean, he’s a beast. He looks great.”

And this, which I loved: “It’s not easy to kind of say, ‘all right, let’s go,’ you know? So for him to have that courage to say that, and work through the things he’s worked through — you can’t do anything but tip your cap to him.”

Now the deadline, because it’s right on top of us. Charbonnet’s twenty-one-day practice window opened October first. It closes October twenty-second — Thursday. Two days. Either he goes on the fifty-three-man roster, or he reverts to the physically-unable-to-perform list for the rest of the season. There is no third option and no extension.

After Monday, I’d be stunned if he isn’t activated. “A full week’s worth of reps” is not what you say about a man you’re about to shelve for the year.

And what Seattle gets: twelve rushing touchdowns last season with zero starts. Twenty-one career rushing touchdowns. And he costs no draft pick and no cap space.

Although note the careful phrase Macdonald used — “in some capacity.” That’s not twenty carries. That might be eight touches and third downs. Which is still eight more than Seattle has had.

Reinforcement number three, and almost nobody is covering this one. Seattle added two bodies built for exactly what’s broken.

On Monday, Seattle signed fullback Jackson Acker and tight end Josiah Deguara to the practice squad. To make room, they released running back Dontae McMillan and receiver Malick Meiga.

Now, why does that matter? Because a reporter asked Macdonald directly whether the signing meant Seattle wanted a different type of fullback — Seattle lost Robbie Ouzts earlier in the year and Brady Russell has been filling in.

Macdonald’s answer: “I wouldn’t say it’s a commentary on our current abilities.” And then, on what the new man brings: “It gives us some more opportunity to be in two-back sets — and even the stuff that Brady does, why not in two-back sets but still be in base.”

Two-back sets. Read that as what it is: Seattle just went shopping for more bodies in the run game two days after its head coach said the team didn’t feel like Seahawks football.

And Deguara is interesting. A 2020 third-round pick out of Cincinnati, seven seasons, 78 games and 12 starts, 53 catches for 471 yards and three touchdowns — and one game this season with Philadelphia. He’s not a receiving weapon. He’s a hybrid blocker who can line up in the backfield. That’s a specific hire for a specific job.

One mechanic to keep straight: both of them are on the practice squad, not the fifty-three. To play Sunday, they’d have to be signed to the active roster or elevated — and remember, a team gets only two elevations per game.

Now the one real loss, and it’s a genuine blow. Elijah Arroyo.

Macdonald: “Elijah, he’s going to be out a significant amount of time. How long that is, I’m not sure. There’s some options on the table that I think we’re just about finalized.”

Asked straight out if that means injured reserve: “That’s what I’m saying. Like, I could say that, but then you never know between now and when I know for sure. So I’ll know more on Wednesday.”

Reports since have said Arroyo is headed to IR. As of Monday night it wasn’t formally done, so I’ll tell you what’s confirmed: out a significant amount of time, IR expected, final word Wednesday.

And here’s why it hurts more than a tight end line in a box score. The one structural thing Brian Fleury built this offense around was two tight ends on the field together — Arroyo and A.J. Barner — because that personnel group forces a linebacker to honor the run and a safety to honor the seam, and that conflict is exactly what loosens the bracket on Jaxon Smith-Njigba. Denver held JSN to two catches for eleven yards in the first half doing precisely that.

Which, by the way, is exactly why Seattle signed a hybrid blocker and a fullback on Monday. They’re not replacing Arroyo’s hands. They’re replacing his job.

And one more roster note: Jadarian Price is not available this week. Macdonald: “I think we got one more game and then we’ll go from there. No update on JD. He’s doing well, though.” Price becomes eligible after Sunday — so the Chicago game on November second is his target.

Also: Drew Lock was dealing with an illness on Monday but was in the building.

Now let me give you the best football of the whole press conference, because Macdonald taught three things and he didn’t have to.

One, on fundamentals. Asked whether the identity problem is just turnovers, he went to tackling: “If we’re not tackling well, it’s not because the guys don’t want to tackle. It’s probably because there’s too much space. It’s probably because there’s a fundamental that maybe we could have worked better.”

And then the punchline: “If we tackle great, then everyone’s going to say, ‘hey man, they’re playing great Seahawk defense’ — well, because our fundamentals are on point.”

Identity isn’t a feeling. It’s a fundamental executed often enough that people give it a name. That’s one of the best things I’ve heard a coach say this year.

Two, on scheme. Asked if he needs to change things schematically: “Short answer is yes. The longer answer is — are we running bad plays? No. The plays are fine. It’s how we get to the plays. It’s how we run the plays. It’s how we choose the plays. How we execute the plays.”

That is a precise diagnosis and most people will misread it. He’s not saying the playbook is wrong. He’s saying the delivery system is. Same plays, better sequencing, better situations, better timing.

Three — and this is my favorite — on “operations.” Asked what he meant by looking at operations, he said this: “You get, I don’t know, on average sixty-something opportunities to snap the ball a game on offense and defense, and with special teams you’re looking at eighty-something. It’s not that many snaps, okay? And so those are precious snaps.”

Precious snaps. Eighty-something chances in a week of work.

And then: “That’s why we end up running the same thing every week. I mean, just watch the tapes — like, the same coverages. But how you deploy people, when you call it, how you get there, what you practice, what information you give the players all week.”

A head coach telling you his defense runs the same coverages on purpose, and the edge is in deployment, not invention. That’s “elegant simplicity” — fewer words meaning more things — and it’s exactly how this team won a championship.

And finally, on all the shotgun against Denver: “As a team, we can do a better job of not being down multiple scores in the second half.” And: “There’s a time and place to kick it into high gear, and there’s a time and place to stay in our normal operation as well.”

Translation: we were in shotgun because we were down eleven, and the real fix is not being down eleven.

Okay. Now let me tell you why Sunday night is a must-win, and I don’t use that phrase lightly in October.

Seattle is 3-3. San Francisco is 5-0 and leading this division. That’s a two-game gap plus the tiebreaker, in a division where the 49ers already beat Seattle 32-29.

And the three Seattle losses have come by two points, three points and three points. Eight points total. This is not a team being beaten up. It’s a team losing the last two minutes — which means every remaining game is genuinely winnable and genuinely loseable.

Then look at what’s coming. Kansas City Sunday night. Chicago on November second. And the trade deadline on November tenth — meaning this game largely decides whether Seattle spends the next three weeks adding or evaluating.

And here’s the part that makes it personal: Kenneth Walker the Third is coming. The MVP of Seattle’s Super Bowl, now in Kansas City, with 537 rushing yards in four games — more than Seattle’s entire team has in five. He’s averaging 134.3 a game, the highest in the NFL, at 6.2 a carry.

Against the streak: Seattle has not allowed a 100-yard rusher in 35 consecutive games, the longest run in franchise history.

So that’s the game. Seattle gets its right guard back, maybe its running back back, and two new bodies for the run game — in the exact week the best running back in football comes to town wearing someone else’s jersey.

Lose it, and you’re 3-4 with a trip to Chicago, three games back of San Francisco, and a front office deciding whether this is a team worth buying for.

Win it, and you’re 4-3 with Price coming back the next week, Bradford settled in, Charbonnet building up, and a win over an undefeated-caliber opponent on national television — and suddenly those three one-possession losses look like a slow start instead of a pattern.

That’s the fork. One game.

Let me close.

For three weeks I’ve been telling you the fixes were in the building and not on another team’s roster. On Monday, the head coach activated them. Bradford practicing and maybe playing the whole game. Charbonnet taking a full week of reps with a Thursday deadline. A fullback and a hybrid blocker signed for two-back sets. And a locker room that spent its break talking instead of leaking.

And Macdonald’s own summary: “I’m excited about where we can go, honestly.” Followed immediately by: “We’ve really had one good day’s worth of work.”

That’s the right amount of hope. Earned, and not spent yet.

“The plays are fine. It’s how we get to the plays.”

Go through, not around.
