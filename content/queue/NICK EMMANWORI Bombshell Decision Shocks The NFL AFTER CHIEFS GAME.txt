First: who this man actually is, because the numbers are not normal and I need you to see them.

Nick Emmanwori came out of the 2025 NFL Combine and broke it.

He ran a 4.38-second forty-yard dash — second-best among 2025 safeties, and thirteenth all-time.

His ten-yard split was 1.49 — best among 2025 safeties, seventh all-time.

He jumped 43 inches vertically — best among 2025 safeties, fourth all-time.

He broad-jumped eleven feet, six inches — best among 2025 safeties, fourth all-time.

And he put up twenty reps on the bench press — again, best among the safeties.

Now here’s the one that stops the conversation. There’s a metric called the Relative Athletic Score, built by Kent Lee Platte, that converts a prospect’s testing into a single number out of ten by comparing him to every player ever tested at his position.

Nick Emmanwori scored a perfect 10.00 — the best Relative Athletic Score ever recorded by a strong safety among 1,236 prospects tested since 1987.

The best. Ever. Out of twelve hundred and thirty-six men. Since 1987.

His prospect grade of 6.41 ranked first among all safeties, fourth among all defensive backs, and eighteenth among every single participant at that combine.

And he fell out of the first round. Safeties get devalued — that’s the business. So Seattle traded up and took him 35th overall in the second round.

Twelves, hold that next to the thing a national analyst noticed on Sunday night: Emmanwori is listed at 220 pounds and ran stride for stride down the field with Xavier Worthy, who is listed at 165 and owns the fastest forty in combine history.

A fifty-pound difference, and nobody was open. That’s what a 4.38 at 220 pounds buys you.

Now — the thing in this press conference that I have never heard a player describe before. His weekly schedule.

A reporter asked what it takes during the week to prepare him for everything he does. And his answer:

“Yeah, it’s a little bit of a process honestly. Making sure I’m getting all my reps all over the field — whether it’s defensive end, nickel, whatever packages they have for me. But also keeping me limited where I’m not burning myself out mentally or physically. It’s something new that I feel like they’re going through too, and we’re steadily growing and changing stuff throughout the week.”

And then came the follow-up, and the answer is the best detail of the week:

“Are you bouncing around different meeting rooms?”

“Yeah. I go to the linebacker rooms on like Wednesday, D-line room on like Friday or Thursday, DB rooms every day. So I’m in different rooms, different areas. I’m hearing a lot of stuff at one time and just processing it the right way.”

Three position rooms. Every week.

Twelves, understand what that means. Every other player in that building learns one job. The corner sits in the corner room. The edge rusher sits in the edge room. They watch their own tape, they get their own coaching, they go home.

Nick Emmanwori learns three. Linebacker on Wednesday. Defensive line on Thursday or Friday. Defensive backs every single day. Three sets of coaches, three sets of calls, three different ways of looking at the same offense — and then he has to keep them straight at full speed on Sunday night.

“I’m hearing a lot of stuff at one time and just processing it the right way.”

And somebody in that room asked the perfect follow-up: “So you’re not in the kicker room yet?”

“No. Not the kicker room.”

Give it a year.

And let me actually spell out the three jobs, because “versatile” is a word people throw around without meaning anything.

He named them himself: “lining up at defensive end, covering in the slot, running, tackling, being aggressive.” Three completely different trades.

Job one — defensive end. His hand is in the dirt on the edge of the line. His job is to beat a 320-pound offensive tackle, set an edge against the run, and get to the quarterback. The men he’s competing against in that room weigh sixty to a hundred pounds more than he does.

Job two — nickel. He’s the fifth defensive back, usually over the slot receiver. Now he’s in space, matched against a route runner, reading releases and breaking on throws. Completely opposite skill set. Instead of fighting through a hand, he’s trying not to get his hips turned.

Job three — safety. Deep middle of the field, eyes on the quarterback, responsible for the whole back half of the defense. The farthest thing from a man with his hand on the grass.

Those are three different sports. And the reason the first one works is the thing he told us himself: “I grew up playing defensive back. So I’m not scared out there in space.”

That’s the key sentence in the whole press conference. Most big, physical defenders get moved around because they’re big. Emmanwori is a defensive back who happens to be built like an edge rusher — which means the coverage isn’t the part he’s learning. The coverage is his native language, and the pass rush is the bonus.

That’s why Macdonald can put him anywhere. And it’s why three meeting rooms is possible instead of insane.

Now the part that matters most, and it’s the cost of all this.

Remember what Mike Macdonald said about him after the Chiefs game? “As coaches, we bear the responsibility of trying to have him — and really our best players — affect the game the best we can without overloading him. That’s a burden that we carry every week.”

“Without overloading him.” I flagged that quote as a coach protecting a young player from his own versatility.

Well, here’s the player’s version of the exact same thing: “making sure I’m getting a lot of reps, but also keeping me limited where I’m not burning myself out mentally or physically.”

The coach and the player described the identical problem, in two different rooms, in the same week. That’s alignment you can’t fake.

And then it got concrete. Nick Emmanwori was listed as questionable going into the Chiefs game. Asked what that was about:

“Just throughout the week, with doing all these different positions and stuff, I get a lot of reps. So just trying to find a good way to make sure I’m getting my reps at whatever position I’m at and keeping me good for the game. So it was just a little bit of tightness that we solved and figured out. It was good.”

Read between those lines. The hip tightness that put him on the injury report on Friday wasn’t a collision. It came from the rep load of playing three positions.

That is the actual price of the most versatile player in football. You don’t get the 220-pound safety running with Xavier Worthy for free. You get him by spending Wednesdays, Thursdays and Fridays on him, and sometimes he shows up on the injury report because of it.

And here’s the competitive edge he just gave away — on purpose.

Asked about covering the fastest man in the NFL downfield:

“I grew up playing defensive back. So I’m not scared out there in space, you know, to cover guys. I know the defensive coaches know what I can do. They know what I have — but other teams probably don’t. But it’s good just to put that on film.”

“Other teams probably don’t. But it’s good just to put that on film.”

Twelves, that’s a twenty-two-year-old telling you he just revealed a weapon. Until Sunday night, the rest of the league had a theory about what Nick Emmanwori could do. Now they have tape.

And it cuts both ways. The edge of surprise is gone — but so is the possibility of anybody ignoring him. Every offensive coordinator left on this schedule now has to game-plan for a safety who might line up at defensive end and might run with a 4.2 receiver. You can’t prepare for both with the same play call.

And he gave away how much he enjoys it: “I like those plays. I like to run, so I was looking forward to it. And it was great. It was great.”

Now the Mahomes chase-down — in his own words, and it’s better than I expected.

You remember the play: Seattle intercepted Patrick Mahomes, and Ty Okada came down with it. Mike Macdonald credited the pressure. Here is what actually happened, from the man who caused it:

“A lot of people were saying usually people don’t chase him down, he usually outruns people. That’s what they were saying on the sideline. But knowing my speed... on that play I had like an inside blitz or whatever, so it wasn’t really my responsibility. But right when I see him pull the ball, I was like, ‘Oh man, it’s on.’ Like it’s a race to him.”

“It wasn’t really my responsibility.”

He left his assignment to go race Patrick Mahomes. And he won.

“I’d say he made a bad throw — or I forced him into a bad throw with my pressure, my speed. And we got an interception off of that. So I know he probably wants that one back.”

“I know he probably wants that one back.” Said about the reigning Super Bowl MVP, with zero arrogance in it. He’s just describing what happened.

Now the number I didn’t have until today, and it’s extraordinary.

Seven.

Not two edge rushers and a blitzer. Seven different men reached the best quarterback of his generation.

And Emmanwori’s explanation is the most honest thing any player has said about this defense:

Any one of us on that defense can disrupt the game — whether it’s our tackles, our corners, our safeties. It’s lights out.”

“We don’t even know what we’re doing in the moment until we watch the stats and look at the film.”

Twelves, that’s not a man admitting confusion. That’s a man describing flow. They’re playing so fast that the accounting happens afterward. Four different people described a swarm. Emmanwori is the one who admitted the swarm doesn’t always know its own shape.

Twelves, I want to flag one word in there, because it’s a real football term and it tells you something: “cloud.”

Cloud coverage means the cornerback rolls down to take the flat and the short area while a safety takes the deep responsibility over the top. It’s a bracket. It’s what you call when you’ve decided a receiver is not allowed to beat you one-on-one, so you assign two men and a rule to him.

So a Seattle defensive player just told you, using the technical term, that Kansas City was cloud-bracketing Jaxon Smith-Njigba — and he still went for nine catches and 150 yards.

That’s the whole story. JSN leads the NFL with 867 receiving yards — 231 clear of second place — against brackets. And he’s four yards behind Lance Alworth’s 871 for second-most through seven games in the Super Bowl era.

And the man saying it plays defense for a living. When a safety who studies coverage says a receiver is unguardable, that’s not fan talk. That’s a professional opinion.
