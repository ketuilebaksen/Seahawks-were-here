“Dark side defense.”

Seahawks Nation, that is not my phrase. I didn’t make that up. I didn’t put it on a thumbnail to get you to click.

That came out of a national television studio on Sunday night, from analysts who do not cover this team, who have no reason to be kind to us, talking about what Seattle just did to Patrick Mahomes.

And the full sentence was better: “Dark side defense. That’s what they are known as. And they put Kansas City in hell.”

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seahawks 20, Chiefs 3. And today I’m not going through a Seattle press conference — I’ve done two of those this week. Today I want you to hear how the rest of the league is talking about us now. Because it changed on Sunday night, and it changed completely.

Let’s go.

First, the thing every analyst in America said about this roster — and then the correction that matters more.

The first observation was the obvious one: “They have identified length and speed.”

That’s how Seattle builds. Long arms, long strides, guys who can run. You’ve heard it a hundred times.

But then the analyst stopped himself and said something I want you to really sit with:

“It’s not just about the talent. I want to be clear about that, because everyone’s trying to identify length and speed — but it’s the way that they’re getting them to play together, too, which I think is really impressive.”

And this: “What Mike Macdonald’s done a tremendous job of is getting them to play hard. And so when you watch the defense play, it looks different.”

“It looks different.”

Twelves, every team in the NFL is trying to draft length and speed. Every general manager has that same list taped to his wall. What Seattle has that they don’t is the part you can’t scout: eleven men who play like one. Analysts watched the tape and said the special thing about this defense isn’t the measurements — it’s the coaching.

Now the physics. Because this was the detail of the entire broadcast and it’s absurd.

Nick Emmanwori was in single coverage with Xavier Worthy on a deep ball.

Let me tell you who those two men are. Xavier Worthy ran the fastest forty-yard dash in the history of the NFL combine. Nobody has ever been timed faster. Nick Emmanwori is a safety who pass rushes off the edge.

And one of the analysts actually looked up the weights on his phone during the game, because he couldn’t believe what he was watching:

“He’s listed at 220 pounds. Xavier Worthy’s listed at 165 pounds, right? So whether those things are accurate or not, we’re talking about 50 pounds different.”

Fifty pounds. And he was stride for stride.

“He’s a gigantic defender who’s stride for stride” with the fastest man ever to run at the combine. That is not a matchup. That is a violation of how football is supposed to work. You are supposed to choose: big, or fast. Seattle has a man who is both, and he’s twenty-two years old.

And then they gave a second example in the same breath:

“Byron Murphy runs down a screen. He’s 306 pounds.”

Three hundred and six pounds, chasing a screen pass sideways across the field and catching a much smaller skill player who was, in his words, “scooting around everywhere.”

That’s a nose tackle doing a cornerback’s job. That’s what “length and speed” actually looks like when it shows up on a Sunday night.

Now the Kenneth Walker side — and the freeze frame that proves Macdonald told you the truth.

Remember what Mike Macdonald said when I covered his press conference? He said the plan for Walker was: “We have to play in waves... he’s still going to break tackles. That’s what he does. And so we had to get people to the football.”

Here is a national analyst, independently, looking at the tape:

“He’s been running the football well against people. They bottled him up. And when you stop the frame and you say, how many guys are in the picture? I mean, there’s ten, eleven guys oftentimes in the picture when it happens. Look, hats to the football.”

Ten and eleven men in the frame.

Do you understand what that means? On a Kenneth Walker carry, when you pause the broadcast, almost the entire Seattle defense is in the shot. Not two guys. Everybody.

Macdonald said get people to the football. The freeze frame says eleven hats. A coach described his plan on Wednesday and an analyst discovered it on the film Sunday night.

And the result: Walker, averaging a hundred and thirty-three yards a game, got thirty-six. The reigning Super Bowl MVP, held to thirty-six. And the streak lives at thirty-six straight games without allowing a hundred-yard rusher.

Now Mahomes. And this one’s going to be fun.

“We rarely see Patrick Mahomes play in a game and you think it looks like it’s going fast around him right now. It looks as he’s getting pursued, people are getting to him.”

“It looks like it’s going fast around him.”

That’s the highest compliment a defense can get. Mahomes’s whole career is about slowing the game down — standing in a muddy pocket, drifting, waiting, finding the throw nobody else sees. On Sunday night, the game sped up on him.

And they named the men who did it: “This is obviously one where Emmanwori gets to him. DeMarcus Lawrence got to him another time.”

A safety and a thirty-four-year-old edge rusher. That’s the two ends of Seattle’s defensive spectrum, both arriving at Patrick Mahomes.

And then they quoted Cris Collinsworth from the broadcast, and this is the line that’s going to live:

“You rarely see Mahomes just look the way he looked, which is like: I’ve had enough of this. Not that he tapped out, but just — there was nowhere to go tonight.”

“There was nowhere to go.”

Not they covered well. Not the pressure was good. Nowhere to go. Patrick Mahomes stood in Lumen Field and ran out of answers.

Now the chain — and twelves, this is the part I’ve been teaching you all month, and a national panel just drew it out loud.

Here’s exactly how they described it:

“They couldn’t establish the run. So now play-action doesn’t work. Now we can just get after you with our front four. He’s running for his life. You don’t have guys that can consistently get open, and then it’s like a feeding frenzy.”

That’s the whole night in five steps. You can’t run the ball. Therefore play-action doesn’t work — because play-action is a loan against your run game, and if the defense doesn’t fear the run, the fake doesn’t buy you a single second. So the defense doesn’t need to blitz; it wins with four. So the quarterback is running for his life. Feeding frenzy.

I’ve been telling you for weeks that play-action is a loan. Kansas City tried to borrow on Sunday night and Seattle’s credit department said no.

And another analyst put the same thing in one sentence: “That Seattle defense made the Chiefs one-dimensional. The fact that they couldn’t run the ball — and when they couldn’t run the ball, all they did was pin their ears back and go get Mahomes.”

“Pin their ears back.” That’s the reward for stopping the run. Everything else is downstream of thirty-six yards.

Now two numbers from that broadcast, because they’re brutal.

The Chiefs have now lost thirty-eight straight games in which they were held scoreless in the first half — including the playoffs. Thirty-eight. Seattle shut them out in the first half, which historically has meant the game is already over. And it was.

And Seattle kept the Chiefs out of the red zone for the entire night. One analyst had to say it twice because his co-host didn’t believe him: “They kept the Chiefs out the red zone the whole night. Four whole quarters.”

It is the first time that has ever happened in a Patrick Mahomes start.

And here’s the standings line, which matters for us more than for them. After Sunday, only one undefeated team is left in the entire NFL: the Minnesota Vikings. The 49ers lost. The Chiefs lost — to us.

San Francisco lost. Write that one down and underline it. Seattle beat the last unbeaten team in the AFC on the same weekend the team ahead of them in their own division finally cracked. The division is live, and it’s live because of Sunday night.

Now the drive that one analyst called a bank robbery. And he’s right.

Everybody wants to talk about Jaxon Smith-Njigba. One of the panelists refused: “I don’t even want to talk about JSN and the offense. I want to talk about Seattle’s defense.”

But another one found the single most impressive offensive sequence of the night, and it wasn’t a touchdown pass:

“They got into a four-minute offense and they said: we’re going to run the football, Kansas City, and you can’t stop it. And they ran the football for a touchdown. They took four minutes and forty-six seconds off the clock and they ran the football for a touchdown.”

Four minutes and forty-six seconds. On the ground. Announced in advance. That’s Emanuel Wilson’s twenty-one-yard touchdown run at the end of a drive where Kansas City knew exactly what was coming and could not stop it.

And then the best analogy of the night: “That is domination. Hey, I’m coming to your bank at noon, and I’m going in your vault, and I’m taking all the money, and ain’t nothing you can do about it.” Somebody called ahead, said when they’d be there, showed up, took the money, and the police couldn’t do a thing.

Twelves, remember who this offense was two weeks ago: twenty-ninth in the league in rush EPA. On Sunday night they ran out the clock on the Kansas City Chiefs with the whole stadium knowing the plan. Anthony Bradford came back at right guard. Zero sacks. A hundred and fifty-three rushing yards. Thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds of possession. One more time: the fix was a guard, not a trade.

Now Jaxon Smith-Njigba — because national analysts have officially stopped arguing.

First, the setup. Back in September, the whole league was debating who you’d take if you could have one receiver. Lots of names. Here’s where that debate is now:

“We were having the conversation early in the year — can’t take one receiver, who do you take? And a lot of names being thrown out there. But JSN is unbelievable.”

And then the other one, listing the men he loves and conceding anyway: “I love Chase. I love Jeudy. I love Puka. It’s just hard — and harder. Oh, he shows up every week. Nine, a buck fifty, one TD.”

“Nine, a buck fifty, one TD.” Nine catches, a hundred and fifty yards, one touchdown.

And then the stat: he is the only player with at least 825 receiving yards and eight touchdowns over the first seven weeks since the merger.

Since the merger. That’s 1970. Fifty-six seasons of football. One man.

Now here’s the part that made me the happiest, because it confirms exactly what Jaxon himself told us at his own podium:

“They’ve done a very good job of moving him around, not having it be predictable in terms of where he’s lined up. But he’s good outside the numbers. He’s remarkable inside the numbers. And they’ve done a good job of giving him some option route stuff where it’s almost impossible to cover him. And when you look at guys that can win at the line of scrimmage when you try to press him — he right now, in terms of the league, has got to be at the top of the list.”

“At the top of the list” at beating the press.

And what did Jaxon say about press coverage himself? “I love to see it. I invite it.”

The player said he invites it. The analysts say nobody in football does it better. Those two statements landed within an hour of each other, and that’s as close to proof as this sport gets.

“Option route stuff where it’s almost impossible to cover him” is the scheme answer behind his own description of attacking the middle. Klint Kubiak isn’t moving him around for fun — he’s built a package where the defender has to be right twice: once at the line, and again at the top of the route.

And one more small thing I loved. The quarterback got called by his receiver’s jersey number: “Meanwhile, Sam Darnold — throw it to eleven.”

“Throw it to eleven.” Jaxon Smith-Njigba wears number eleven. That’s a national analyst reducing Seattle’s passing game to three words — and doing it with respect, nine days after the entire internet wanted Sam Darnold benched. Twenty of twenty-eight, 257 yards, zero sacks, hit twice. And his own receiver took the blame for the interception. That’s the quarterback who was supposedly finished.

Now let me be fair to Kansas City, because the panel was, and because you deserve the full picture.

They were honest that it wasn’t all Seattle: “Obviously there were some key drops, obviously missed field goals that could have probably made the game a little closer.” True — though when you gain 213 yards and never reach the red zone, a little closer is about as far as that argument goes.

And they were genuinely shocked by the tackling: “That was awful tackling for a Spags defense. So many broken tackles... the running back was getting by one man, the second man missing, and the third person from pursuit is making the tackle. The tackling was horrible tonight. Horrendous.”

“Horrible. Horrendous.” About a Steve Spagnuolo defense. That’s not a small thing to say.

And a structural problem: “Something is missing from this Kansas City defense. They get pressure, but they don’t get the quarterback on the ground.” That’s why Sam Darnold was hit twice and sacked zero times. Pressure that doesn’t finish is just exercise.

They also noted the Chiefs are short on vertical threats with Tyquan Thornton out — “they don’t really have that vertical threat other than Worthy” — and Worthy spent the night running next to a 220-pound safety.

And then one of them just said the quiet thing out loud.

“And maybe it’s a situation where Seattle is just a better team. That’s all — you also have to factor that in, too. Maybe, from top to bottom, they’re just the better team.”

Followed by: “They are a Super Bowl defense now. You know what I mean? This is the best of the best.”

And: “This was a total domination by a Seattle defense that looked like they did the back half of last year. This is the best that we’ve seen them look this year.”

“The best of the best.” “A Super Bowl defense.” From people who get paid to be skeptical.

And I appreciated the cold water too: “I don’t want to say this ain’t a big deal, because they came into the game 5-0. But it’s one game. I think they still got some holes in their offense. But I think Seattle just answered the bell today.”

“Answered the bell.” One game. But the right game.

And twelves — they talked about you. On national television, discussing why Seattle is so hard to visit:

“It’s one of the toughest places to play. Their home field advantage — the crowd really gets into it. They call their crowd the twelfth man. And they really give those guys something to cheer about.” Then: “They don’t have a basketball team, but they rally behind those guys — and they gave them something to cheer about tonight.”

Yeah. We don’t have a basketball team. And national analysts just explained to America that it’s part of why nobody wants to come here in October. Macdonald said “shout out to the twelves” from his podium. Now the rest of the country is saying it too.

Alright — now let me be straight with you about this video’s title, because I always am.

There is no “shocking decision” in any of this. Nobody announced anything — no press conference, no roster move, no trade. It was a national panel watching the tape and arriving at a conclusion.

But the conclusion is the story. If you want the decision framing, here’s the honest version: the rest of the NFL decided something about Seattle on Sunday night. They decided this is a Super Bowl defense. They decided Jaxon Smith-Njigba is the best receiver in football. They decided, out loud, that Seattle might just be the better team.

That’s not a decision Seattle announced. That’s a decision the league made about Seattle.

And the honest asterisk, same as always. Kansas City was banged up — Rashee Rice out, Kelce limited all week, Brashard Smith and Tyquan Thornton out, and down at least one starting tackle. But Seattle played without Charbonnet, Drake Thomas, Chazz Surratt and its backup quarterback, and lost Charles Cross and Rashid Shaheed during the game. Both teams were hurt. One of them scored three points.

So where does this leave us? Seattle is 4-3 — three losses by a combined eight points, and a seventeen-point win over the team everybody had going back to the Super Bowl. The 49ers lost. Minnesota is the last unbeaten. The NFC West is wide open. Chicago comes next, November second, the deadline is November tenth, and we need updates on Cross, Shaheed and Lock — Cross most of all, because this whole revival was built on a healthy offensive line.

Let me close.

A week ago this team was 3-3 and the national conversation about the Seattle Seahawks was whether the head coach was in trouble and whether the quarterback should be benched.

Today the national conversation is: dark side defense. A 220-pound safety running with the fastest man in combine history. A 306-pound tackle chasing down screens. Eleven hats in the freeze frame. Mahomes with nowhere to go. A four-minute, forty-six-second drive announced in advance. A Super Bowl defense. And maybe — just maybe — the better team.

They didn’t win a press conference on Sunday night. They won the argument.

Emmanwori running with Worthy fifty pounds heavier, Byron Murphy running down a screen at 306, the eleven hats in the frame, or Mahomes looking like a man who’d had enough? Mine’s the fifty pounds.
