Two blockbuster trades. Four days. One Monday night game.

Seahawks Nation, I need you to sit down for this one, because the Chicago Bears just did something insane and almost nobody has connected it to us yet.

Seattle traded a 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick for Quenton Nelson — a six-time All-Pro guard.

Chicago traded their 2027 first-round pick, their 2028 first-round pick, and a 2027 fourth-round pick for Jeffery Simmons — an All-Pro defensive tackle.

Two first-round picks. Against a third and a fifth.

And on Monday night at Lumen Field, those two men are going to line up across from each other.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is 4-3, defending Super Bowl champions. Chicago is 5-2. Mike Macdonald held his Wednesday press conference, and twelves — he gave us everything. The trade, the line shuffle, Charles Cross, Rashid Shaheed, and the hardest conversation he’s had to have all season.

Let’s go.

He opened in the most Macdonald way possible.

“All right, great to see everybody. Welcome to our football Wednesday. I don’t want to hear any otherwise.”

“I don’t want to hear any otherwise.” The man has just traded for a Hall of Famer, his left tackle is hurt, and the first thing out of his mouth is that it’s football Wednesday and nobody’s allowed to say different.

And the message: “Really, the message of the team is how fast we can evolve and move forward and stack wins. And it starts with today’s practice.”

“Stack wins.” Not ride the momentum. Stack. One on top of the other.

Now the trade — exactly what Seattle gave and exactly what Seattle got.

A 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick. No players. Seattle waived guard Christian Haynes to open the roster spot, and the deal becomes official once Nelson passes his physical. Confirmed Tuesday, October twenty-seventh, two weeks before the deadline. Bob Condotta of the Seattle Times had the details.

And here’s who they got.

Quenton Nelson. Thirty years old. Eight-time Pro Bowler. Six-time All-Pro. The sixth overall pick out of Notre Dame in 2018.

One hundred and thirty-six career starts. All seventeen games in each of the last four seasons. All seven this year.

And the number that matters for Monday: his Pro Football Focus grade is 85.7 — third among seventy-four qualified guards in the NFL. He has not allowed a sack this season. He has allowed one quarterback hit.

One hit. All year. Seattle just handed that man to a quarterback who was sacked fourteen times in his first six games.

And the money is the cleanest part. Nelson is on an expiring contract with a $16 million base salary. Remember the mechanic: signing-bonus proration doesn’t travel in a trade; base salary does, and only the unpaid part. So Seattle pays a prorated $9.77 million for the rest of 2026. Seattle had just over $13.4 million in cap room.

And then he’s a free agent. No 2027 money. No 2028 money. It’s a rental, and the rent is a third-round pick.

Why was he available? Because Indianapolis wouldn’t pay him and then fired the man who decides.

Nelson and the Colts couldn’t agree on an extension — reportedly because he wanted to be the highest-paid interior offensive lineman in football, a bar currently set by Tyler Linderbaum at $27 million a year. Then Indianapolis fired general manager Chris Ballard after a 2-4 start, and Ed Dodds took over personnel on an interim basis.

I told you last week exactly what that means: an interim executive collects picks. He doesn’t protect the last guy’s expiring contracts. Seattle understood it first.

Now Macdonald’s own words on the player, and he did not hold back.

“Obviously we’re really excited to have the opportunity to acquire Quenton Nelson. I think John and his team did a great job of making that happen.”

And then: “It’s an opportunity to bring along an all-time, really, probably a surefire Hall of Fame guard — with a great track record, and from our understanding about the type of person he is to join our locker room, it was just an opportunity that’s going to make the Seahawks better.”

“Surefire Hall of Fame guard.” A head coach said that about a man in his second day in the building.

And then he described the tape in Seattle’s own language: “The style of play is the first thing you think of when you think of Quenton. Obviously he’s got incredible ability, but the physicality, the relentless play. We talk about decisive, shock and relentless. I think that’s probably a good way to sum up what it looks like on tape.”

“Decisive, shock and relentless.” Twelves, that’s not a scouting report — that’s the internal standard of this football team, and the new guy already meets it.

And then he was asked how he reacted when the trade went down, and gave the most revealing answer of the day.

“Call me crazy. I don’t really like to react as things are kind of happening in the wild, because we like to be active in a lot of things — and so you kind of put your eggs all in one basket, and then to me emotionally it’s just like, hey, let’s just stay the course, and we’ll coach the heck out of whoever ends up on our team.”

“We’ll coach the heck out of whoever ends up on our team.”

That is a head coach who has deliberately trained himself not to get emotionally attached to trade rumors — because Seattle is “active in a lot of things,” and if you fall in love with every name, you spend your week disappointed. He finds out who’s on the bus and then coaches the bus.

He also explained the job honestly: “As the head coach, you wear two hats. You understand how it’s going to affect your team in the short run, but you think in the long run how great it is for the Seahawks.”

And that brings us to the hardest part of the week. Anthony Bradford.

Before anything else, Macdonald volunteered this — unprompted:

“I just felt like it was necessary saying, I think our offensive line probably played their best game of the season this past Sunday night. And I just want to be clear, this isn’t a commentary on any of our personnel or anything like that. We love our guys.”

“Their best game of the season.” He said it out loud. The week Anthony Bradford came back, Seattle’s offensive line played its best game of the year — and then Seattle traded for a guard anyway.

His framing: “You understand how it affects the guys that have been here and working really hard and have earned a great role in our team. But also, two things can be true at the same time.”

Then a reporter asked him directly: What do you tell Anthony Bradford? What’s the message?

And twelves, this answer is the reason I made this video:

“It’s not an easy situation. I thought Anthony played really good football for us, and he’s earned a significant role in our team — a starting-level role. So it’s one of those situations with AB that is not easy. Really no way to sugarcoat it.”

“Really no way to sugarcoat it.”

He kept going: “I think he believes this when we say this is how much we love him, his teammates — the guys love him in the locker room. He’s a fantastic teammate. We don’t expect him to be jumping for joy in this situation, because that’s not realistic. But he’s going to be a great teammate and he’s going to stay ready. And we know how this league works. I mean, you’re a play away from him playing every play again.”

“We don’t expect him to be jumping for joy, because that’s not realistic.”

Twelves, do you know how rare that is? Most coaches give you he’s a pro, he’ll handle it. Macdonald said: this hurts, he’s allowed to be unhappy about it, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

And he finished: “He’s a tough cookie. The guys will have his back, and we’re behind him as well. But it’s not easy.”

Anthony Bradford has forty career starts at right guard. He came back from a knee injury, started against the Kansas City Chiefs, and Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards with zero sacks allowed. I spent two weeks on this channel telling you he was the fix. He was. And four days later he lost the job to a six-time All-Pro.

That’s the NFL. And at least his head coach said it plainly instead of insulting him with a cliché.

Now the new offensive line, confirmed from the podium.

“He’s going to play left guard, and Grey’s going to slide to the right.”

So: Quenton Nelson at left guard — all 136 of his career starts are there. Grey Zabel to right guard. Zabel has started all twenty-four games of his career at left guard, but Macdonald says the switch is manageable: “He’s had a lot of experience playing on the right side, so that’ll help with the learning curve.” He also played all five line positions at North Dakota State.

And Macdonald was honest about the cost: “We’ve put a lot of time and effort into the chemistry of our offensive line, and we got a couple things shaken up right now — but the guys will do a great job of meshing and getting on the same page, and I think we’ll be better for it.” Plus: “There’s a process to be had, and we got the right guys for the job — but we understand it’s a process as well, and we got to attack the heck out of it.”

“We got a couple things shaken up right now.” That’s a coach admitting he just disturbed the one thing that was working, five days before a prime-time game. I respect it.

And at left tackle: “Josh is our third tackle.”

Josh Jones starts at left tackle while Charles Cross is out. And he’s not walking in cold — Jones played the final twelve snaps against Kansas City when Cross went down, and Seattle didn’t give up a sack in them either.

Now the injury news, and there’s one genuinely big piece of it.

On Cross: “Charles is coming along great. He’s not going to practice today, so he’s doing great.”

I laughed at that one. He’s doing great, and he’s not practicing. But then came the question that actually mattered: Have you ruled out injured reserve?

“As of right now, he’s not going on injured reserve.”

Twelves, that is the headline of the injury report, and here’s why. Putting a player on injured reserve means a minimum of four games. Four. If Seattle thought Charles Cross was going to miss a month, he’d be on IR today and Seattle would have the roster spot.

They didn’t do it. Which means they believe he’s back before four games are up. Reports said a high ankle sprain and a few weeks — and Macdonald has pointedly declined to confirm the severity, saying the early talk didn’t come from him or the medical staff.

“Not going on injured reserve” is the most optimistic sentence available about Charles Cross, and he said it on the record.

On Rashid Shaheed: “Rasheed’s doing well. Practice status today — I don’t think he’s going to practice today. I’ve been wrong before, but I don’t believe he’s going to be out there.” Earlier in the week he called the knee “positive.”

And on Nelson playing Monday: Do you expect Quenton to play all of Monday night? “We expect him to play. Yeah.”

Now Chicago. And this is where it gets scary, because the Bears went further than we did.

The Chicago Bears acquired Jeffery Simmons from the Tennessee Titans — and the price was staggering: Chicago’s 2027 first-round pick, Chicago’s 2028 first-round pick, and a 2027 fourth-round pick. Tennessee sent back a 2028 fifth.

Two first-round picks. And Simmons is on a three-year, $105.8 million extension with $100 million guaranteed, signed in June. He was an All-Pro in 2025.

Here’s what he’s done this season, per Next Gen Stats: twenty-eight pressures — the most of any defensive tackle in football. Ten pressures and three sacks when double-teamed, both the best at his position in the league. Twenty-one run stops, tied for second among defensive tackles.

Ten pressures while being double-teamed. You can put two men on Jeffery Simmons and he still gets there.

Macdonald was asked about it, and he didn’t flinch: “Jeffery is a great player. We feel like the two practices with him have been a great experience. I think you just got to understand how that’s going to affect what we do. He’s on the team now. We got a lot of respect for him. They do a great job on defense, so he’s only going to make them better — obviously. And we expect him to play and be out there, so we have to plan for him.”

“We have to plan for him.”

And it’s not just Simmons. Montez Sweat is back for Chicago from a two-game suspension, and Grady Jarrett is on that front too. Macdonald on Sweat: “He’s a tremendous player... getting him back will help.”

So here is Monday night’s actual matchup:

Quenton Nelson — six-time All-Pro, two days in the building, learning a scheme he himself called “completely different” — against Jeffery Simmons, the most disruptive defensive tackle in football, who also just arrived.

Plus Grey Zabel playing right guard for the first time in his professional life. Plus Josh Jones at left tackle. Against a Bears defense that is fourth in the NFL in pressure rate with ten takeaways, now reinforced by two first-round picks’ worth of defensive line.

That’s the game. That’s the whole game.

And a quick word on the Bears generally, because Macdonald’s respect was real.

“The best compliment we can give them is that they have a clear identity on how they want to operate in all three phases — and they do a great job of backing it up.”

On head coach Ben Johnson, whose Detroit offense Macdonald faced as a defensive coordinator: “We have a lot of respect for their offense. He does a great job of utilizing their personnel to the best of their ability. Their offense has evolved over that amount of time, which is a tip of the cap to how they operate. They play decisive. It’s a style that we respect.”

“They play decisive.” Same word he used for Nelson. That’s a coach telling you the opponent shares his values, which is the highest compliment he has.

Two lighter things, because the presser had them and they’re great.

Asked what stood out on Nelson’s film, Macdonald said: “Run — they run it left.” Then, after the laughter: “I just made that up. I actually don’t know if they ran left or right. But he’s a guy you want to run behind, I know that.”

And then the real answer: “He’s a great puller. I remember him pulling out in space and getting into the alleys. He’s a great athlete. Gets to the second level.” And on fit: “Some of the run schemes are a little bit more wide zone than they’ve been, but nothing that we think that skill set’s going to transfer just fine.”

A great puller who gets to the second level, in a wide-zone offense. Twelves, that is the exact profile for what Seattle runs. This isn’t a luxury signing. It’s a scheme fit.

And the human part. Nelson went to Notre Dame, met his wife in Indiana, and was a Colt for eight years. Asked who introduces him to Seattle’s culture:

“It’s everybody. I think it’ll just happen naturally.” And then this: “Having a similar experience of — you’re coming out here and your wife’s going to be back at home, and there’s a transitional period. I think it’s a blessing in disguise right now where you can focus on football a hundred percent, but it’s also going to be a process.”

His head coach has lived that exact move, and he named the staff who help: Mo, Soup and Connie. Macdonald’s words: “We have a lot of great people in place to help them through that.”

Also, quietly, Seattle made four practice-squad moves Tuesday. They signed receivers Montorie Foster Jr. and Malick Meiga and released fullback Jackson Acker and linebacker Josh Ross. And here’s the detail I love: Foster was waived on Saturday to open a roster spot for Anthony Bradford’s activation from injured reserve.

Seattle cut a wide receiver to activate Anthony Bradford. Bradford’s return produced the best offensive line game of the season. And now Bradford is a backup and the receiver is back on the practice squad. That’s an NFL roster in one sentence: fifty-three chairs, no exceptions.

Now let me be straight about this video’s title, like always.

There is no QB decision in this press conference. None. Zero. Mike Macdonald did not mention Sam Darnold. He did not mention Drew Lock. The word quarterback appears once, when he was asked about Chicago’s pass rush. Nine minutes of podium time and not one syllable about Seattle’s quarterback.

And he doesn’t need to. The real news is a surefire Hall of Famer, an offensive line reshuffled four days before Monday night, a left tackle staying off IR, and a coach refusing to sugarcoat what he did to Anthony Bradford.

That’s bigger than a made-up quarterback story, and you deserve the real one.

Finally — I’m changing my prediction, and I’ll tell you why.

On Tuesday I said Seahawks 27, Bears 17. I’m revising it.

Seahawks 24, Bears 20.

Why? Jeffery Simmons. I made my pick before Chicago spent two first-round picks on the most disruptive interior defender in football. Ten pressures while double-teamed is a problem for a line with two men in new positions and a backup at left tackle. Seattle still has the edge — Chicago is 26th in run defense DVOA and allows 4.4 yards a carry, and Seattle’s defense is first in opponent yards per play and allows the fewest explosive passes in the league.

But this got harder on Thursday, and I’m not going to pretend it didn’t.

Let me close.

Seattle is the defending Super Bowl champion. They just held the best offense in football to three points. Their head coach then traded a third-round pick for a six-time All-Pro, told the press his line had just played its best game of the year anyway, and looked a reporter in the eye and said there’s no way to sugarcoat what that means for Anthony Bradford.

That’s a team that believes this is the year — and a coach honest enough to admit what believing costs.

Twelves, two questions. First: was it worth it? A third and a fifth for ten games of a Hall of Famer, at the cost of moving two linemen and benching the guy who just fixed your run game. And second, the Monday night question: Nelson versus Simmons — who wins that?
