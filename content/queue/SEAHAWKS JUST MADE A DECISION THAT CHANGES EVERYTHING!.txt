Seven days.

Starting today, the Seattle Seahawks have exactly seven days to make a decision that will shape the rest of this season — and if they get it wrong, they lose a man who scored twelve touchdowns last year for the entire rest of the year.

October twenty-second. That’s the date. And it is one week from right now.

And yesterday, ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler reported, citing multiple sources, that Zach Charbonnet has a legitimate chance to make his 2026 debut against the Kansas City Chiefs on October twenty-fifth.

Read those two dates again, Seahawks Nation, because almost nobody is noticing the gap between them.

The deadline is the twenty-second. The debut is the twenty-fifth.

Which means Seattle has to commit to him on paper three days before he actually plays a down. They have to spend the roster spot, burn the option, and sign the cheque — and only then find out if they were right.

Today: the clock on Zach Charbonnet, why he’s a genuinely bigger deal than people remember, the rule that’s kept him invisible all season, and the final injury report for tonight — which has two enormous names on the Denver side. Let’s go.

But first I owe you a straight answer, because the headline going around says Seattle just stole a star running back.

Nobody got stolen this week. There was no trade and there was no new signing.

Here’s what actually happened on the roster, and I’ll be honest, it’s strange: on Tuesday, Seattle moved running back Velus Jones Jr. to the practice squad. On Wednesday, they signed him right back to the fifty-three man roster. A one-day round trip. Meanwhile Irv Charles was activated off injured reserve on Tuesday, released Wednesday, and re-signed to the practice squad the same day.

Neither man had to clear waivers, and here’s the small rule that made it possible: both are vested veterans. A player with four or more accrued seasons who’s released during the season doesn’t go through the waiver wire — he becomes a free agent immediately and can re-sign anywhere, including right back where he came from. That’s how Seattle shuffled two players through the door and back in forty-eight hours without losing either one.

So: real moves, no theft. And one more door I have to close, because I opened it on this channel last week — the Tennessee Titans are reportedly not planning to trade Tony Pollard. I told you Schefter had flagged him as a candidate and that a fifth-round pick might do it. The latest reporting says Tennessee isn’t shopping him. That one’s off the table unless something changes before November tenth.

But here’s why the real story is better than the fake one. Seattle doesn’t need to steal a star running back. They already have one, he’s been in the building all year, and they have seven days to decide whether to let him out.

So let me tell you who Zach Charbonnet actually is, because I think this fan base has forgotten the résumé.

He was the fifty-second pick in the 2023 draft — a second-rounder.

He started at Michigan, where as a true freshman in 2019 he ran for 726 yards and eleven touchdowns and set a Michigan freshman touchdown record. Then he transferred to UCLA, and this is where it gets serious.

2021 at UCLA: 204 carries, 1,137 yards, thirteen touchdowns. Second-team All-Pac-12 and the conference’s Newcomer of the Year.

2022 at UCLA: 195 carries, 1,359 yards, fourteen touchdowns — at seven point zero yards a carry, seventh-best in the entire country. Plus 37 catches. 1,680 all-purpose yards, the seventh-highest single season in the history of UCLA football. And he was named a first-team All-American.

A first-team All-American. That’s the guy.

Now the NFL. 2023: 462 rushing yards and 33 catches as a rookie. 2024: 569 yards and eight touchdowns with eleven starts, including his first hundred-yard game — 134 yards and two scores in Arizona.

And then last season, the Super Bowl season, here’s the line: 184 carries, 730 rushing yards, and twelve rushing touchdowns.

Twelve.

And now the detail that makes that number remarkable, and I had to check it twice: he did that while starting zero games.

Zero starts. Twelve touchdowns. He was the complement — the guy who came in and finished drives — and he scored twelve times doing it.

Career totals: 427 carries, 1,761 rushing yards, twenty-one rushing touchdowns, ninety-five catches for 693 yards. And a Super Bowl LX ring, from a team that beat New England 29-13.

That’s not a depth piece. That’s a starting-caliber NFL running back with a first-team All-American season and a dozen touchdowns in his last healthy year, sitting in a building where the head coach stood at a podium and said “we’re down runners” and “I can’t go out there and carry the ball.”

So why haven’t you seen him? Let me explain the rule, because it’s genuinely one of the strangest in football and it explains every bit of the drama.

Charbonnet tore his ACL on January nineteenth of this year — in the divisional round win over San Francisco, the game Seattle won on its way to a title. He had surgery February twentieth.

He began this season on reserve slash physically-unable-to-perform. Here’s what that means. A player on PUP must miss the first four games. No exceptions. He can’t practice, he can’t play, and — the important part — he doesn’t occupy a spot on the fifty-three man roster. The team gets to keep a full roster and keep him too.

Then, after four games, the team can open a twenty-one day practice window. Seattle opened his on October first. During those twenty-one days he practices with the team — full reps, team periods, everything — and still doesn’t take a roster spot. It’s a free look. The team gets three weeks to watch him move and decide.

And at the end of those twenty-one days there are exactly two outcomes. Activate him to the fifty-three man roster, or he reverts to PUP for the entire rest of the season. There is no third option, no extension, no “let’s see next week.”

October first plus twenty-one days. October twenty-second.

And now you see the trap, and it’s a real one.

If Fowler’s reporting is right and the target is Kansas City on the twenty-fifth, then Seattle must activate him on or before the twenty-second — which means they are making the call based on practice, not on a game. They have to decide he’s ready before he has ever been hit.

Macdonald’s own framing of the decision, back on October first, was all about this: “It’s just about what’s the best time and how does that create the most opportunities to get them ready to play?”

And here’s the one thing in Seattle’s favor, and it’s the hidden gift of this miserable short week.

Playing on Thursday night instead of Sunday gives Zach Charbonnet ten extra days before Kansas City.

Ten days. The schedule that is punishing this football team tonight — four days’ rest, a road trip, altitude — is the same schedule that hands a man ten months removed from ACL surgery an extra week and a half of runway. Tonight hurts. Tonight also helps him.

Now let me tell you why this matters more than any signing Seattle could make, with the honest numbers.

Seattle’s offense right now ranks fourth-worst in the entire National Football League in expected points added. Fourth-worst, on a 3-2 team that’s being carried by its defense.

Seattle went three-for-twelve on third down against San Francisco. Sam Darnold was sacked five times — two of those by men recording the first sack of their NFL careers. Seattle turned the football over four times, and San Francisco converted those into twenty-one points in a game decided by three.

And Emanuel Wilson, who I have defended all month and will keep defending, is averaging about fifty rushing yards a game. He is doing an admirable job as an undrafted free agent carrying an entire position group. He is not a twelve-touchdown back.

Remember what the national panel said after Sunday? Ran Carthon, who used to work in San Francisco’s front office, gave the simplest diagnosis anybody has offered: “The Seahawks need to get healthy in the backfield. You need to get Jadarian Price back. You need to get Zach Charbonnet back. If you have those two in the backfield, that will settle things down for Sam Darnold.”

That will settle things down for Sam Darnold.

And it’s exactly right, and here’s the mechanism. A back who scored twelve touchdowns is a red zone weapon, and the red zone is the one place on the field where a receiver can’t help you, because there’s no space. A credible runner inside the ten means the safety has to come down, which is how you score instead of kicking. And a credible runner on first down means second-and-four instead of second-and-nine — which is how you stop living in third-and-long, which is how you stop getting sacked, which is how you stop throwing the ball into harm’s way.

Darnold’s own phrase from Sunday night was “a us or nobody ball.” You get more of those when you’re not desperate.

So this is the upgrade. Not a trade. Not a free agent off the street with a medical question mark. A second-round pick, a first-team All-American, a twelve-touchdown season, already in the building, already practicing, and the paperwork is due in seven days.

Plus a genuinely lovely story: Irv Charles is expected to be elevated from the practice squad for his Seattle debut tonight. Think about this man’s road. He tore his ACL in December of 2024, missed the last four games of that season and all of 2025. Seattle traded for him from the Jets in May. He started this season on injured reserve. He has never played a single snap for the Seahawks. And tonight, in Denver, on a Thursday, he’s expected to run down the field on a punt team for the first time in a Seattle uniform. With the Jets he put up 450 special-teams snaps, fourteen tackles and a blocked punt.

That’s twenty-two months between football games. Stay ready.

I know “we’re about to activate a guy off the PUP list” doesn’t sound like a blockbuster. So let me put it in the language it deserves.

Seattle is seven days away from adding a second-round pick who was a first-team All-American, who ran for 1,359 yards at seven yards a carry in college, who has twenty-one rushing touchdowns in three NFL seasons, who scored twelve times last year without starting a single game, and who has a Super Bowl ring — to an offense currently ranked fourth-worst in football.

He costs nothing. No pick, no cap space, no phone call to another general manager. He’s been here the whole time, doing team reps, with his coach saying “he looks pretty dang good” about the cutting and the change of direction — the exact thing an ACL takes from you.

October twenty-second. Activate him, or lose him until next September.
