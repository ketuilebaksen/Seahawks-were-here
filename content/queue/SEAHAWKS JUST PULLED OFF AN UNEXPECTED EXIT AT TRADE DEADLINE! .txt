“An unexpected exit at the trade deadline.”

Seahawks Nation, I’m going to be straight with you in the first fifteen seconds, like I always am: Seattle did not trade anybody.

What actually happened on November tenth — right after the deadline closed — is that the Seahawks released a rookie defensive tackle off the practice squad and signed a different one. And the man they let go spent the start of this season with the Las Vegas Raiders.

The team Seattle plays on Sunday.

So no, it wasn’t a blockbuster. But the timing is strange enough that it deserves an explanation, and I’ve got it for you.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four after a seventeen to three beatdown of Arizona. Las Vegas is Sunday. And I’m going to hand out five winners from that game, address one very popular “loser” take that I think is flat wrong — and I’ve got the numbers to prove it — and then break down this roster move. Let’s go.

First, let’s appreciate what Sunday actually was.

Seventeen to three. No Sam Darnold. No Charles Cross. No Quenton Nelson. No Leonard Williams. No Rashid Shaheed. No Uchenna Nwosu.

Twelves, Seattle had every excuse in the world to lose that football game. The drop-off from Leonard Williams — a man who was about to miss his first game since Week 4 of 2024 — is enormous. If Arizona was ever going to break through, this was the week.

Instead: seventeen to three. Clean. Tough. No panic.

And one quick correction on something I keep hearing: people love to say Arizona hasn’t beaten Seattle “since the moon landing.” That’s a fun line, but here’s the real number — Seattle has now won the last ELEVEN straight meetings in this series. Eleven. That’s the actual stat and it’s impressive enough on its own.

Now — the five winners.

WINNER NUMBER ONE: Drew Lock. And it’s not close.

Nineteen of twenty-eight. One hundred seventy-five yards. A touchdown. Plus five carries for sixteen yards. And zero turnovers.

Now add the context, because the context is the whole story.

Drew Lock threw roughly TWENTY footballs all week. We know that from two separate sources — Lock said he only threw on Thursday, and a reporter put the number at twenty. A healthy starter throws well over a hundred and fifty in a normal week.

He did it on a bad elbow. And then, in the third quarter, on a designed keeper, he hurt his oblique — and finished the game anyway.

His own description of the tent visit is my favorite thing: “They said let me see how it feels throwing the ball. I was like, ‘Wait — I’ve already thrown the ball. You’re going to be all right.’”

And look at what he actually did out there, because this is the part people are underselling.

He wasn’t explosive. One hundred seventy-five yards is not a big day. But he did not turn the football over. And that’s now three straight games without a turnover for him.

Lock explained his own philosophy perfectly: “knowing that we can control the game with the run, operate on third downs well, keep the ball in our hands, allow our defense to have long fields to defend — not giving the team the ball on the thirty or the twenty.”

He didn’t try to win the game by himself. He let the team win it. And Mike Macdonald’s verdict: “he’s a beast.”

Nineteen of twenty-eight on twenty practice throws with two injuries. Respect. That’s all there is to say.

WINNER NUMBER TWO: Nick Emmanwori — and this is a bounce-back story.

He had a rough Week 8 against Chicago. Then on Sunday he came back as the difference-maker on the single biggest defensive play of the game.

Fourth quarter. Seattle up seventeen to three. Arizona at the goal line. And Nick Emmanwori breaks up a pass intended for Trey McBride on fourth down.

Twelves, think about what a touchdown there does. Seventeen to ten. One score game. A stadium that gets nervous. A defense that’s been on the field all day having to go hold a lead.

Emmanwori erased all of it with one play.

And here’s the coaching detail that almost nobody has mentioned. Macdonald revealed this after the game: “We didn’t rush Nick as much this week. Different reasons for why we did that.”

That’s a scheme change. Emmanwori has been used as a blitzer — a six-foot-three, 227-pound safety coming off the edge. And this week Seattle took that away and assigned him to cover Trey McBride instead.

McBride is one of the two or three best tight ends in football. And Macdonald’s words were: “we put him on McBride and felt confidently he could cover him. And he did.”

He was on McBride on third down after third down and the ball never went there. And when it finally did, on fourth down, he won.

That’s not luck. That’s a game plan built around one rookie safety’s ability, and it worked exactly as designed.

And Macdonald’s praise was about preparation, not talent: “he really had a good week of focusing in on details. I’m glad he had the production to back that up.”

WINNER NUMBER THREE: Dante Fowler Junior. Finally.

He’s done very little this season. But with Uchenna Nwosu out again, Seattle needed him — and he delivered a key sack in the first half.

Macdonald named him in his opening statement, unprompted, as one of the men who stepped in and didn’t flinch.

And twelves, here’s why this matters more than one sack.

Look at Seattle’s edge situation right now. Uchenna Nwosu out with an ankle. Derick Hall added to the injury report with a back problem last week. Connor O’Toole questionable with a foot.

That room is thin. And Seattle quietly re-signed outside linebacker Jamie Sheriff to the practice squad the day before Derick Hall showed up on the report.

What Seattle needs is a real rotation at edge rusher — not two good players and a hobbled third one. If Dante Fowler Junior can be a reliable piece of that, it changes the back half of this season.

One sack doesn’t prove it. But it’s the first evidence we’ve had.

WINNER NUMBER FOUR: the offensive coordinator — and this one comes with a condition.

Klint Kubiak called a better game on Sunday. More running. Move the chains. Nothing cute.

And twelves, I want to be fair about why, because it isn’t just “he called better plays.”

He had tools he hasn’t had all year. Zach Charbonnet, healthy, with games under his belt. Jadarian Price, with his snap restrictions lessened. Those two had played together exactly twice all season before Sunday.

And he had a quarterback who had thrown twenty footballs in a week. You cannot build a complicated passing attack on twenty throws. You have to simplify — and simplifying was the correct answer.

But I’ll also say the honest part: the slow-start problem is still real. Macdonald himself refused to let it go, even in a win: “we want to be on our game to START games, for sure. So we’ll look at that and try to come up with a solution.”

He’s not a finished product. But Sunday was the best game he’s called in a month, and the four-minute drive to close it out was the proof.

WINNER NUMBER FIVE: AJ Barner. And he was literally the difference in the game.

Two first downs on the push and a touchdown. The sneak worked every single time Seattle used it. And then he made a magnificent catch at the goal line to score.

Twelves, here’s the strategic reason this matters so much, and Mike Macdonald spelled it out himself.

His words: “to put the ball away in the red zone WHEN THEY TAKE JAX AWAY — to have another option down there is really helpful.”

“When they take Jax away.”

Jaxon Smith-Njigba leads the entire NFL in target share, and he just crossed a thousand receiving yards in nine games. Every single defense Seattle plays from here on is going to roll coverage to him in the red zone. It’s the obvious plan.

Arizona did exactly that. And AJ Barner caught two touchdowns.

While playing through a hand injury.

A national analyst called this offense a “one-man band” last week. His exact game plan was “limit Jaxon Smith-Njigba and we have a chance.” Arizona limited him in the red zone — and lost by fourteen, because the tight end beat them twice.

And Macdonald on the sneak: “to get into some of those short-yardage situations where we can run the sneak is kind of galvanizing — energizes the group.”

That’s the word. A short-yardage package that converts every time doesn’t just move the ball. It changes how an offense feels about itself.

NOW — the “loser,” and twelves, this is where I have to disagree with a very popular take.

The argument going around is that the loser of this game was Sam Darnold, who didn’t even play. And the stat being used is that Seattle is three and oh when the backup takes most of the reps, and one and five with Darnold.

I checked that. And it doesn’t hold up.

Here’s Seattle’s actual season. Week 1, beat New England thirteen to ten. Week 2, Drew Lock starts at Arizona and wins thirty-one to seven. Week 3, lost at Washington thirty-three to thirty-one. Week 4, beat the Chargers thirty to twenty-three. Week 5, lost to San Francisco thirty-two to twenty-nine. Week 6, lost at Denver seventeen to fourteen. Week 7, beat Kansas City twenty to three. Week 8, lost to Chicago thirty-four to seventeen. Week 9, Drew Lock starts and beats Arizona seventeen to three.

That’s nine games. Drew Lock has started two of them and won both. Sam Darnold has started seven and gone three and four.

Two and oh versus three and four. Not three and oh versus one and five. Those numbers don’t exist — there aren’t enough games in the season for them.

And twelves, even the real numbers don’t say what people want them to say, and I want to explain why.

Drew Lock’s two wins are against Arizona. Both of them. A three-and-five Arizona team, twice.

Sam Darnold’s seven games include San Francisco, Washington, Denver, Chicago, and a twenty to three dismantling of the Kansas City Chiefs.

That is not the same schedule. Not remotely.

Now — am I going to pretend Darnold’s season has been good? No. I’ve said this on this channel before and I’ll say it again: eight touchdowns and eight interceptions with an eighty passer rating is not good enough. One-to-one touchdown-to-interception from the man who’s supposed to be the stabilizing adult in that offense is a real problem and I’m not sugarcoating it.

But here’s the part I keep coming back to, and it’s not sentimental — it’s evidence.

Sam Darnold cracked a rib on the second play of a Monday night football game and played all four quarters without telling his own head coach. Mike Macdonald didn’t find out until after.

And then this week, with his own job being filled by somebody else, Sam Darnold spent the week helping Drew Lock get ready.

That’s not me guessing. Cooper Kupp volunteered it, completely unprompted, in the middle of an answer about somebody else: “Sam was awesome this week for Drew. I’m sure Drew will mention that.”

And here’s the circle that makes it beautiful. Last January, Sam Darnold couldn’t practice in the playoffs — and Drew Lock took the practice reps for him. Lock said it himself this week: “I was practicing for him.” And when Lock built his own twenty-throw plan this week, he said it was “basically the same template” with “a three-minute tweak.”

They have now done this for each other, in both directions, in the same calendar year.

Is there a quarterback controversy? No. Not yet, and not from inside that building. Mike Macdonald has volunteered something flattering about Sam Darnold in three consecutive press conferences, including the one right after Drew Lock won this game, where he said: “both quarterbacks that have played — I don’t know how you get any tougher. BOTH guys tough as nails.”

Nobody asked him about Sam. He brought him up anyway. Coaches don’t do that by accident.

Will the noise get louder if Lock beats the Raiders? Of course it will. I understand why. But the people making that argument should at least use real numbers — and the real numbers are two and oh against Arizona twice.

NOW — the roster move everybody’s asking about.

On November tenth, right after the trade deadline closed, Seattle released rookie defensive tackle Gary Smith the Third from the practice squad.

Here’s who he is. An undrafted rookie out of UCLA, twenty-five years old. He signed with the Las Vegas Raiders after the 2026 draft, got released in roster cuts, landed on the Raiders practice squad — and Seattle brought him in on September twenty-ninth. He never played a regular season game for the Seahawks.

And the résumé was legitimately interesting. Last year at UCLA he earned honorable mention All-Big Ten, started all twelve games, and put up forty tackles, a tackle and a half for loss, and half a sack. Real size, real ability to occupy space in the middle. A genuine developmental piece.

So why cut him?

Because Seattle brought in a different young defensive lineman: Cole Brevard. Six foot three, three hundred forty-six pounds. Undrafted out of Texas, time with the Chiefs, and before that Penn State and Purdue. His best season was Purdue in 2024 — honorable mention All-Big Ten, nineteen tackles, five and a half tackles for loss, and a sack and a half.

And here’s the thing people are raising eyebrows about: Gary Smith the Third was with the Raiders earlier this year. Seattle plays the Raiders on Sunday. And Seattle cut him days before the game.

So — did the matchup have anything to do with it?

Twelves, I’m going to be honest with you instead of entertaining: nobody knows, and there is no official indication that the Raiders game was the reason. I’m not going to invent a conspiracy for views. That’s not what this channel does.

But I’ll tell you why I understand the suspicion, because there’s a real rule underneath it.

A player on your practice squad knows things. He has sat in your meeting rooms. He’s heard the calls, seen the installs, run the scout team. And the NFL actually has a rule acknowledging exactly this problem — a practice squad player can’t sign with his own team’s next opponent after a certain point in the week, specifically because teams worry about information walking out the door.

So the instinct isn’t crazy. It just points the wrong direction. Because here’s the thing: if you were worried about a player taking your information to the Raiders, cutting him days before you play the Raiders is the WORST possible move. You’d keep him through Sunday. Cutting him sets him free at the exact moment he’s most useful to them.

So no — I don’t think that’s what this is. Here’s what I think it is, and it’s much simpler.

Look at the numbers side by side. Gary Smith the Third: forty tackles, one and a half for loss, half a sack. Cole Brevard: nineteen tackles — but FIVE AND A HALF for loss and a sack and a half.

And three hundred forty-six pounds.

That’s a completely different player profile. Smith was a space-eater. Brevard is a space-eater who also gets into the backfield. And given that Seattle just played a game without Leonard Williams for the first time since 2024, and had Brandon Pili out with a back injury on top of it — a three-hundred-forty-six pound body who can disrupt is exactly the shape of the problem Seattle has right now.

This is a team evaluating its defensive line depth in the middle of an injury crisis. That’s all. And honestly? I like that the staff isn’t sitting still.

And to Gary Smith the Third — genuinely, I wish him the best. Undrafted, waived, practice squad, waived again. That’s a brutal way to live. I hope somebody gives him another shot.

Now let me put the whole week together, because the picture is clearer than people think.

On one side: Seattle beat Arizona seventeen to three with six starters out. Drew Lock played through two injuries on twenty practice throws and didn’t turn it over. Nick Emmanwori bounced back and saved a goal-line touchdown. Dante Fowler Junior finally showed up. The offense simplified and closed the game out with a four-minute drive from its own one yard line. And AJ Barner was the difference, twice, with a bad hand.

On the other side: this team is still banged up, still making roster moves out of necessity, and still doesn’t know who its quarterback is on Sunday.

Because that’s the real headline nobody’s leading with. Mike Macdonald said it plainly after the game: “I’m assuming we’re probably going to have to make a decision on who we’re going to get ready. Drew’s situation, we’ll have to evaluate that as well. It seems like it’s kind of up in the air right now with Drew.”

Sam Darnold re-evaluated on a cracked rib. Drew Lock being checked for an oblique on top of the elbow. And Jalen Milroe, who just took his first live NFL snap in this game, standing behind both of them.

And here’s the thing I keep thinking about: you cannot run a twenty-throw week for two quarterbacks at the same time. Somebody gets the practice reps. That IS the decision.

Then it’s at the Las Vegas Raiders on Sunday, and then the bye in Week 11 — which, by the way, is the key to Quenton Nelson’s entire timeline after his knee procedure. Macdonald said it himself: “at least this game and X amount of practices, and then going into the bye.”

Big picture, twelves? Seattle is playing to stay relevant and to get healthy. In that order. The bye week might be the most valuable thing on this schedule.
