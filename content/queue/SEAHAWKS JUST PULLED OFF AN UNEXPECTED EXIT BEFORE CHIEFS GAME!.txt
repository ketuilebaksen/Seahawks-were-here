One of the three running backs in this script is not a trade target anymore.

Najee Harris was released by the New York Giants yesterday.

Seahawks Nation, read that again, because it changes the entire conversation. You do not need a draft pick for Najee Harris. You do not need to call another general manager. He is a free agent. Today. Right now. All you need is a phone number and a contract.

And the detail that makes this a Seattle story: the Seahawks hosted Najee Harris for a visit last offseason, before he signed with New York. They already brought him in the building. They already looked him in the eye.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. And today’s video is about exits — three of them, all real, all in the last seventy-two hours — and about the single most useful thing a football fan can understand, which is that “we need a running back” is not one question. It’s three questions, and they cost completely different things.

Let’s go.

First, the title, because it’s close and I want to be precise.

“Unexpected exit” — absolutely true. There have been several. But Seattle didn’t pull off the Harris one. The Giants did that. Seattle’s exits were two practice squad releases on Monday, plus a tight end headed for injured reserve.

So let me just give you the honest accounting of who left, who did it, and what it means — because all three exits matter and two of them nobody is covering properly.

Exit number one: Najee Harris, and it got ugly fast.

Harris started for the Giants in Week 6 against the Saints. And in that start he had four carries for two yards in a 34-27 loss.

Four carries. Two yards. And he was released on Tuesday, October twentieth — days after starting.

His full 2026 with New York: 31 carries for 99 yards. That’s about 3.2 yards a carry.

And the reaction around the league was basically bewilderment, because the Giants had benched second-year back Cam Skattebo — who played six offensive snaps and got zero carries in that same game — to start Harris, and then cut Harris. One fan summary that got passed around said the Giants’ backfield decisions “just get uglier,” and honestly, that’s fair.

Giants head coach John Harbaugh on the running back room: “I do understand it, respect it. Also understand that that’s earned.” That’s a coach saying nobody in that room had earned the job.

And Harris himself, on what sporadic carries do to a running back, which I thought was genuinely insightful: “The first couple runs will be like, ‘OK, how are they playing it?’”

That’s a real thing, by the way, and it’s worth understanding. A running back’s first few carries are reconnaissance. He’s reading how the defense is fitting the run — are the linebackers scraping over the top, is the safety coming down, is the backside end chasing or sitting. By carry eight or ten he knows where the game is. Give a man four carries and you’ve only let him ask the question. You never let him answer it.

So: should Seattle sign Najee Harris? Let me give you both sides straight.

The case for: He costs nothing but money — no draft pick, no trade call, no cap gymnastics beyond a veteran minimum-type deal. He’s a genuine bruiser, famously durable, an Alabama workhorse built for third-and-two. And Seattle already liked him enough to host a visit, which means the evaluation work is done and somebody in that building already said yes once.

The case against, and it’s the stronger one: He has never been an efficient ball carrier, he didn’t live up to the hype coming out of Alabama, and 3.2 yards a carry is not a fix for a run game ranked near the bottom of the league. Seattle’s problem is not a shortage of bodies — it’s yards per carry. Adding a back averaging 3.2 to solve an efficiency problem is bringing a bucket to a leak.

My honest read: he’s a Tuesday phone call if Charbonnet’s knee doesn’t hold, and nothing more than that. A depth signing, not a solution. And I’d make that call — because free is free, and you cannot have too many healthy bodies in a backfield that’s lost two men to injury.

Now here’s the part I actually wanted to make this video for. Three names, three doors, three completely different prices. Learn this and you’ll never be confused by a trade rumor again.

Door one: Najee Harris. Free agency. Cost: money only. No pick, no permission, no deadline. This is the cheapest door in professional football and it’s open right now. The trade-off is you’re shopping in the bin where other teams already decided no.

Door two: Tony Pollard. And this is the textbook example of a tradeable contract — I want to spend real time here.

Pollard is in the final year of a three-year, twenty-one point seven five million dollar deal, with a six point seven four million dollar base salary this season. And here’s the part that matters: Tennessee would save about seven point two five million dollars by trading him and carry only about two million in dead money.

Now compare that to the Saquon Barkley fantasy everybody spent two weeks on. Philadelphia trading Barkley saves about one million dollars and absorbs roughly twenty-six million in dead cap.

Look at those two side by side. Pollard: seller saves seven million, eats two. Barkley: seller saves one million, eats twenty-six.

That is the difference between a trade that can happen and a trade that cannot. And it has nothing to do with how good the players are. It’s entirely about when the money was paid. Barkley’s money was paid up front as signing bonus years ago, and all of that remaining proration slams onto Philadelphia’s cap the moment he’s traded — and it does not travel with him. Pollard’s money is mostly base salary in the current year, which simply walks out the door with the player.

Base salary travels. Signing bonus doesn’t. That’s the whole rule. Write it down and you’ll be ahead of ninety percent of the internet every November.

And Pollard is good, not just convenient: 349 rushing yards on 87 carries, zero fumbles, eight catches, and a PFF grade of 73.0 — thirteenth among forty-five qualifying running backs. Tennessee is 1-5.

So: a 1-5 team, a productive back, an expiring contract, a seller that saves money. That is the most logical trade in football.

And it’s probably not happening — because the latest reporting says Tennessee isn’t planning to trade Tony Pollard. Which is the final lesson in this section: a perfect contract still needs a willing seller. You can do all the math right and still get told no.

Door three: Kenneth Gainwell. Tampa Bay. He’s buried behind Bucky Irving and has 17 carries for 44 yards in five games this season. Kristopher Knox of Bleacher Report named Seattle and Philadelphia as potential suitors — so credit to this script for sourcing it rather than inventing it.

He’s a real NFL pass protector and a third-down specialist, which has value. But 17 carries for 44 yards is not a player who unlocks an offense. That’s a cheap depth trade, and it should cost almost nothing.

So there’s your map: free agency, an expiring contract, and a buried backup. Cash, picks, and a roster spot. Three currencies. Most people arguing about this online don’t know which one they’re spending.

Exit number two: Elijah Arroyo. And I have a number that reframes this completely.

ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler reported Monday that Arroyo is “most likely” headed to injured reserve. He hurt his shoulder against Denver on October fifteenth and was assisted off the field. He’s ruled out for at least the next four games.

Now here’s the number nobody has put on screen, and I had to go look it up myself.

Elijah Arroyo has one catch, on two targets, for 17 yards. In six games. All season.

One catch. All year.

So let’s be honest about what Seattle is actually losing, because “starting tight end goes on IR” sounds like a catastrophe and the receiving production says otherwise. For context, as a rookie last year — a second-round pick, fiftieth overall, out of Miami — he had 15 catches for 179 yards and a touchdown in 13 games.

What Seattle loses isn’t his hands. It’s a personnel group.

The one structural thing Brian Fleury installed this year was two tight ends on the field together — Arroyo and A.J. Barner. And the reason you want that isn’t catches. It’s that two tight ends force a linebacker to honor the run and a safety to honor the seam at the same time, and that conflict is exactly what loosens the double-team on Jaxon Smith-Njigba. Denver took that away and held JSN to two catches for eleven yards in the first half.

That is precisely why Josiah Deguara is here. Seattle is not replacing Arroyo’s production. It’s replacing his job.

Exit number three: Dontae McMillan and Malick Meiga, released from the practice squad on Monday.

And McMillan deserves a sentence, because he’s from here. Chief Sealth International High School in West Seattle. 2,019 yards and 44 touchdowns as a senior. Fifty career college games, 493 carries, 2,785 rushing yards, 20 touchdowns, and in 2025 he started all twelve games with five separate hundred-yard games. He played with Rashid Shaheed at Weber State.

He got squeezed because Emanuel Wilson and George Holani are ahead of him and Charbonnet is coming back. That’s the business, and this script was right to say so plainly. I didn’t enjoy that one either.

Now the arrivals — and this script found a detail I genuinely loved, so let me give it full credit.

Jackson Acker. Undrafted rookie fullback out of Wisconsin. He originally signed with Buffalo after the draft and was released in August, right after their first preseason game. At Wisconsin: 50 games, 14 starts, playing both fullback and running back while contributing on special teams. Two-time Academic All-Big Ten.

And then the detail: in high school he was a state champion in both the discus and the shot put.

That is not trivia. Discus and shot put are rotational power events. You win them by generating force from your hips and transferring it through a braced core in a fraction of a second. That is exactly the athletic trait a lead-blocking fullback needs — the ability to put a moving linebacker on his back from a standstill. Whoever scouted that noticed it. Great catch by this script, and I’d never have gone looking for it.

Josiah Deguara. A third-round pick out of Cincinnati in 2020, now twenty-nine, a seven-year veteran with 78 games and 12 starts, 53 catches for 471 yards and three touchdowns for his career, and one appearance with Philadelphia this season before they released him after Week 5. He’s not a receiving weapon. He’s a hybrid blocker who can line up in the backfield.

And one mechanic that decides whether either man plays Sunday: they’re both on the practice squad, not the fifty-three. To get on the field they must be signed to the active roster or elevated — and the rules are hard: two elevations per game, three per player per season. Seattle makes that call Saturday, and it’ll tell you exactly what Macdonald thinks he needs.

Now the numbers behind all of this, and then the thing that fixes it tomorrow.

This script’s run-game figures check out as reported, and they’re brutal. Per Hawk Blogger: Seattle is 24th in rushing success rate at 37.7 percent and 29th in rush EPA per play at negative point one eight five.

Twenty-ninth. Out of thirty-two. For the defending Super Bowl champion.

And the cheapest door of all four opens tomorrow.

Zach Charbonnet’s deadline is October twenty-second. Tomorrow. His twenty-one-day practice window opened October first, and when it closes there are two outcomes: on the fifty-three-man roster, or back on PUP for the rest of the season.

Macdonald on Monday: “Zach is going to have a full week’s worth of reps, so that’s exciting. And there’s a possibility that you could see him on Sunday in some capacity.” He’s called a Week 7 debut “a realistic target,” and Gregg Bell of Seattle Sports has reported Charbonnet would likely take a large share of the carries once he plays.

He scored twelve rushing touchdowns last season with zero starts. Twenty-one career rushing touchdowns. And he costs no pick, no cap space, and no phone call.

And the other free fix: Anthony Bradford. Designated to return Monday after five games on injured reserve with knee and hip injuries. And Macdonald, breaking his own habit: “I know normally I’m pretty coy on this one, but it’s pretty straightforward. If he has a good week, he’s going to play a significant amount, if not the whole game.”

Why that matters more than any name on the trade board: Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks in Denver and went 6-of-19 for 47 yards under pressure. He’s taken fourteen sacks this year — five against Denver, five the week before against San Francisco. In Bradford’s place, Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens both allowed multiple pressures.

You don’t fix 29th in rush EPA by buying a runner. You fix it by blocking. A back averaging 3.2 a carry behind a broken right side is still a back averaging 3.2 a carry.

And now let me address the thing this script opens with, because it deserves a real answer and not a shrug: “we let Kenneth Walker walk.”

That’s true, and the frustration is completely earned. Walker has 537 rushing yards in four games in Kansas City — more than Seattle’s entire team has in five — averaging 134.3 a game, the highest in the NFL. He was the MVP of Seattle’s Super Bowl. Watching that is genuinely painful and nobody should pretend it isn’t.

But I’m going to give you the part that makes it a decision instead of a blunder.

Winning a championship makes your roster more expensive. Every man who starred in that run came out of it with a stronger case for more money, and the salary cap does not grow because you won. So John Schneider had to choose.

Where did the money go? Leonard Williams: three years, ninety million dollars, fifty-six million guaranteed — at thirty-two years old. Devon Witherspoon: four years, a hundred and thirty-two million. Plus Sam Darnold’s deal. He protected the front seven, the secondary and the quarterback.

And the bill came due at running back. Seattle has roughly twelve point eight million dollars in cap space right now. That’s what’s left.

So here’s the honest framing, and you can disagree with it — I go back and forth myself. Seattle bet that a defense is harder to replace than a running back. And in a vacuum that bet is defensible; it’s the bet most smart front offices make, because running back production is the most line-dependent production in football.

What makes it look bad right now isn’t the logic. It’s the timing. Seattle let Walker go and then lost Charbonnet’s knee in the playoffs, and then lost Jadarian Price to IR, and then lost Anthony Bradford for five games. The plan assumed one of those three things, not all three.

That’s the fair verdict: a defensible choice that got hit by three injuries it couldn’t have budgeted for. And it’s why the answer this week is Charbonnet and Bradford — the two pieces the plan was actually built on — rather than a name off a trade board.

Let me close.

Three exits in seventy-two hours. Najee Harris out of New York — which turned a trade target into a free agent overnight. Elijah Arroyo out for at least four games — a man with one catch all season whose real value was a formation. And two practice squad players out, including a local kid, to make room for a fullback and a blocking tight end.

And underneath all of it, the same question Seattle has been asking since September: who blocks, and who runs?

The answer might cost a draft pick in November. Or it might cost a signature tomorrow. Charbonnet’s deadline is twenty-four hours away, Bradford might play sixty minutes on Sunday, and both of them are already in the building.

Macdonald said it Monday and it’s still the right note: “I’m excited about where we can go, honestly.” Followed immediately by: “We’ve really had one good day’s worth of work up to this point.”

Go through, not around.

Twelves, two questions. First, and I want a real answer: do you sign Najee Harris? He’s free, he’s a bruiser, Seattle already hosted him once — and he’s averaging 3.2 yards a carry. Free depth, or a distraction from the actual problem? And second: of the three doors — a free agent, Tony Pollard’s expiring contract, or Kenneth Gainwell for almost nothing — which one would you actually spend on, knowing Tennessee says Pollard isn’t available? Tell me, because I’d take the free one and save the pick, and I’m not certain I’m right.
