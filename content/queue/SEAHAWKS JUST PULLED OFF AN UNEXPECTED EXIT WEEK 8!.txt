Twelves, this title is right. Somebody did just exit Seattle unexpectedly — and it probably isn’t who you think.

Christian Haynes is a Miami Dolphin.

A 2024 third-round pick. The eighty-first overall selection. A man who played about 390 offensive snaps and made five starts at guard for the Seattle Seahawks this season.

Seattle waived him on Tuesday. The Dolphins claimed him on Wednesday. He was gone in about twenty-four hours.

And here’s the part that’s going to surprise you: two teams claimed him. Baltimore put in for him too. Adam Schefter reported that Miami had the higher claim and was awarded him — and Miami released a 2026 sixth-round guard, DJ Campbell, to make room.

Two NFL teams wanted Christian Haynes badly enough to take him onto their active roster within a day of Seattle letting him go.

First: why did Christian Haynes leave?

Because Quenton Nelson arrived.

Seattle traded a 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick to Indianapolis for Nelson — an eight-time Pro Bowler and six-time All-Pro that Mike Macdonald called “a surefire Hall of Fame guard.”

And you cannot add a fifty-fourth man to a fifty-three-man roster.

So somebody had to go, and the front office looked at its interior offensive line and picked the man with the fewest starts. That was Christian Haynes. Six seasons at UConn, drafted in the third round, active every game as a rookie without starting one, and this year he finally got on the field — five starts, three hundred and ninety snaps, and not a single penalty since his rookie season.

He did nothing wrong. He was just fifth in line in a room that suddenly had a Hall of Famer in it.

And twelves, I want to pause on him for a second, because this franchise once had real plans for Christian Haynes.

Seattle took him with the eighty-first pick in 2024 specifically to fix the guard position. When they drafted him, John Schneider and Mike Macdonald described him as “classy and nasty” — which is about the highest compliment this front office hands out to an offensive lineman. He was supposed to be the answer to exactly the problem that Quenton Nelson just got traded in to solve.

It took him two years to get on the field. He finally did it this season — five starts, 390 snaps, zero penalties since his rookie year. And the week he was finally part of a functioning offensive line, the front office went out and bought a better one.

That’s not cruelty. That’s a team that won a Super Bowl and refuses to wait on development when a six-time All-Pro is available for a third-round pick. But I’m not going to pretend it’s painless either. Somebody’s plan ended this week.

Now let me explain how waivers actually work, because this is the part nobody teaches and it’s genuinely fascinating.

There’s a difference between being “released” and being “waived,” and it depends on how long you’ve been in the league.

A veteran with four or more accrued seasons who gets cut is released — he’s a free agent immediately, and he can sign anywhere he wants, including back where he came from.

A player with fewer than four accrued seasons gets waived. And waived does not mean free. It means he goes on a league-wide list for about twenty-four hours, and every other team gets a chance to claim him.

And the order is the beautiful part: waiver priority runs in reverse order of record. The worst team in football gets first call.

So when both Baltimore and Miami put in a claim for Christian Haynes, the tiebreaker wasn’t who wanted him more. It was who had the worse record. Miami won. Christian Haynes is a Dolphin because Miami has lost more football games than Baltimore has.

That’s the system. It is designed so that the teams who need help most get the first shot at the men who get squeezed out by teams who don’t.

And here’s the crucial part for Seattle fans: because he was claimed, Seattle could not put him on the practice squad. When a man clears waivers unclaimed, you can bring him back. When he’s claimed, he’s gone. No safety net, no second phone call.

Christian Haynes did not get the Montorie Foster treatment. He got the exit.

And there’s one more wrinkle worth knowing, because it’s about to matter a lot.

The trade deadline is November tenth. After that date, you cannot trade for a player at all. No deals, no phone calls, nothing — the market is closed until the offseason.

So what happens if a contender loses a starter in week twelve? The waiver wire. That’s it.

After November tenth, the waiver wire becomes the entire player market — and the priority order still runs worst-record-first, which means a 4-3 defending champion is at the back of the line all the way to January. Seattle will be sitting somewhere around twentieth or worse in the claim order while a 2-9 team gets first look at every man who gets cut.

That’s why this week mattered so much. Seattle did its shopping with ten days to spare, because in two weeks the only way to add a player is to hope twenty teams in front of you pass on him.

And a last note on the vocabulary, because the difference decides whether you lose a man or keep him. A player with four or more accrued seasons is released and becomes a free agent instantly — you can re-sign him to your own practice squad if he’s willing. A player with fewer than four goes through that twenty-four-hour claim window first. Christian Haynes, drafted in 2024, had three. He was eighteen months of service time away from Seattle being able to keep him.

Eighteen months. That’s the whole difference between “see you at practice tomorrow” and “welcome to Miami.”

Now let me give you the full list, because the Nelson trade cost seven departures and almost nobody has counted them.

Exit one: Christian Haynes. Waived, claimed by Miami, gone.

Exit two: Jackson Acker. The fullback was released from the practice squad on Tuesday.

Exit three: Josh Ross. The linebacker was released from the practice squad the same day.

Those two spots went to receivers Montorie Foster Jr. and Malick Meiga — which brings us to:

Exit four: Montorie Foster Jr. himself, nine days ago. The undrafted camp standout who made the initial 53-man roster was waived last Saturday to open the spot for Anthony Bradford’s activation from injured reserve. He’s back now, on the practice squad. But he left the 53, and he isn’t on it anymore.

Exit five: Charles Cross — out of the lineup. The starting left tackle hurt his ankle against the Chiefs and will not play Monday night. Josh Jones starts at left tackle. Macdonald: “Josh is our third tackle.” And the important part: “As of right now, he’s not going on injured reserve.” IR is a minimum of four games. They didn’t do it, which tells you they expect him back sooner.

Exit six: Grey Zabel — out of his position. He has started all twenty-four games of his career at left guard. On Monday night he plays right guard for the first time as a professional. Macdonald: “He’s had a lot of experience playing on the right side, so that’ll help with the learning curve.” He played all five line spots at North Dakota State.

And exit seven, the one that hurts: Anthony Bradford — out of the starting lineup.

Twelves, you know how I feel about this one. Anthony Bradford has forty career starts at right guard. He came back from a knee injury, started against Kansas City, and Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards and allowed zero sacks. On Wednesday Macdonald volunteered, unprompted, that “our offensive line probably played their best game of the season this past Sunday night.”

Four days later, Bradford is the backup.

And his coach refused to dress it up: “It’s not an easy situation. I thought Anthony played really good football for us, and he’s earned a significant role in our team — a starting-level role. Really no way to sugarcoat it.” Plus: “We don’t expect him to be jumping for joy in this situation, because that’s not realistic.” And: “You’re a play away from him playing every play again.”

Seven exits. One arrival. Plus a 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick that walked out the door too.

That is what a Hall of Famer actually costs. Not just picks — people.

Now flip the ledger, because Indianapolis is having a much worse week than we are.

The Colts are 2-5 after three straight losses. They fired general manager Chris Ballard last week in his tenth season. Assistant GM Ed Dodds reportedly engineered this week’s trades, presumably at the direction of owner Carlie Irsay-Gordon.

And they didn’t just sell Quenton Nelson.

Exit: DeForest Buckner — traded to the San Francisco 49ers for a 2027 fourth-round pick.

And twelves, that’s a reunion. San Francisco drafted DeForest Buckner and traded him to Indianapolis back in 2020. Now he’s going home.

And I need you to understand that this one is a Seahawks problem.

San Francisco just acquired a Pro Bowl defensive tackle for a fourth-round pick. That’s the team sitting ahead of Seattle in the NFC West. We paid a third and a fifth for a guard; they paid a fourth for an interior defender who has spent his career wrecking offensive lines.

Seattle plays San Francisco again this season. Quenton Nelson may end up earning his entire trade value against DeForest Buckner. Nobody in these highlight videos is connecting that, and it’s the most important thing on the board.

Meanwhile Indianapolis is building a war chest. They have no 2027 first-round pick — that went to the Jets in the Sauce Gardner trade — but they now hold one second, two thirds and two fourths, and they’re projected to have well over sixty million dollars in cap space next spring. They’re also expected to entertain offers for Grover Stewart and Josh Downs.

That’s not a fire sale. That’s a demolition with a permit.

Which brings us to Jonathan Taylor — and twelves, the real reporting here is much more interesting than the proposal going around.

Let me give you the actual state of it, with names attached.

ESPN puts the odds of Indianapolis trading Jonathan Taylor before the deadline at about thirty percent.

Jeremy Fowler’s words: “Teams I’ve talked to aren’t totally sold that Indianapolis will trade Taylor despite heavy interest.”

“Aren’t totally sold.” That’s the opposite of a done deal.

Fowler and Matt Bowen ranked Taylor number one among twenty-five potential trade candidates, and named five possible destinations: Seattle, Washington, New Orleans, Denver and Minnesota. But — and this matters — those rankings measure the impact a player could have, not how likely a trade is.

And Adam Schefter reportedly understands that the owner does not want to trade him.

And then there’s the man himself, who nobody is quoting.

Jonathan Taylor has not requested a trade. His words: “I haven’t heard anything, and I can’t control that.” And: “I’m always proud to wear the horseshoe, proud to be here.”

Read that again. The player everybody is writing trade proposals about just said, publicly, that he wants to stay and hasn’t asked to leave. That’s not nothing. That’s a man telling you where he stands.

Now here’s the number that I think actually decides this, and it’s the mechanic I’ve been teaching you for weeks.

If Indianapolis trades Jonathan Taylor before the deadline, they eat about $17.6 million in dead money on their 2026 books.

Seventeen point six million dollars.

That’s the acceleration. Remember the rule: signing-bonus proration does not travel in a trade. The old team keeps it, and the moment the player is dealt, every remaining year of it slams onto their cap at once. Taylor signed a two-year, $44 million extension in August with $39 million guaranteed, running through 2028. That bonus money is Indianapolis’s problem forever, and trading him makes the bill come due immediately.

So Indianapolis would be paying $17.6 million for the privilege of not having their best offensive player.

That is the single strongest argument that this trade does not happen — and it explains Fowler’s reporting perfectly. Teams aren’t “totally sold” because the math makes the Colts hesitate.

Reporting says the asking price should start at a second-round pick.

Alex Kay at Bleacher Report has Seattle sending Emanuel Wilson, a 2027 second-round pick and a 2027 fourth-round pick to Indianapolis for Taylor. The man who wrote it called it a projection. It is a writer’s idea. It is not a report, not an offer, and not a deal.

The problem was the line. Seattle bought a line. The shopping is done.
