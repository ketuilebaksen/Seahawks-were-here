Last time I told you two outlets disagreed on Drew Lock’s line — one had him at nineteen of twenty-eight for a hundred seventy-five, another at fourteen of seventeen for two hundred forty.

And twelves, look at what fourteen of seventeen actually means.

That is EIGHTY-TWO PERCENT completions. Seventeen attempts. Two hundred forty yards.

That’s over FOURTEEN YARDS PER ATTEMPT.

Fourteen yards per attempt is not a good day. Fourteen yards per attempt is a historically efficient day. The league average is somewhere around seven. He doubled it.

And he did it having thrown a football on exactly ONE DAY of the practice week. For the second straight week. A reporter asked him point blank — “did you only throw on Thursday again?” — and he said: “Yeah.”

Eighty-two percent completions and fourteen yards an attempt on one day of throwing, with elbow tendinitis and a strained oblique.

NOW — the two fumbles, because that’s the other half and I’m not going to skip it.

He fumbled twice. Including on Seattle’s VERY FIRST POSSESSION.

And Mike Macdonald said it himself without being pushed: “we DID turn it over twice today, which — you know, WE WANT TO BE BETTER.”

That’s fair. But let me give you what we actually know about each one, because the details matter and nobody else is giving them to you.

On the first fumble, a national analyst’s read was: “that one, to me, is a quarterback who SHOULD HAVE JUST EATEN THE SACK. Take the sack, live to fight another down, and it is not a big deal.”

And twelves, that’s exactly right — and it violates Drew Lock’s own stated rule. His words two weeks ago: “we’re always trying to avoid playing HERO FOOTBALL, because that’s when bad things happen.”

He broke his own rule. Second and seventeen is survivable. A fumble on your opening drive is not.

On the second one, the read was: “that is more on ZACH CHARBONNET. By my read, about SIXTY-FORTY on him. It was a simple read play where the quarterback either hands it off or fires out a quick slant — and I THINK HE HAD A TOUCHDOWN THERE.”

So that’s a mesh-point miscommunication on a read play — and the analyst thinks Lock saw a touchdown and may have been pulling it when the exchange went wrong.

And Macdonald’s framing supports it: those turnovers were “REALLY EARLY IN THE PLAY.”

Early in the play. Operational, not reckless. Not a quarterback forcing a ball into traffic.

But twelves — two fumbles is two fumbles. And it ends a real streak: Drew Lock had gone four straight games without turning it over. That’s over now.

And here’s the thing I keep coming back to on this. Lock’s actual philosophy for how Seattle wins is the one that got violated: “knowing that we can control the game with the run, operate on third downs well, KEEP THE BALL IN OUR HANDS, allow our defense to have LONG FIELDS to defend.”

You can’t give a defense long fields if you put the ball on the ground on your own first possession.

NOW — Jaxon Smith-Njigba, and the headline number has arrived.

And twelves, the voices on this are now unanimous across the board.

Mike Macdonald: “he’s a BEAST,” and then listed every single phase — “run solutions, quick game, catch-and-runs, the intermediate and the NINE BALLS, and on extended plays he’s able to UNCOVER.”

And the best one, from Drew Lock, quoting the man himself: “Jax would tell you that IF HE’S DOUBLED, HE’S STILL OPEN. That’s kind of been the funny line this year.”

And the seventy-one yard touchdown — twelves, Lock told us exactly how that type of throw works, and it’s the best football lesson of this season:

“SEVEN’S HELMET — see the BACK of the helmet? That’s the CLASSIC. Just throw it AT the helmet, throw it OVER the helmet. HE WON’T BE ABLE TO SEE IT.”

If you can see the back of a defender’s helmet, his head is turned downfield. He cannot see the ball coming. So you drop it right over the top of him, and a man who can’t see the ball can’t play the ball.

And the receiver’s half: “Jax does a great job of LATE HANDS — flashing hands late, NOT LETTING HIM GET A HAND OUT.”

Hands down until the last instant, so the defender has no signal. By the time he reacts, it’s already caught.

NOW — Devon Witherspoon, and twelves, this one deserves its own section.

TWO SACKS. FROM A CORNERBACK.

And Seattle got to Fernando Mendoza SIX TIMES total.

And the line that makes it land: “this is why we made him the HIGHEST PAID CORNERBACK IN THE LEAGUE. Money well spent, my friends.”

Twelves, I want to tell you why those two sacks happened, because it’s the single most damning tactical story of this entire game.

A national analyst who played offensive line in this league said this: “for some reason, the Raiders DID NOT HAVE AN ANSWER for Devon Witherspoon blitzing off the edge ONCE THE WHOLE GAME. That’s a problem, because you KNOW the Seahawks do that a lot.”

NOT ONCE. All afternoon.

And then he explained what Las Vegas failed to do, and there were only two options:

“Whether you SLIDE THE PROTECTION to Spoon, or you at least ID HIM and Mendoza knows he needs to THROW HOT — the Raiders did NEITHER. THEY PAID THE PRICE FOR IT.”

Option one is mechanical: shift your five offensive linemen toward the blitz so somebody’s in his path.

Option two is “throwing hot” — and Drew Lock used that exact phrase describing his own job: going “through reads, through progressions, KNOWING YOU’RE HOT.” It means you accept that the blitzer comes free, and you have a predetermined quick throw that beats him. The blitz costs the defense a coverage defender, so you punish the space he vacated.

Las Vegas did neither thing. Three or four times in a single half.

And Macdonald told us exactly why he kept going back to it: “he was HOT. I think we called one earlier, he didn’t get there BUT THE BALL WAS OUT — BUT HE WON HIS RUSH. So we were just like, all right, LET’S KEEP SENDING HIM.”

And then: “what’s on the sheet that we can BRING 21?” And the credit: “that’s our COACHES’ credit. They SAW it, they ENCOURAGED ME to dial it up.”

Twelves, that’s the whole coaching philosophy in one sequence. He judged a blitz by whether his player BEAT HIS MAN, not by whether it produced a sack. The process was good even when the result was nothing. So he kept calling it — and it ended with two sacks and a game.

He told us on Wednesday that adjusting live was the plan. His own former coordinator had a good first-half script. Seattle fixed it at halftime and allowed twenty-seven yards.

Mike Macdonald told us before the Bears game that Charbonnet “hasn’t had a live rep SINCE THE INJURY” — not one, in eight months. NOW — THE ROSTER MOVE, and twelves, let me give you the full picture on this young man.

Seattle waived rookie defensive tackle Devin Eastern on Monday afternoon.

Here’s who he is. A SEVENTH-ROUND PICK in the 2026 draft, out of MINNESOTA. Twenty-three years old. SIX FOOT FIVE, THREE HUNDRED TWENTY POUNDS. A THREE-YEAR STARTER for the Golden Gophers who earned All-Big Ten honorable mention in 2025.

And his path has been brutal. Seattle waived him after the preseason to get to fifty-three. Kept him on the practice squad. He spent time on the physically unable to perform list when camp opened in July after missing part of the offseason program with an injury.

And then on NOVEMBER SEVENTH, Seattle signed him off the practice squad to the active roster — specifically because they needed bodies at defensive tackle with Leonard Williams and Brandon Pili both hurt.

He was on the fifty-three for two games. And he never played a single snap in either one.

And then on Monday, he was gone.

So — why? And twelves, the honest answer is that nobody has told us, so let me give you what I think and label it as my read.

First, the fair framing: “is this a huge shock? NOT REALLY. It’s a seventh-rounder who didn’t play. BUT THE TIMING is what makes me raise an eyebrow.”

That’s right on both counts. A seventh-round rookie who didn’t take a snap is the easiest cut on any roster. But doing it while your starting defensive tackle and your backup nose tackle are both hurt is odd.

And the two possibilities laid out were: “either the staff is FEELING BETTER ABOUT THE INJURIES, or they’re just KEEPING THE ROSTER FLEXIBLE DURING THE BYE.”

Twelves, I think it’s mostly the second one, and here’s why — because of what Mike Macdonald told us.

On Leonard Williams: “this is one of those things where you just have to — IT’S KIND OF NOT GOOD UNTIL IT IS. So, NOT GOOD YET.” And: “you’re able to move around and do all the things. It’s just — stingers, it’s MORE OF A CONTACT THING.” And: “he’s doing all the LEO THINGS. He’s just not playing right now.”

And asked if anybody’s going on injured reserve: “NOT TO MY KNOWLEDGE. No. No.”

So Leonard Williams is not on IR and is doing everything but hitting people. With a two-week bye in front of him. A stinger is a nerve injury — it’s binary. It’s bad, and then one day it isn’t. Two weeks of no contact is exactly the medicine.

And here’s the practical part: an open spot on the fifty-three during a bye week is the most valuable kind of flexibility there is. Seattle just lost Abe Lucas to an ankle — initial reports suggesting a HIGH ANKLE SPRAIN, though Macdonald said flatly “what degree, we’re not sure.” And George Holani went down with a knee.

If you need to add an offensive tackle, or a running back, or a healthy body anywhere — you need a spot. And a seventh-round defensive tackle who hasn’t played a snap is the spot you create.

And remember what Seattle did the week before this: they waived a different rookie defensive tackle, Gary Smith the Third, and brought in Cole Brevard — a three-hundred-forty-six pound body with five and a half tackles for loss in his best college season.

That’s two defensive tackle moves in two weeks. This staff is actively churning that position looking for the right profile. It’s not panic. It’s evaluation.

But twelves, I’ll say exactly what the honest version is: “the defensive line is NOT A PLACE WHERE I WANT US THIN.”

Agreed. And I’ll be watching that open spot all week.

And to Devin Eastern — genuinely, good luck. Drafted in the seventh round, waived at cuts, PUP list in July, practice squad, promoted, never played, waived again. That’s an unbelievably hard way to live, and he’s twenty-three years old with three years of Big Ten starts behind him. Somebody will give him another look.
