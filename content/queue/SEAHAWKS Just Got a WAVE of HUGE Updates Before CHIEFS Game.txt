Update one. Zach Charbonnet. October twenty-second. Four days from right now.

And I want to explain where that date comes from, because once you understand this mechanic you’ll be ahead of ninety percent of the people talking about it.

On October first, Seattle designated Zach Charbonnet to return to practice from the physically-unable-to-perform list. That designation does something very specific: it opens a twenty-one day practice window.

During those twenty-one days, Charbonnet can practice fully with the team without occupying a spot on the fifty-three-man roster. It’s a free look. The team gets three weeks to watch him move, cut, take contact, and decide.

And when the window closes, there are exactly two outcomes. Either he’s added to the fifty-three-man roster, or he reverts to PUP for the remainder of the season. No extension. No third option. No pausing the clock.

October first, plus twenty-one days, is October twenty-second.

Now here’s the part of Friday’s news that changed my thinking, and I want to be honest about it because I’ve been beating the Charbonnet drum for three videos.

The reporting now says Seattle will likely put him on the roster before the Chiefs game — but that doesn’t mean he’ll be ready to play. Macdonald gave no clear update on Friday and, by every account, did not sound optimistic. And back on October twelfth he said it even more plainly: the team was “not planning on activating him from PUP.”

So separate two things in your head, because they are not the same decision. Activating him is a paperwork decision. Playing him is a football decision. Seattle can absolutely do the first without doing the second — put him on the fifty-three to protect the asset, then list him as a limited participant or hold him another week.

If you hear “Charbonnet activated” on Wednesday, do not assume that means twenty carries on Sunday. It might just mean Seattle refused to lose him for the year.

Update two. Anthony Bradford is eligible, and this is the one nobody’s excited about that matters most.

Week 6 in Denver was Bradford’s fourth missed game. Four games is the minimum stay on injured reserve. That means the starting right guard of the Seattle Seahawks is now eligible to be restored to the roster, and he could be on it for Kansas City.

Macdonald, Friday: “Anthony Bradford will probably have a really good chance of being back this week.” And then the sentence I keep circling: “It’ll be part of our decision-making as we go forward.”

Here’s why this is the most important update in the entire wave, and it has nothing to do with Bradford’s name recognition.

In Denver, Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks. And under that pressure he went 6-of-19 for 47 yards. Nineteen of his fifty-one throws were made with a defender arriving. Five sacks. And five sacks the week before that, against San Francisco.

In Bradford’s absence, Seattle has rotated a second-year player and a rookie at right guard — Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens — and against Denver, both of them allowed multiple pressures.

Everybody in football wants to talk about the quarterback’s decisions. A guard is a decision. And it’s the one Seattle gets to make this week for free.

Update three. Jadarian Price will not play against Kansas City. That’s now confirmed by the math.

Price has missed three games with a chest injury. The injured reserve minimum is four. So Week 7 against the Chiefs is off the table no matter how good he looks in rehab — and Macdonald says he’s “doing great.”

The target is Week 8. Chicago. November second. That’s the realistic return for Seattle’s rookie back.

Update four. Elijah Arroyo, and this is the bad one.

Arroyo left the Denver game with a shoulder injury and didn’t return. Macdonald after the game: “He has a shoulder. We’ll see what the extent is, but he obviously wasn’t able to finish.” And on Friday: “He’s getting some imaging and testing as we speak, so no update yet.”

As of right now, we still don’t have the result of that imaging. And the reason this lands harder than a normal tight end injury is Seattle is thin at the position, and the one structural thing Brian Fleury actually built this offense around was two tight ends on the field together — Arroyo and A.J. Barner — because that personnel group forces a defense to respect the middle of the field, which is exactly what loosens coverage on your best receiver.

Take Arroyo off the field and you’ve removed part of the answer to the bracket coverage that held Jaxon Smith-Njigba to two catches for eleven yards in the first half on Thursday.

And there’s a fifth update, which is the absence of one. Seattle has made no roster transaction since October fifteenth. The last moves were elevating linebacker Jimmy Ciarlo and receiver Irv Charles from the practice squad for the Denver game. So if you see a video this weekend claiming Seattle signed somebody, check the date on it. Nothing has happened.

That’s the wave. Four injury updates, one roster silence, and two hard deadlines: October twenty-second for Charbonnet, November tenth for the trade deadline.

Now let’s talk about the game, because this is the part I actually came here to say.

The document mentions — in one passing sentence, before moving on to Saquon Barkley — that “Kenneth Walker the Third is now with the Kansas City Chiefs, Seattle’s next opponent on October twenty-fifth.”

That sentence deserves its own video. Let me give you the numbers.

Kenneth Walker the Third has 537 rushing yards in four games. He’s averaging 134.3 yards per game, which is the highest in the National Football League. He’s averaging 6.2 yards per carry. He has four rushing touchdowns and two receiving touchdowns. He’s gone over a hundred yards in three of his four games — the exception being seventy against Miami.

Last Sunday against the Las Vegas Raiders he ran for 177 yards on 22 carries. Eight yards a carry. Two touchdowns. Kansas City won 30-27.

And those 537 yards in his first four games with the Chiefs? That’s a Kansas City franchise record for any player in that span. It’s also the most by any running back in a team’s first four games in the last fifteen years — he passed DeMarco Murray’s 534 from 2014.

Now here’s the pace, and you need to sit down for it. Extrapolated across a full season, Kenneth Walker is on track for 2,282 rushing yards.

Eric Dickerson’s single-season rushing record is 2,105 yards. It has stood since 1984.

Walker needs to average about 112.5 yards per game the rest of the way to reach two thousand — and only nine running backs in the history of this sport have ever done that.

And now put it next to the Seattle number one more time. The Seahawks had 530 team rushing yards through five games. One hundred and thirty-nine carries, 530 yards. Walker, by himself, on 87 carries, had 537 in four.

And I have to say the rest of it, because it’s the part that actually stings. Seattle won the Super Bowl last season. Kenneth Walker the Third was the MVP of that game.

He’s in red on Sunday.

So here’s the collision, and this is the real preview of this football game.

The Seattle Seahawks have not allowed a 100-yard rusher in 35 consecutive games. That is the longest streak in franchise history.

Thirty-five games. That’s more than two full seasons. It ran through the championship year, through every week of last season, through all six of these. Nobody has done it to this defense in two years.

And the man averaging 134.3 a game — more than any human being in professional football — walks into Lumen Field on October twenty-fifth.

That’s the game. Not a trade. Not a quarterback change. The best run-defense streak in Seahawks history against what might become the best rushing season in NFL history, and the running back used to wear our colors.

So let me tell you what to actually watch, because this matchup has a specific shape.

Walker’s damage comes from the second level. Eight yards a carry against Las Vegas was not twenty-two runs of grinding out four yards. That number comes from a handful of carries where he cleared the line of scrimmage clean and made a safety wrong in open space. That’s always been his game — not the first five yards, the next twenty.

Which means this game is about two things: gap integrity up front, and whether Seattle’s second level is healthy.

And that second part is a genuine question right now. Drake Thomas was out against Denver with an ankle. Chazz Surratt was out with a calf. That’s two linebackers, which is exactly why Seattle had to elevate Jimmy Ciarlo from the practice squad for linebacker depth.

Watch the Wednesday injury report for Thomas and Surratt. I’m telling you plainly: those two names will tell you more about Sunday’s result than any trade rumor you will read this week. A thirty-five game streak built on a full linebacker room is a different thing from a thirty-five game streak defended by a room missing two men.

And here’s the case for Seattle, which is real. That defense held the Denver Broncos to 168 total yards on Thursday. A hundred and sixty-eight. Seattle outgained Denver 353 to 168 and lost anyway. The defense is not the problem on this team. It’s been carrying the thing.

So the question isn’t whether Seattle’s defense is good. It’s whether a historic streak survives a historic runner with two linebackers in the training room.

And let me give you one more thing to watch, because it’s the hidden lever in every hundred-yard-rusher streak and almost nobody mentions it: volume.

A streak like Seattle’s thirty-five games does not survive on tackling alone. It survives on score and clock. A running back needs carries to reach a hundred yards, and he gets carries when his team is ahead and wants to shorten the game. Walker’s 177-yard night against Las Vegas came on 22 carries — and Kansas City won. If Seattle leads in the fourth quarter on Sunday, Kansas City has to throw, and Walker might finish with sixteen touches and seventy yards.

Which means Seattle’s offense is part of its run defense. That sounds like a paradox until you’ve watched enough football: every three-and-out your offense has is another series your defense has to stop, and every lead your offense builds is a carry your opponent’s star never gets. Complementary football isn’t a slogan. It’s arithmetic.

And that’s exactly why the two updates at the top of this video matter so much. Bradford coming back is run-defense help. A quarterback who isn’t getting hit on two out of five snaps means longer drives, means fewer possessions for Kansas City, means fewer carries for Kenneth Walker.

As for the Chiefs themselves — they were 4-0 as of a week ago, they came off a bye, and Walker himself described feeling rejuvenated after it. Then he put up 177 and two scores on the Raiders. This is not a team Seattle catches on a bad week.
