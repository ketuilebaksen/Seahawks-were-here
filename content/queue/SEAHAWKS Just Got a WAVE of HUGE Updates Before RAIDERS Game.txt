Do you think 2,000 yards is possible with eight games left?”

Seahawks Nation, that question was asked out loud, in a Seattle Seahawks press conference, about Jaxon Smith-Njigba.

Because on Sunday, in a seventeen to three win over Arizona, Jaxon Smith-Njigba crossed one thousand receiving yards for the season.

In nine games.

And twelves, the man they asked about it is Cooper Kupp — a man who once put up a receiving triple crown and a Super Bowl MVP. So if anybody on earth is qualified to tell you where the ceiling is, it’s him.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four, Las Vegas is Sunday, and Cooper Kupp just gave us a wave of information — about the quarterback room, about the running backs, about how Arizona played coverage, and about what Sam Darnold did behind closed doors this week. Let’s go through all of it.

Let’s start with that number, because I want you to understand what we’re actually watching.

One thousand receiving yards in nine games.

That’s a pace of roughly a hundred and eleven yards per game. Over a full seventeen-game season, that projects to somewhere near nineteen hundred yards.

Twelves — the all-time single-season receiving record is nineteen hundred and sixty-four yards. Calvin Johnson, 2012.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba is not having a very good season. Jaxon Smith-Njigba is in the conversation with the best receiving season in the history of the National Football League.

And he did it on a team that has started two different quarterbacks, lost its left tackle, lost its left guard, traded for a new left guard who then needed a knee procedure, and played three straight prime time games.

Two thousand would require about a hundred and twenty-five yards a game the rest of the way, every week, with a quarterback situation nobody can predict. It’s a staggering ask. But the fact that a reporter could say the number without getting laughed out of the room tells you exactly where we are.

And remember what Cooper Kupp said about him last week, when a reporter invited him to complain about his own targets: “Jax is a great player. He’s going to command targets. I think he’s one of, if not the best receiver in the league right now. If he’s open, the ball’s going to go his way.”

That wasn’t politeness. That was an evaluation. And the thousand-yard mark just proved it.

Now — the biggest piece of news in this press conference, and it’s a number nobody had.

A reporter said this to Cooper Kupp: “Drew Lock threw maybe twenty balls this week.”

TWENTY.

Twelves, Drew Lock told us himself that he only threw on one day — Thursday. Now we have the count. Roughly twenty footballs in an entire week of preparation.

Let me put that in perspective. A healthy starting quarterback throws somewhere between a hundred and fifty and two hundred and fifty footballs in a normal week of practice, and then another forty or fifty in pregame warmups.

Drew Lock threw twenty. And then went out and won a division game seventeen to three.

And Cooper Kupp’s description of watching it happen is the most respectful thing one professional can say about another:

“Yeah, man. It’s tough. I mean, you’re watching him go through his daily process and what it takes just to be able to get out there to throw the football early in the week. He didn’t throw much. And so battling through, and being able to mentally prepare for a football game without being able to go out there and actually do it — that’s really tough to do. I did think he did an unbelievable job, and toughed through obviously some stuff today as well.”

“Being able to mentally prepare for a football game without being able to go out there and actually do it — that’s really tough to do.”

That’s the whole thing, twelves.

Because throwing is not the hard part of quarterback preparation. Timing is. Every route in this offense has a rhythm — the receiver’s break happens on a specific step of the quarterback’s drop. And the only way to build that rhythm is to do it, over and over, with live bodies.

Drew Lock couldn’t do that. So he had to build the whole week in his head.

And Lock told us how: tape, walkthrough reps, and “re-watching practices.” Not watching — re-watching. Watching practice film of a practice he was standing at, so he could see it from the quarterback’s angle.

And then Kupp gave the simplest, highest compliment available:

“Yeah, I mean, he’s a football player, man. Some guys can go out there and do that, and it’s just like — when the lights come on, you can go out there and play ball. And he showed an ability to do that obviously through this week. Just the poise that he had in the huddle was great.”

“He’s a football player, man.”

Twelves, in a locker room that is the highest form of praise. It’s not “he’s talented.” It’s not “he’s got a big arm.” It means: whatever the circumstances, he functions. Twenty throws, a sore elbow, an oblique in the third quarter. He functioned.

“The poise that he had in the huddle.” That’s the detail only the ten other men in that huddle can give you. Nobody watching on television can see whether a quarterback’s voice is calm in the huddle. Cooper Kupp can.

AND NOW — the thing I actually wanted to make this video about.

Cooper Kupp said this, completely unprompted:

“And that QB room, they work so well together. They really take such a collaborative approach to the game. All those guys are talking constantly with one another. Whatever it takes to be able to help whoever that is that’s out there playing.”

And then: “I mean, SAM WAS AWESOME THIS WEEK FOR DREW. I’m sure Drew will mention that.”

“Sam was awesome this week for Drew.”

Twelves, stop. Sit with that one.

Sam Darnold is the starting quarterback of this football team. He is on a contract that runs out. He is playing for his next deal. He has a cracked rib and a bad quad. And he just watched his backup start a game in his place.

And according to a teammate, he spent the week helping that backup get ready.

Not tolerating it. Not sulking through it. “Awesome.”

And here’s why this is the most beautiful detail of the entire season, and I need you to follow the whole circle.

Last January, Sam Darnold couldn’t practice in the playoffs. And Drew Lock took the practice reps for him. Lock said it himself this week: “I was practicing for him.” And when Lock built his own throwing plan this week, he said: “it was basically the same template” — the plan he helped execute for Sam Darnold, with “like a three-minute tweak in the middle of it.”

So read the full sequence.

Last year: Darnold can’t throw. Lock runs the practice week for him. Darnold plays great in the playoffs.

This year: Lock can’t throw. Darnold runs the week for him. Lock plays great against Arizona.

They have now done this for each other, in both directions, in the same calendar year.

And Mike Macdonald’s comment after this game was: “both quarterbacks that have played — I don’t know how you get any tougher. Both guys tough as nails.” He said “both” when nobody asked about Sam.

Twelves, I have spent weeks on this channel refusing to make videos claiming Sam Darnold is finished, or benched, or wants out. This is why. The receiver said it. The head coach said it. The backup said it. Three independent voices, same story.

“Sam was awesome this week for Drew.” That’s a quarterback room, not a competition.

Now the drive that won the game, and Kupp’s description of it is excellent.

Asked what it means to close out a game backed up at the one yard line and run off four and a half minutes:

“Yeah, that’s huge. That’s a big deal. You’re backed up there on the one yard line, whatever it is — being able to not only just move the ball out into a safe place, if you did have to punt it, but be able to just FINISH with the ball. We talk about that all the time. And Zach did a great job putting that on his shoulders and just making some great runs, and leaning on that offensive line.”

And then: “That’s the world we want to be able to live in.”

“We want to be able to live in that world.”

Twelves, let me explain how hard what Seattle just did actually is.

You’re on your own one yard line. Four and a half minutes left. You have a lead. Everybody in the stadium knows you’re running the ball. The defense knows. The defense is playing run. And you have to run it anyway, and you have to gain enough yards that the clock runs out.

There is nowhere to hide. No play-action, no deep shot, no trickery. Just eleven men who know what’s coming against eleven men who have to stop it.

And Seattle didn’t just escape. Kupp’s word was “finish.” They never gave the ball back. The Cardinals’ offense did not touch the football again.

That is the most mature thing a football team can do, and six days earlier this same offense was producing three-and-outs and putting its own defense back on the field. Cooper Kupp said so himself.

Now Zach Charbonnet — and Kupp gave us a math lesson.

“That’s huge. It’s a huge deal when you’re talking about giving a guy an edge, and the ability to run through arm tackles and, like you said, fall forward.”

And then this: “I mean, you stay ahead of the six, and you’re at second and six — that’s a big difference than second and ten. And so that was a huge thing today.”

“You stay ahead of the six.”

Twelves, that’s a professional football player explaining down-and-distance economics and it’s perfect.

First and ten. You run the ball. If you get four yards, it’s second and six. If you get nothing, it’s second and ten.

And those two situations are completely different games. On second and six, everything is available — run, pass, play-action, screen. The defense has to respect all of it. On second and ten, you’re already losing. The defense knows you have to throw, the safeties can play deeper, the pass rush can pin its ears back.

So “falling forward” is not a highlight-reel trait. It’s the difference between an offense that gets to call whatever it wants and an offense that’s already behind schedule.

And remember what Drew Lock said about the same thing: “they were fighting through contact. First guy wasn’t tackling.”

And Kupp’s genuine joy about it: “just pumped for Zach, really. I mean, man, just looked great. And so I’m excited for him. Confidence that’s going to continue to build for him coming back from what he’s coming back from.”

“Coming back from what he’s coming back from.”

He tore his ACL in January, twelves. He had not taken a live football rep in eight months before the Bears game. He got held under thirty yards on Monday night and a national analyst’s word for him was “rust.”

Eight days later, a Super Bowl MVP is pumped about him.

Now Jadarian Price, and Kupp gave us a scouting term I love.

Asked what makes him so tough to stop in the open field:

“Yeah, I mean, he’s electric. He’s electric. He’s a slash runner. He’s decisive, obviously has great hands. And being able to activate him in different parts of the field — I think all of our backs have shown an ability to do that, but Jadarian did a great job of that today.”

“He’s a slash runner.”

Twelves, that’s a real description and it’s specific. A slash runner doesn’t dance. He doesn’t gather himself and look for the perfect hole. He picks a line and cuts through it on one step. Decisive, downhill, violent change of direction — and then he’s past you.

And “activate him in different parts of the field” is the modern running back job. Not just handoffs. Screens. Routes out of the backfield. Motioning him out wide. Making the defense account for him in five different places.

And look at the agreement here. Macdonald’s word for Price on Sunday was “decisive.” Kupp’s word was “decisive.” Same word, two different rooms.

And one week earlier, a national analyst’s word for him was “hesitant.”

That’s how fast this changed.

Now the coverage answer — and this is the best scheme breakdown anybody has given us this week.

A reporter noted that Kupp and Jaxon Smith-Njigba both had success on in-breaking routes over the middle, and asked how that and the run game complemented each other.

“Yeah, well, a lot of that is just kind of dependent on the coverages you’re getting. Arizona’s doing a good job there, showing some stuff — being able to show a heavy box, and then roll into some two-high stuff. Get into some places where we felt like the middle field was open, and attacking places where safeties were pushing and things like that.”

Let me translate this, because it’s the whole game plan.

“Showing a heavy box” means Arizona lined up like they were stopping the run — extra bodies near the line, a safety down low. That’s a picture designed to make you think the middle of the field is closed.

“And then roll into some two-high stuff” means at the snap, that safety sprints back and they end up with two deep safeties instead of one.

That’s disguise, twelves. Show one coverage, play another. It’s the single hardest thing for an offense to deal with, because the quarterback’s read is based on a picture that no longer exists by the time he’s finished his drop.

But here’s the trade-off, and it’s the reason Seattle beat it: when both safeties go deep, the middle of the field opens up. You can’t be deep and in the middle at the same time. So the in-breaking routes — the dig, the slant, the over route — become available.

And “attacking places where safeties were pushing” means reading which direction the safety is leaning and throwing to the side he just left.

And then Kupp tied it all the way back to the run game, and this is the part I want you to catch:

“When you can keep defenses in this place where they are playing two-high, it’s a great opportunity to build around the ball. And it puts the onus on us as receivers to make sure we’re getting down the field blocking the guys we’re supposed to — to turn those short runs into the big ones.”

“It puts the onus on us as receivers to make sure we’re getting down the field blocking.”

THIS is the complete picture, twelves.

A two-high safety look means there are fewer defenders near the line of scrimmage. Which means running the football gets easier. Which means the only thing standing between a six-yard run and a thirty-yard run is a receiver blocking a cornerback twenty yards downfield.

And Cooper Kupp — a nine-year veteran, a Super Bowl MVP — just publicly made that his own responsibility.

That’s the exact thing he said last week: “you’ve got to compete without the ball as well.” And here he is, one week later, explaining the specific unglamorous job that turns Zach Charbonnet’s good run into a great one.

Nobody claps for a perimeter block. Cooper Kupp doesn’t care.

Now the all-seventy answer, and he added something nobody else did.

Asked what it says about this team to win decisively while missing a ton of starters, including the quarterback:

“Yeah, that’s huge. It’s a big deal. A lot of guys down, man — and a lot of guys asked to do things that they maybe haven’t been asked to do in games, but have been preparing to do regardless. And so I’m excited for those guys to be able to watch the film and see those guys’ preparation come to life.”

“A lot of guys asked to do things they maybe haven’t been asked to do in games, but have been preparing to do regardless.”

That’s what “the power of our seventy” actually means in practice. Not a slogan. It means a man who has not played a meaningful snap all year has still been preparing every single week as if he would.

And “watch their preparation come to life” is a lovely way to put it. That’s what the film session on Monday is for these guys — proof that the work mattered.

But then Kupp did the thing that makes him Cooper Kupp. He refused to let it be a feel-good ending:

“And yeah, there’s going to be some really cool stuff to see. There’s also going to be some really tough stuff to watch that we continue to get better at.”

“There’s also going to be some really tough stuff to watch.”

Seventeen to three. Six starters out. A division win. And the veteran receiver’s closing thought is that Monday’s film session is going to hurt in places.

And that’s exactly what Mike Macdonald said too: “there’s some things we fell short at, that of course we’re going to go back and attack.” And his grade was deliberately modest: “I thought we took a step today.”

A step. Not a leap. Nobody in that building is overselling this, which is the main reason I believe it.
