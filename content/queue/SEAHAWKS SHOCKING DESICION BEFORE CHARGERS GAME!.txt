Mike Macdonald was asked about Jim Harbaugh this week, and he said something you almost never hear a head coach say about the man standing on the other sideline.

"A lot of our principles are built off of his principles about how he builds his football teams."

Read that again. Not "we respect them." Not "they're well coached." The head coach of the defending Super Bowl champions just said that the foundation of how he builds a football team came from the man he's playing on Sunday.

And Seahawks Nation, it gets better than that. Because on Seattle's own coaching staff, right now, is Jim Harbaugh's son.

Macdonald talked for about fifteen minutes on Wednesday and it was one of the most revealing press conferences he's given — the Harbaugh connection, a story about his one year in college football, the phrase that explains how this entire defense is built, an admission about his own coaching that nobody's talking about, and every injury update you need before Sunday. Start with the family, because this is genuinely special and it's the part the national media is going to miss.

Jay Harbaugh is thirty-five years old and he is the Seattle Seahawks' special teams coordinator. He spent nine seasons coaching with his father at Michigan before coming back to the NFL. And on Sunday, at Lumen Field, he coaches against his dad.

Macdonald's words: "I'd like to say I'm excited for Jay. I don't — I imagine it's a different feeling for him. Pretty special though. I mean, how often does a father and son get to coach against each other in an NFL game?"

Almost never. And the answer for these two specifically is: once, twelve years ago. Back in 2014, Jay was an offensive quality control coach for the Baltimore Ravens — working under his other relative, his uncle John — and Baltimore beat Jim's 49ers 23-3.

That's the only time. Since then, nothing.

And Macdonald added a detail I loved, because it's exactly what you'd expect from this building: "just talking to Jay, you know, he's trying to keep it as consistent as possible."

Keep it consistent. Not make it a storyline, not make it about himself. The same phrase Macdonald used about his entire week.

And here's what Jay himself has said about his father, and I think it tells you everything about the relationship: "I think he's the best coach in the world, and I enjoy being able to ask him for advice and learn from him."

That's a son calling his father the best coach in the world — and then preparing a kick coverage unit to beat him.

Now let me go deeper on the Macdonald side of this, because he was asked about his year at Michigan and he opened up more than he usually does.

For anybody who doesn't know the history: Mike Macdonald spent 2021 as the defensive coordinator at the University of Michigan, under Jim Harbaugh. One season. His only year in college football. Then he went back to Baltimore as the Ravens' defensive coordinator, and two years after that he was a Super Bowl-winning head coach in Seattle.

And the story he told about arriving at Michigan is fantastic.

He said, and I'm quoting: "That was wild. That was wild. I remember Jim was asking, 'We're running the Ravens defense, right?' I was like, 'Yeah, I think so.' Kind of."

Yeah, I think so. Kind of.

That's a thirty-three-year-old coordinator walking into one of the biggest jobs in college football while his head coach asks a reasonable question and he's not totally sure of the answer yet.

And then he described the actual problem he hit, which is a genuinely interesting football detail. College formations. His words: "You'd come up with something sweet. You put it on the board and like, 'Oh, what do we do against unbalanced?' Like, 'Ah, crap. We can't run it.' There's seventy guys over there. There's nobody over there. The formations are a pain in the butt."

Seventy guys on one side and nobody on the other. That's what college offenses do to a defensive coordinator who learned his craft in the NFL, where formations are far more conventional.

But here's where it stops being a funny story and becomes the most important thing he said all day.

He was asked what he took from that year that helped him when he came back to the pros. And his answer was about communication.

In college you can't put a radio in a player's helmet. You have to signal everything from the sideline. And Macdonald said his calls had been "pretty wordy" — so he had to package things, make them simpler.

And then he gave us the phrase: "We talk about the elegant simplicity — less words mean more things."

Elegant simplicity. Less words mean more things.

Twelves, that sentence explains this entire defense.

Think about what Seattle does. They allowed seventeen points in two games with pre-snap pictures that lie — a safety who could be a deep defender or a blitzer, a 220-pound chess piece standing on the line of scrimmage one snap and in the nickel the next. That whole system only works if players can process the call in under a second and then execute multiple jobs out of one look.

You can't do that with a wordy call. You do it with a short call that means several things, and players who are trained well enough to know which one.

He had to learn that because of a rule about helmet radios in college football. And now it's the backbone of a Super Bowl defense.

And he said one more thing about that year: "How we played was really a point of emphasis. That became very clear — the style of play, how we defeat blocks, how we stay square, how we play run defense."

Defeat blocks. Stay square. That's still the vocabulary he uses now, and it's the exact thing that failed in Washington when Rachaad White broke around the right end on third-and-nine.

Alright. Now the part that I think is the heart of this whole thing, because he was asked where "twelve as one" came from — the phrase he brought to Seattle — and he traced it directly back to that Michigan team.

His answer: "I just felt the power of that team. That team was really connected. That was my first experience being in charge of a unit and being responsible for the spirit of that group. And we had a great group and they were really connected, and you just felt the synergy of the group, of the belief and the hunger and the daily work ethic, the toughness. When it didn't go right, you felt the resolve. You felt the togetherness."

That was my first experience being responsible for the spirit of a group.

That's an enormous admission from a head coach. He's telling you that Michigan 2021 is where he learned the part of the job that isn't scheme — and he was honest that he'd been around it in Baltimore but hadn't been the one carrying it: "we had that at our teams in Baltimore, but I wasn't responsible. I wasn't really the one leading the charge."

And he contrasted it with an earlier stop. He was asked about his time at Georgia, where by his own description it was rough every day and he felt like he was getting fake-fired constantly. Compare that to how he talks about Michigan: "I'm really proud of that year. There's a lot of adversity, a lot of noise in the offseason, and you felt the building come together." He talked about the staff being connected, about living in Ann Arbor, about friends he still has.

His summary: "I look back at the experience and it's overwhelmingly positive."

So on Sunday he plays the man who gave him that.

Macdonald has said before — on Marshawn Lynch's podcast — that the number one thing he took from both Harbaugh brothers, Jim and John, was this: "They love their players. That's probably the number one thing that sticks out. They have their players' backs at all times."

They have their players' backs at all times.

Now let me get to the news, because there was real news in there — and the sharpest moment of the whole press conference was him criticizing himself.

A reporter did his homework. He pointed out that the film actually showed Seattle's pass rush was pretty good against Washington, and that the time-to-pressure numbers were good. So the question was: does that mean the problem is the coverage?

And here's Macdonald's answer: "I think we can do a better job as a team, frankly. I think we can do a better job as a head coach game planning coverages and calling coverages as well."

Stop there. "I think we can do a better job as a head coach... calling coverages."

That's a man who built his entire reputation as a defensive mastermind — who won a Super Bowl with the best defense in football — saying out loud that he didn't call it well enough.

And it reframes what happened at the end of that game. Remember the third-and-nine with 1:55 left, Seattle holding all three timeouts? Macdonald told us earlier in the week that Washington audibled against zero coverage, made a good check, and Seattle didn't make the play. Zero coverage means no deep safety — everybody locked up, everybody else coming.

So the most aggressive call in football got read at the line and beaten. And rather than blame the players who didn't execute it, he's pointing at the man who called it.

That's the second week in a row he's done this. "No, we didn't get enough pressure today." Red zone "not good enough." "We need to find more ways to be aggressive." And now the coverage calls.

I'll say what I said last week: a coach who audits himself in public is a coach whose players will run through a wall for him. And it also tells you something concrete — expect the coverage picture to look different on Sunday.

Which brings us to the run game, and Macdonald refused to panic about it.

His words: "Again, it's a process. If you would have asked me this last week, we ran the ball like forty-five times for X amount of yards, closed the game out — we're excited. So I'm not going to totally flip the switch on your answer."

And then he listed what he sees: "I think our guys are running off the rock. I think they're playing hard. I think we're practicing extremely well. I think our staff's working well together.
