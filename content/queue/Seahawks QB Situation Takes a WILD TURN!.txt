“Elbow tendinitis.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s the word. That’s the actual diagnosis on Drew Lock’s elbow, and it changes how you should read this entire situation.

Because a few weeks ago, a national analyst guessed at exactly this. His words were: “as anybody knows who’s had tennis elbow or anything like that — that’s annoying. It can hang around for a quarterback.”

Tennis elbow IS tendinitis. He nailed it without having the diagnosis.

And here’s the rest of the picture: Sam Darnold has been ruled OUT for the Raiders game. Drew Lock is managing an oblique AND that elbow. Jalen Milroe is getting more snaps this week. And the signs are pointing toward Sam Darnold being Seattle’s starting quarterback again after the bye.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four, Las Vegas is Sunday, and the quarterback room just got genuinely complicated. Let’s go through all of it — and I’ll give you my honest answer on who should start after the bye.

First, the piece of news that settles this week: Sam Darnold is out.

Not questionable. Not a game-time decision. Out.

So Drew Lock starts in Las Vegas, and Mike Macdonald already confirmed the plan: “Drew’s making good progress. We have a plan. And similar to last week, we’ll go from there. He’s not going to take every rep. He’s not going to make every throw again.”

Similar to last week. And twelves, we now know exactly what “last week” meant in numbers.

Roughly twenty-something passing snaps in an entire week of practice.

Let me put that in perspective one more time, because I don’t think it’s sunk in for most people.

A healthy starting quarterback throws somewhere between a hundred and fifty and two hundred and fifty footballs in a normal practice week, plus another forty or fifty warming up before the game.

Drew Lock threw twenty-something. And then went nineteen of twenty-eight for 175 yards and a touchdown, with zero turnovers, in a seventeen to three win.

And Lock himself told us the mechanics: he threw on Thursday only. He didn’t throw Wednesday. And he didn’t throw in pregame until he had the shoulder pads and the helmet on — a plan Seattle spent all of Saturday building.

His words afterward: “the arm felt fresh.”

That was the entire point.

Now — let’s talk about what tendinitis actually is, because the word matters.

Tendinitis is not a tear. It’s not a break. It’s inflammation of a tendon — an overuse injury.

And twelves, that’s why this is such a nasty problem for a quarterback specifically, and I want you to understand the trap.

With a broken bone, you rest it and it heals on a timeline. With a torn muscle, same thing — there’s a clock, and the clock runs whether you like it or not.

Tendinitis doesn’t work like that. Tendinitis is caused by the repetitive motion. Which means the treatment is: stop doing the motion.

And the motion is throwing a football.

So Drew Lock is in an impossible loop. To get better, he needs to throw less. To be a quarterback, he needs to throw. Every single practice rep he takes is both preparation AND aggravation.

That’s why Seattle isn’t trying to heal it during the season. They’re MANAGING it. They’ve accepted that it isn’t going away until he stops throwing for a real stretch of time — and the only real stretch of time on this calendar is the bye week.

And remember how it started: Macdonald told us Lock hurt it in a rain practice. “We practiced in the rain and he got it. It got aggravated in a rain practice.”

That’s about as un-dramatic an origin story as exists in professional football. And it may end up shaping the back half of Seattle’s season.

Now layer the oblique on top, because that’s the newer problem.

Lock hurt it on a designed keeper in the third quarter against Arizona — not a hit, not a sack, just his own torso doing something it didn’t want to do. He went into the medical tent, and his own account of it is still my favorite thing he’s said:

“They said let me see how it feels throwing the ball. I was like, ‘Wait — I’ve already thrown the ball. You’re going to be all right.’”

And then he finished the game.

And twelves, here’s why an oblique is such a cruel pairing with a sore elbow: the oblique is the muscle on the side of your torso, and it’s the muscle that ROTATES you. Throwing a football is a rotation. Every pass he throws goes through that muscle.

So he now has one injury in the arm that throws and one in the core that generates the throw.

And the latest update on him was described as positive. Macdonald’s version: “making good progress... trying to be as prudent as possible to make sure he’s feeling his best on Sunday.”

“Feeling his best on SUNDAY.” They’re spending his health on the game, not on practice. That’s the whole philosophy and it worked once already.

Now — what Seattle actually needs from him on Sunday, and there’s one specific improvement being asked for.

The things that have been praised are the things you’d expect: getting the ball out quickly, delivering on time, finding his targets. That’s what he did against Arizona and it’s what a limited-practice quarterback has to do — if you can’t build deep timing in practice, you live in the quick game.

But the ask is ball security.

And twelves, here’s the honest version of that, with real numbers.

Drew Lock has now gone three straight games without a turnover. Three.

And his season line is excellent: seventy-three percent completions, 8.8 yards per attempt, four touchdowns, ZERO interceptions.

Zero.

So when the expectation is “another performance like that, but take care of the football” — the record says he already is. That’s not a flaw to fix. That’s a thing to maintain.

But I understand why it gets said, and here’s why it’s the right thing to emphasize: Lock explained his own job better than anybody else could.

His words: “knowing that we can control the game with the run, operate on third downs well, keep the ball in our hands, allow our defense to have long fields to defend — not giving the team the ball on the thirty or the twenty, whatever it may be, even at the fifty. Playing complementary football.”

And then: “We have the best punter in the world. So getting to fourth down, it is what it is, when you have Michael Dickson being able to punt and pin guys deep and allow our defense to have a ninety-yard cushion.”

That’s the formula, twelves. Don’t turn it over, run it, convert third downs, and when the drive dies, let Michael Dickson bury them.

Arizona scored THREE POINTS. The formula works.

Now Jalen Milroe, and this is the detail people are misreading.

Milroe is expected to get additional snaps this week.

And twelves, that does NOT mean he’s about to start. Let’s be clear about that, because I’ve seen people run with it.

Drew Lock is still the preferred option. Milroe is the contingency.

But the contingency just got a lot more realistic, and here’s why: Seattle’s starting quarterback is managing two injuries, and his backup has thrown zero passes in an NFL game.

Although — he has now taken a live snap. Macdonald confirmed it after the Arizona game: “Jalen Milroe too — operating our offense when he took the snap that he took, and being ready to play in case Drew couldn’t go or couldn’t finish.”

“In case Drew couldn’t FINISH.” Given that Lock got hurt in the third quarter, that was a live possibility.

And remember what Drew Lock himself said about development: “reps for quarterbacks is better than any tape they can watch, better than any footwork drills they can go do on the side.”

And he said something even smarter about Milroe’s situation specifically. He talked about the difference between being QB2 and QB3 — and it’s a psychological difference, not a depth chart one:

“There’s the ‘I’m a backup, I’m not playing, but I might actually play.’ And then when you’re in that three, it’s like ‘I’m going to be ready, but I’m definitely not playing.’ But there’s different mindsets that you can fall into, get TRAPPED into.”

“Trapped.” Because the second you accept you’re not playing, you stop preparing like you are — and then the phone rings.

And Lock knows, because he was the three eight weeks ago.

So the extra snaps this week aren’t a hint. They’re insurance — and they’re development. Macdonald’s read on Milroe’s first real week as the two: “he hasn’t taken a lot of reps at the one, so to see him do that and operate the offense has been impressive.”

NOW — the part that actually matters most, and it’s about what happens AFTER Sunday.

The signs are pointing toward Sam Darnold returning as Seattle’s starting quarterback after the bye week.

And twelves, I want to tell you why I think that’s correct — and then I want to tell you honestly why a lot of you are going to disagree with me.

Here’s the case for Darnold, and it is not sentimental.

First: he was never benched. He got hurt. There’s a difference and it matters. Darnold cracked a rib on the second play of a Monday night game and played all four quarters without telling his own head coach. Macdonald found out after. Then the quad injury on his final drive. That’s an injury absence, not a performance decision.

Second: the building has not wavered once. And I mean not once.

Mike Macdonald has volunteered something flattering about Sam Darnold in THREE consecutive press conferences — including the one right after Drew Lock won by fourteen, where nobody asked about Sam, and Macdonald said: “both quarterbacks that have played — I don’t know how you get any tougher. BOTH guys tough as nails.”

He said “both.” Unprompted. In a press conference about his replacement.

And then there’s Cooper Kupp, who volunteered this in the middle of an answer about somebody else entirely: “Sam was awesome this week for Drew. I’m sure Drew will mention that.”

“Sam was awesome this week for Drew.”

Twelves, a starting quarterback in a contract year, playing on a cracked rib, spent the week helping the man taking his job get ready. And a teammate said so without being asked.

And the circle on this is beautiful. Last January, Darnold couldn’t practice in the playoffs, and Drew Lock took the practice reps FOR him. Lock said it himself: “I was practicing for him.” And when Lock built his own twenty-throw plan this week, he said it was “basically the same template” with “a three-minute tweak in the middle of it.”

They have now done this for each other, in both directions, in the same calendar year.

Third, and this is the practical one: Drew Lock’s body cannot do this indefinitely.

Elbow tendinitis doesn’t resolve while you keep throwing. The oblique needs rotation rest. You cannot run a twenty-throw week every week for nine weeks and expect a quarterback to still be functional in January.

The bye week is the only chance either of these men gets to actually heal. And if Sam Darnold’s rib and quad clear in that window, you get your starter back AND you get to let Drew Lock’s elbow quiet down.

That’s not favoritism. That’s roster management.

Now the honest counter-argument, because this channel doesn’t do propaganda.

Sam Darnold’s season is eight touchdowns and eight interceptions with an eighty passer rating. That’s a one-to-one ratio from the man who’s supposed to be the stabilizing adult in that offense. It isn’t good enough and I’ve said so every time.

And Drew Lock is two and oh as a starter with zero interceptions.

But I’ll give you the context people keep leaving out: both of Lock’s wins are against Arizona. The same three-and-five team, twice. Darnold’s seven starts include San Francisco, Washington, Denver, Chicago — and a twenty to three dismantling of the Kansas City Chiefs.

That is not the same schedule. Not remotely.

And here’s my actual position, twelves: I don’t think this is Darnold versus Lock at all. I think that’s the wrong question.

I think the question is which quarterback can be healthy, and the answer right now is neither of them fully. So the right move is: Lock on Sunday, bye week for both bodies, and then play whoever walks out of that bye week actually healthy.

And if that’s Sam Darnold — and the signs say it will be — then it’s Sam Darnold, and the man who played four quarters on a cracked rib has earned the chance to finish what he started.

NOW — Quenton Nelson, and there’s a real question in this one that deserves answering.

Here’s the timeline. The knee issue was NOT new. Macdonald confirmed Nelson was already managing it when Seattle acquired him — “it was something he was dealing with coming out of whatever game he was in before the trade,” and “obviously passed the physical,” and “it’s been aggravated since he got here.”

He played every single offensive snap on Monday night against Chicago. On that knee.

Then the news came Thursday. Friday he was ruled out. And the procedure didn’t actually happen until MONDAY.

So the fair question is: why the delay?

And twelves, I’m not going to invent a sinister answer, because there’s an obvious one.

Seattle had a football game on Sunday.

Think about it practically. Once you’ve ruled a man out for Sunday, there is no football reason to operate on Thursday instead of Monday. Monday is when the medical staff isn’t running a game week. It’s when the surgeon has a clean calendar. It’s when the recovery clock can start without competing with travel, meetings and a stadium.

And more importantly — it gives you the weekend to re-image, consult, and be certain you’re doing the right procedure. Macdonald said exactly that: “we’ve kind of done the due diligence and everybody agrees it’s best to take care of it now and play the long game.”

“Done the due diligence.” That’s what those four days were.

And the result is the best news of the week: “He’s here. He’s back in the building, and he looks good. We were able to get to some things treatment-wise that are of the caliber — which is MUSIC TO MY EARS.”

“Music to my ears.” Surgery Monday, in the building Wednesday, coach using those words.

And “we were able to get to some things” is the key phrase. When a procedure goes badly, you don’t hear that. You hear “we’ll know more in a few weeks.”

He’s week to week. He will not play in Las Vegas. And Macdonald added: “I know he’s going to be back sooner than later, IF IT’S UNDER HIS CONTROL” — which is a coach telling you his player is going to try to come back faster than he’s supposed to.

And here’s the calendar, which is doing Seattle an enormous favor.

Sunday at Las Vegas. Then the bye in Week 11. Then at San Francisco on November twenty-ninth.

That’s two and a half weeks of calendar with only one football game in it after Sunday.

And Macdonald told us the plan himself when he moved the line back: “at least this game and X amount of practices, and then going into the bye.”

The bye is doing the heavy lifting. San Francisco on November twenty-ninth is live. And Seattle never put him on injured reserve — which would have cost four games minimum, no negotiation.

Now — the offensive line puzzle, and this is more interesting than people realize.

For now it stays as it is: Grey Zabel at left guard, Anthony Bradford at right guard. Both men where they’ve played their whole careers. Macdonald’s reasoning: “let’s put those guys back in where they banked all those reps. I think that was the most wise decision.”

And it worked. Zero sacks allowed against Arizona, behind a third different interior combination in two weeks, without Charles Cross at left tackle.

But when Nelson comes back, Nelson goes to left guard. Which means Grey Zabel moves across to right guard again.

And twelves, there’s been discussion that Zabel and Bradford might SHARE the right guard job rather than one man owning it. Nothing confirms that’s going to happen — I want to be clear it’s a possibility, not a plan — but the fact that it’s even being discussed tells you something important.

Because here’s the thing everybody misses about adding a great offensive lineman: you don’t just add him. You disturb everybody.

Offensive line play is five men moving as one. It depends on knowing how the man next to you steps, how he communicates, who he passes a defender off to on a twist, what he does when a blitzer comes late. That’s not talent. That’s familiarity, and familiarity only comes from repetitions together.

So when Nelson returns, Seattle doesn’t just gain a Pro Bowl guard. It also resets Grey Zabel’s position AGAIN — and possibly Anthony Bradford’s role.

That’s why the approach is long-term instead of rushed. Getting Quenton Nelson into a uniform is the easy part. Getting him comfortable inside this specific five is the actual job.

And it’s worth remembering what Macdonald called him: “a force multiplier for our group — not just our offensive line, but really our whole offensive unit and our football team. So it’s worth that initial investment.”

A force multiplier. A man who makes the four guys beside him better. That’s worth being patient for.
