TOUCHDOWN NUMBER ONE — the scramble, and this answer is a masterclass in something nobody ever talks about.

A reporter asked how he knew where the line of scrimmage was while running around.

“Yeah, somebody was talking to me about it. He said kind of the same thing and I was like — was I really close? Kind of just like an INTERNAL FEELING, so to say. You’ve taken a million reps, scrambled maybe half a million, kind of have a good sense of where that line of scrimmage is.”

“You’ve taken a million reps, scrambled maybe half a million.”

Twelves, this is a genuinely underappreciated skill and I want to make sure you understand what was at stake on that play.

If a quarterback crosses the line of scrimmage and then throws the ball, that’s an illegal forward pass. Five yards and a loss of down. On a touchdown play, it erases the touchdown.

And there is no line painted on the grass where the line of scrimmage is. It’s an invisible line that moves every single snap.

So a scrambling quarterback, with men chasing him, eyes downfield, has to know — without looking down — exactly where an imaginary line is.

And Drew Lock’s answer for how he does it is: a million repetitions.

That’s it. There’s no trick. He has been in that situation enough times that his body knows where the line is without being told. That’s eight years in the NFL doing something invisible.

And then the actual decision he made is even better:

“Once I stepped out of the tackle, I was like, ‘All right, I’m going to SIT HERE.’ Because I didn’t think there was anyone really around me — and see if anybody works open.”

“I’m going to sit here.”

Twelves, that’s the hardest thing to do in football when the play breaks down.

Every instinct in your body says RUN. The play is dead, somebody just tried to tackle you, and the ground in front of you is empty.

And Drew Lock made the opposite choice. He escaped the tackle, scanned the field, decided nobody was close to him, and then STOPPED MOVING — and waited for a receiver to uncover.

That is exactly the thing Cooper Kupp described weeks ago: “the play really — the second play starts once he gets out of the pocket.”

And on this one, the second play worked. Lock sat. And here’s what happened:

“And Coupe did a great job of grabbing my eyes and running across that back end line and making the catch.”

“GRABBING MY EYES.”

Twelves, that’s the scramble drill, executed perfectly, by a nine-year veteran.

When a quarterback escapes, the receiver’s job isn’t to keep running his route — the route is dead. His job is to get into the quarterback’s field of vision and MOVE, so the quarterback’s eyes find him.

And Cooper Kupp ran across the back line of the end zone to do it.

And remember — Kupp publicly took responsibility for this exact thing after the Bears loss. His words: “we’ve got to do a better job of being able to get open and find ways to let him get the ball out of his hands when he does extend like that.”

He named his own failure in October. He executed it for a touchdown in November. That’s a professional.

TOUCHDOWN NUMBER TWO — and twelves, this one is the best football explanation anybody has given on this channel all season. I’m going to walk you through it slowly.

A reporter noticed that Jaxon Smith-Njigba went down over the middle, then came back — and asked whether Lock was about to throw it and had to reset.

Here’s the full answer:

“Yeah, we got matched front side on a concept that’s a little more meant for ZONE. But Jax is a good MAN BEATER for us coming back to it. And he got matched up with the MIKE. Was ready to throw it to him, saw the SLIP, worked out away — and then when we do that, Jax just starts running up field.”

Okay. Let me translate every piece of that.

“We got matched front side on a concept that’s a little more meant for zone.”

That means the play Seattle called was designed to beat ZONE coverage — where defenders guard areas of the field. And the Raiders played MAN coverage instead. So the play was wrong for the defense they got.

That happens constantly, and it’s why quarterbacking is hard.

“But Jax is a good man beater for us coming back to it.”

Translation: the play didn’t fit, but it doesn’t matter, because Jaxon Smith-Njigba beats man coverage anyway. That’s what you get when your number one receiver is one of the best in football — he turns a bad play call into a good one by himself.

“And he got matched up with the MIKE.”

Twelves, THIS is the moment the touchdown was created.

The “Mike” is the middle linebacker. And Jaxon Smith-Njigba got matched up on him in man coverage.

That is the single best matchup in football for an offense. A thousand-yard receiver against a middle linebacker, one on one, in space. That’s a mismatch so severe it’s almost unfair.

“Was ready to throw it to him, saw the SLIP, worked out away.”

Somebody slipped. So Lock came off the throw he was about to make and worked to another part of the field.

And then the magic: “and then when we do that, Jax just starts running up field.”

Twelves, that’s an unscripted, unspoken agreement between a quarterback and a receiver.

Nobody called that. Jaxon Smith-Njigba, covered by a linebacker, saw his quarterback’s eyes leave him — and instead of standing still or giving up on the play, HE JUST STARTED RUNNING UPFIELD.

Because he knew the quarterback would come back. And because he knew a linebacker cannot run with him.

And now the finish, and this is my favorite coaching detail of the entire season:

“Seven’s helmet — see the back of the helmet? That’s the CLASSIC. Just throw it AT the helmet, throw it OVER the helmet. He won’t be able to see it.”

“Throw it at the helmet.”

Let me explain, because this is a real quarterback rule and it’s brilliant.

When you’re throwing to a receiver running away from you with a defender in trail, the defender’s head position tells you everything. If you can see the BACK of his helmet, that means his head is turned downfield — which means he CANNOT see the football coming.

So you throw it right at him. Over the top of his helmet.

Because a defender who can’t see the ball can’t play the ball. He can’t jump. He can’t get a hand in. He just runs with the receiver while the ball sails over his head.

“That’s the classic.” Eight years in the league and he had the answer instantly.

And then the receiver’s half of it:

“And Jax does a great job of LATE HANDS — flashing hands LATE, not letting him get a hand out. And he kind of just did the rest after it got in his hands.”

“Late hands.”

Twelves, this is the thing elite receivers do that nobody at home ever notices.

If you put your hands up early, you’ve told the defender the ball is coming. He hears nothing, he sees nothing — until your hands go up. That’s the signal. And now he knows exactly when to turn and swipe.

So you keep your hands down, running like nothing’s happening, and you flash them at the last possible instant. By the time the defender reacts to your hands, the ball is already in them.

Throw it over the helmet. Flash the hands late. That’s a quarterback and a receiver separately executing the two halves of the same deception on the same play.

That’s the touchdown. And that’s why the best receiver in football is the best receiver in football.
