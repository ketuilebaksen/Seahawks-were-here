Aden Durde described exactly how the Chicago Bears would beat the Seattle Seahawks. He did it four days early. He did it out loud, at a podium, in front of reporters.

And then it happened, almost play for play.

Seahawks Nation, I’ve gone back through Seattle’s defensive coordinator’s press conference from Friday with the box score sitting next to it, and I need you to see this. The man called the game. He called the opening drive. He called the quarterback. He called the receiving distribution.

And there’s one single answer in there — one — where he waved something off. That’s where the game was lost.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Chicago 34, Seattle 17. The Bears are 6-2. Seattle is 4-4. And for the record, before anybody asks: I predicted 24-20 Seattle and I was wrong by twenty-four points. I owned that in my last video and I’m not going to re-litigate it here. Today is about what the coordinator told us.

Let’s go.

Prophecy number one, and it is uncanny. Asked about Chicago’s run game, Durde said this:

“It really stands out the way they operate and the way they run off the ball. The run game’s very good and schemed up well. They play with good technique and they work good combinations — but they play with a style, and they get on the ball fast. They make you think. And if you’re not ready and you’re not ready to operate, they punish you.”

“They punish you.”

Chicago’s first drive of the game: seventeen plays, eighty yards, eight minutes and thirty-two seconds. It ended with Caleb Williams throwing a one-yard touchdown to Luther Burden — Burden’s fourth catch on that single drive.

Seattle’s defense was on the field for eight and a half minutes before the offense ever touched the ball.

“If you’re not ready to operate, they punish you.” Four days early. Word for word.

Prophecy number two. Asked about Chicago’s receivers:

“They do have a good variation of players. You see guys that can run great routes — fifteen — and then I see the ability in guys like ten getting the ball in his hands and being able to get the yac after the catch. They do a great job of moving the ball around the field, getting the ball in different people’s hands, and making you work all the time.”

Twelves, he’s using jersey numbers because that’s how coaches watch film. Fifteen is Rome Odunze, Chicago’s route-runner. The man he called “ten” — a get-it-in-his-hands, yards-after-catch player — fits Luther Burden the Third, though I’ll be straight with you: Burden has changed his number more than once and I couldn’t confirm it to my own satisfaction, so I’m telling you that instead of pretending.

But look at what happened.

Caleb Williams threw to seven different receivers.

Luther Burden: seven catches, 56 yards, the opening touchdown. Colston Loveland: seven catches, 65 yards. Rome Odunze: four catches, 87 yards.

“Moving the ball around the field, getting the ball in different people’s hands, making you work all the time.”

Seven different targets. Three players with four or more catches. Nobody over 90 yards. Nobody to key on. That is precisely the offense Durde described, and it’s the hardest kind to defend, because there is no one man to double.

Prophecy number three. Somebody asked a brilliant question: if you didn’t know who the coach was, what on the film would tell you it’s a Ben Johnson offense?

Durde’s answer was four words: “Speed that they play with pre and post snap.”

And he expanded: “They operate fast and they do a good job of controlling situations.”

“Controlling situations.” Remember that phrase, because it’s about to become the whole video.

Prophecy number four. On Caleb Williams, whom Durde faced as a rookie two years ago:

“I think he’s a lot more decisive. He makes all the throws. He plays ahead of snaps at times. And when he’s filtering through and the pocket’s collapsing around him, he does an excellent job of navigating the pocket. You have to be on your stuff.”

Monday night: twenty-four of thirty-four. 247 yards. One touchdown. Zero interceptions. A 101.0 passer rating. One sack taken all night.

“A lot more decisive.” That’s the scouting report and that’s the box score. The same sentence.

Prophecy number five, and this one hurts: “They’re efficient on early downs and on third down. They do a good job, and the quarterback is very elusive and can play on time and off schedule.”

Efficient on early downs and third down. And how did Seattle do in that same department?

One for eleven on third down.

The coordinator identified Chicago’s superpower and Seattle lost the exact same battle on the other side of the ball.

Now — the one answer where he waved something off. And twelves, this is the center of the whole thing.

A reporter handed him the single most relevant fact about the Chicago Bears and asked him directly:

“The Bears run the most plays on offense. They dominate time of possession. How much does that come into the conversation or the game planning this week?”

And Aden Durde said: “Um, it does — and I would say less. I think it’s more about us, and how we attack different situations, how we play down and distances, and what our plan is.”

“I would say less. It’s more about us.”

Now let me give you the numbers he was being handed.

The Chicago Bears lead the entire NFL in time of possession at thirty-five minutes and thirty seconds a game. And they lead the league in total offensive plays with 505 — by far the most of any team. They are the only team in football averaging more than seventy offensive plays per game.

And then Chicago opened the game with a seventeen-play drive that ate eight and a half minutes.

And here’s the part that makes this genuinely fascinating: Ben Johnson doesn’t believe in the stat either.

His own words about his own league-leading time of possession: “I don’t know that that really equates to winning and losing, necessarily.” And: “I would rather have a one-play drive for a touchdown than a ten-play drive for a touchdown.”

So both coordinators in this game publicly dismissed time of possession. And then time of possession decided it.

But twelves — I’m not going to cheap-shot Aden Durde, because “it’s more about us” is the correct coaching answer and I want to explain why.

You cannot game-plan against a statistic. You can’t call a defense that produces time of possession. Possession is an outcome, not an input — it’s what happens after you win first and second down, after you convert third down, after you stop somebody on third down.

Durde was right that it’s about situations. And then Seattle lost the situations. Chicago converted on early downs and Seattle went one-of-eleven on third. The possession gap wasn’t a strategy that beat Seattle — it was the scoreboard of every individual down, added up.

The philosophy was right. The execution lost. Those are different problems, and only one of them is fixable in a week.

Now the best football content in the whole press conference, and it’s about Nick Emmanwori.

First, a fact I didn’t have until now: Nick Emmanwori played every single snap against Kansas City.

Every snap. The man who attends three position meeting rooms a week — linebackers Wednesday, defensive line Thursday or Friday, defensive backs every day — played one hundred percent of the defensive snaps in a game where his hip was on the injury report.

And Durde explained what that unlocks: “Having Nick on the field gives you the ability to give him opportunities in different parts of the field because he’s so versatile. But we also have a lot of versatile players.” And he named them — “Spoon can move in different places” (Devon Witherspoon), “Leo can move in different places” (Leonard Williams), “Drake” (Drake Thomas), and “T9” (Tyrice Knight).

“So just the versatility throughout the defense allows us to kind of be who we want to be on different down and distances.”

And then the detail on the interception that I’ve been waiting two weeks for.

A reporter described the play Ty Okada intercepted: it looked like Emmanwori “had a gap blitz, or he was at least over the center — and he went from the right hash mark to the left.”

From the right hash to the left hash. Across the entire middle of the field, to chase down Patrick Mahomes.

The reporter asked whether that was coached — are you telling him, hey, if they go, you’ve got to get out there?

Durde’s answer: “No. He just — I think that’s ability. That’s how unique that play is. He has unique closing speed. It’s hard to judge, I think, players — when he closes, it’s hard to judge how fast he can close, because he can cover a lot of ground in a short amount of time.”

“That’s not coached. That’s ability.”

And remember what Emmanwori said about that play himself: “On that play I had like an inside blitz, so it wasn’t really my responsibility. But right when I see him pull the ball, I was like, ‘Oh man, it’s on.’”

The player said it wasn’t his assignment. The coordinator said it wasn’t coachable. They’re describing the same forty yards.

And then Durde gave the single cleanest description of Emmanwori’s value that exists:

“You can line him up as a wide nine one snap, and he can cover a 4-2 guy on a slot fade — like he did last weekend — the very next.”

Let me translate. “Wide nine” is an alignment outside the offensive tackle’s shoulder — it’s where you put a pure speed pass rusher. “A 4-2 guy” means a man who runs a 4.2-second forty — that’s Xavier Worthy, the fastest combine time in history. And a “slot fade” is a deep ball from inside the formation.

So: edge rusher on one play, and chasing the fastest man in football down the field on the next. That’s two sports. On consecutive snaps.

Now the most honest thing I have ever heard a coordinator say about himself.

Asked what having a player like that lets a defensive coordinator do, Durde said this:

“It allows you to create versatility in your defense. But also, you have to be cognizant of yourself to say: okay, why are you doing this? Just to kind of look cool, or scheme something up? And you’ve always got to take it back to Nick, and take it back to the players that have the ability to do these different things, and ask yourself: are they playing as fast as they possibly can? Are they comfortable? Are you overloading their plate?”

“Why are you doing this? Just to kind of look cool?”

Twelves, that is a defensive coordinator publicly asking whether his own play calls are vanity. Most coaches at that level would never say that sentence into a microphone. And he finished it the right way: “I think it’s about putting him in situations where he can be himself and show his talents.”

That’s the third week in a row somebody in that building has said the same thing about Emmanwori. Macdonald: “without overloading him.” Emmanwori himself: “keeping me limited where I’m not burning myself out.” Durde: “are you overloading their plate?”

Three men, three rooms, one sentence. That’s an organization that actually agrees on something.

And the “wheelhouse” answer, which explains how this defense got good.

Asked whether three years of continuity has made them deeper in what they do or broadened it:

“I think it’s a deeper understanding of how you get attacked, and how you play, and what different situations and looks talk to you within the game. It allows players to kind of play at times ahead of the snap — and ultimately be allowed to do what we say: play 12-as-one football and attack the football.”

“What different situations and looks talk to you.” That’s a beautiful way to put it. The film talks to you if you’ve been listening for three years.

And “play at times ahead of the snap” is the payoff of continuity — recognition becomes anticipation. It’s the same thing Caleb Williams does, which Durde praised in the same press conference: “he plays ahead of snaps at times.”

Now the coaching points against a mobile quarterback, and this is where I have to be honest about the result.

“You have to create a rush plan, a pressure plan. You have to understand who has what. And you can’t play like robots in these situations. You can’t go in and say, okay, we’re playing a certain guy and we’re going to slow down and play like this — because you take away all the good stuff that the players have and how they attack the pocket. It’s just about being connected, playing fast, and understanding how each other play and play off each other.”

“You can’t play like robots.”

And here’s the result: Seattle sacked Caleb Williams one time.

Chicago sacked Sam Darnold three times, with a thirty-one percent pressure rate, per Next Gen Stats. Jeffery Simmons alone — in his Bears debut, on forty-nine defensive snaps — had six tackles, two for loss, a sack and a pass breakup.

So the rush plan worked better on the other sideline. Durde had the right philosophy about not playing like robots — and Chicago’s new defensive tackle outplayed everything Seattle did up front. That’s not a scheme failure. That’s a player Chicago bought for two first-round picks being worth it in week three.

And one more answer that tells you what this defensive room is.

Asked about Tyrice Knight, who keeps getting pushed down the lineup and keeps playing well when called:

“It tells you that they’re a Seahawks player. That’s the expectation for everyone in the defensive room. And I think for Mike, it’s the expectation of everyone on the team — when your name’s called, you’ve got to be prepared, ready, and you’ve got to be ready to execute.”

“It tells you that they’re a Seahawks player.”

That’s the same philosophy Macdonald gave us in his own words: “I’m on the 70. I have a responsibility, and when given the opportunity, I’m going to be ready to rock.”

And on Connor O’Toole, with Uchenna Nwosu doubtful: “Connor’s doing a great job on special teams. He does a great job in practice. He just keeps building, keeps working on his craft. And I think he’s done a much better job on early downs this year — getting stronger in his lower half, setting edges and playing the run.”

“Getting stronger in his lower half, setting edges.” That’s a real, specific, unglamorous development note about a special-teamer. Coaches who say things like that are paying attention.

Now let me be fair to this defense about Monday night, because the picture is mixed.

Thirty-four points is the most Seattle’s defense has allowed all season. That’s a fact and it’s bad.

But: the streak lived. D’Andre Swift needed twenty-five-plus carries to get into the sixties — about two and a half yards a crack — which makes thirty-seven consecutive games without allowing a hundred-yard rusher. His three touchdowns came from one yard, five yards and five yards. The run defense won the field and lost the goal line.

And they were on that field all night, because the offense went one-of-eleven on third down and finished with 252 total yards and 81 rushing yards.

You cannot ask eleven men to hold a seventy-play offense when your own offense can’t stay on the grass.

Two more honest notes. Darnold’s interception — his ninth of the season — was tipped at the line and caught by James Lynch. That’s not a bad read; that’s a batted ball. And Jaxon Smith-Njigba finished with six catches for 118 yards and the 43-yard touchdown — which puts him at 985 receiving yards through eight games and past Lance Alworth, exactly as I said he would be.

Now the title, and here’s the verdict.

There is no bombshell decision from Aden Durde. He announced nothing. No scheme change, no benching, no ultimatum. He answered eleven questions about an opponent and his own players, and one of them was about cold hands.

And this transcript is from Friday, before a game that has now been played and lost. So if you’re seeing a pregame breakdown anywhere today, it’s a day late.

But what Durde actually gave us is more valuable than a bombshell: a scouting report that came true. The punishment, the ball distribution, the decisive quarterback, the early-down efficiency. All of it. Four days early.

And that’s the real lesson of this loss, and it isn’t a comfortable one: Seattle knew exactly what was coming and could not stop it.

Knowing is not the same as beating. The 2026 Seahawks just learned that at home, in prime time, by seventeen.
