March sixteenth.

Seahawks Nation, write that date down, because it is the single most important date in the future of this franchise and almost nobody is talking about it.

March sixteenth is when the Seattle Seahawks have to decide whether Sam Darnold is their quarterback in 2027.

And here’s the number attached to that decision: a thirty-five and a half million dollar salary, plus a ten million dollar roster bonus due the very next day, March seventeenth.

Forty-five and a half million dollars in cash. For one season.

And here’s the amount of that money Seattle is currently obligated to pay:

Zero.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. And today we’re doing the real Sam Darnold question — not the fake one.

Because I have now been asked five separate times to make a video about Sam Darnold being tired and wanting out of Seattle. And every time, I’ve gone and looked, and every time the answer is the same: there is nothing there.

No report. No insider. No beat writer. No anonymous source. And his own press conferences point the opposite direction — this is the man who, after the San Francisco loss, walked into his offensive coordinator’s office and asked to focus the playbook on what the offense does best. That’s not a man packing a bag.

But here’s the thing: there IS a scenario where Sam Darnold isn’t a Seahawk in 2027. It’s real, it’s on paper, and it has a date on it.

It just has nothing to do with how he feels. It has to do with forty-five and a half million dollars.

Let’s go through it properly.

First, the contract, laid out cleanly — because you’re going to see three different numbers for the same season and I want you to know why.

In March of 2025, Sam Darnold signed with Seattle for three years and one hundred million, five hundred thousand dollars. That’s an average of thirty-three and a half million a year.

Fifty-five million was guaranteed in total, with thirty-seven and a half million fully guaranteed at signing.

Now 2026, this season. And here’s where people get confused, because there are three legitimate figures:

His base salary is twelve million, three hundred thousand dollars. His cash compensation for the year is about twenty-seven and a half million, with another four million available in incentives. And his salary cap number is thirty-seven million, nine hundred thousand dollars — twelve point one percent of Seattle’s entire cap.

Those are all true at once. Base salary is what he’s paid to play. Cash is base plus bonuses actually received. Cap number includes the prorated signing bonus, which is why it’s the biggest. When somebody tells you a quarterback “makes” a number, always ask which of the three they mean.

And now 2027 — the year everything hinges on.

Base salary: thirty-five million, five hundred thousand dollars.
Cap number: forty-four million, nine hundred thousand — thirteen point seven percent of the cap.
Plus a ten million dollar roster bonus due March seventeenth, 2027.

And guaranteed money for 2027: zero dollars.

ESPN’s Dan Graziano put it as plainly as it can be put: “He has no money guaranteed beyond 2026.”

So Seattle’s position is enviable and brutal at the same time. They hold a quarterback who won them a Super Bowl, on a deal that obligates them to absolutely nothing after this season. They can walk away in March and owe nothing new.

Now here’s the detail nobody has covered, and it’s the smartest thing in this entire contract.

Seattle deliberately built itself extra time to make this decision.

Look at last year’s structure. Seventeen and a half million dollars of Darnold’s 2026 money became fully guaranteed on the fifth day after the Super Bowl. Five days. That’s almost no runway — the confetti is still on the floor and you’ve already committed.

This time, the decision on his 2027 salary isn’t due until March sixteenth. Graziano flagged exactly this: the setup gives Seattle roughly four more weeks to decide than it had a year ago.

Four extra weeks. That’s not an accident. That is a front office that lived through a five-day deadline once and said never again.

Think about what those four weeks buy. The combine happens. Free agency opens. The draft board takes shape. Seattle gets to see what the quarterback market actually costs before committing to its own quarterback. That is enormously valuable, and it was written into the paper on purpose.

And it tells you something about how this front office thinks, which brings us to the real constraint.

Seattle has a policy, and the policy is the whole story.

Graziano’s reporting on it is specific: the Seahawks don’t build future-year guarantees into veteran contracts, and they are “deliberate about the way in which they value quarterbacks.”

And there’s a second piece, reported by Brady Henderson: Seattle doesn’t extend players with more than a year left on their deals.

Put those two together and the whole picture snaps into focus. Darnold has a year left after this one. So by policy, Seattle isn’t negotiating an extension right now — not because of how he’s playing, but because that’s not how they do business. The conversation happens next spring or it doesn’t happen.

And that is exactly what John Schneider said when he was asked about revisiting the deal. Five words:

“Sam signed a three-year deal.”

That’s it. That’s the whole answer. No warmth, no coldness, no door opened, no door closed. A general manager pointing at a document. And at the time people read it as dismissive. It wasn’t. It was accurate. Schneider was telling you the structure, and the structure says March.

Now the other side of the equation: what would it actually cost to keep him long-term?

Gregg Bell of the Tacoma News Tribune has written that a Super Bowl-winning quarterback should command fifty million dollars a year or more to stay.

Fifty million. And honestly, that number is not crazy in this market — it’s roughly where the top of the quarterback market has gone.

So look at the two paths side by side.

Path one: Seattle plays out 2027 at forty-five and a half million in cash, with no long-term commitment and full freedom afterward.

Path two: Seattle extends him at something approaching fifty million a year, which buys certainty and costs flexibility for years.

And path three: Seattle moves on. Release or trade. The dead money figures appear to be in the neighborhood of nineteen million before June first and around six million after — and I’ll say honestly that the public tables on this are messy enough that I’d want them confirmed before I quoted them as gospel. But directionally: the post-June-first exit is cheap. Seattle also has void years running through 2029 on this deal, which is how the cap charge was smoothed out in the first place.

And let me explain why that $37.9 million cap number this season is the hidden pressure in all of this, because it constrains everything else Seattle does.

Twelve point one percent of Seattle’s entire salary cap is currently attached to one player.

That’s not unusual for a starting quarterback — it’s roughly the going rate. But think about what it means in combination with everything else this front office committed to. Leonard Williams at ninety million. Witherspoon at a hundred and thirty-two. And a quarterback eating an eighth of the cap.

That’s why Seattle has roughly twelve point eight million dollars in space right now, and it’s why the answer to the run game was a fullback off the street instead of a trade. The money is already spoken for.

And now look at 2027: forty-four million, nine hundred thousand — thirteen point seven percent. The number goes up, not down, if nothing changes.

So Seattle’s March decision isn’t really “is Sam Darnold good enough.” It’s: are we spending nearly fourteen percent of our cap on this position, in this year, with this roster getting more expensive around it?

That’s a very different question, and it’s the one that actually gets asked in that building. A general manager isn’t grading a quarterback in a vacuum. He’s deciding what else he can’t afford if he says yes.

And one more mechanic worth knowing, because it explains the shape of the whole deal: void years. Darnold’s contract carries them through 2029. A void year is a contract year added purely to spread signing-bonus proration further and lower the cap hit in the real years. Nobody plays in it. But the money doesn’t disappear — it comes due whenever the contract actually ends.

Which means Seattle already borrowed from 2028 and 2029 to make 2025 and 2026 affordable. That’s not a scandal; almost every team does it. It just means the bill for Sam Darnold arrives no matter which path they choose. The only question is whether they get football in exchange for it.

And now the honest part, because this is where it gets uncomfortable.

Every interception Sam Darnold throws between now and January moves that number in the wrong direction.

Here’s where he actually is through five starts: seven touchdowns, seven interceptions, and a 77.4 passer rating.

Last season — the championship season — he finished at 99.1. That’s a drop of more than twenty points of passer rating from his own baseline.

And Pro Football Focus has charged him with ten turnover-worthy plays this year. Not seven. Ten. Meaning three more balls could have been intercepted and weren’t.

And sitting right behind him: Drew Lock, who went thirty-five of forty-eight for 422 yards with four touchdowns and zero interceptions in his two appearances. Seattle went 2-0.

That combination — the starter’s rating collapsing while the backup posts four scores and no picks — is exactly the combination that makes a front office comfortable walking away in March.

And yet. Here’s the thing that outweighs all of it, and Graziano named it.

Asked what would force Seattle’s hand on an extension, Graziano’s answer was that a second strong season could change the thinking — but that winning another Super Bowl would matter more than individual statistics, because nothing outranks a repeat title.

Nothing outranks a repeat title.

So read that carefully, because it reframes everything. Sam Darnold’s leverage is not his passer rating. It’s a second ring. If Seattle wins a championship in February with Sam Darnold as its quarterback, nobody in that building is going to look at a 77.4 passer rating from October and let him leave.

Which means the answer to the 2027 question does not get decided in October. It gets decided in January. And that’s exactly how Macdonald has been talking all month: “We’re not in the business of comparing this year to last year.” His quarterback used the identical phrase. That’s an organization that talks to itself.

And here’s the piece of this that I think is genuinely underrated: the four extra weeks aren’t just about watching the market. They’re about watching the playoffs.

Think about the sequence. The Super Bowl is played in early February. Under last year’s structure, Seattle had five days after that game to commit seventeen and a half million dollars. Five days is not long enough to be objective about anything — you are either euphoric or devastated, and neither state makes good decisions. Macdonald said almost exactly this about the Denver loss: “You’re frustrated, but also without the facts, and without the objectivity behind everything.”

March sixteenth is six weeks after the Super Bowl. The emotion is gone. The tape has been watched four times. The free agent class is public. You know what the market actually pays, and you know how your own season actually ended.

That’s a front office protecting itself from its own feelings — and after watching this organization operate for a month, that is exactly in character. It’s the same instinct as “we’re not in the business of comparing this year to last year,” and the same instinct as a head coach saying “the result doesn’t tell you you’re sweet.”

This building makes decisions on process, not on how Sunday felt. And they wrote that philosophy into a quarterback contract.

Let me add one more factor that matters and almost nobody mentions: age.

Darnold is in his age-29 season. So the 2027 decision is about a quarterback who’ll be thirty — and the extension question after that is about his early thirties.

That’s the single hardest call in roster building. You’re not paying for what a player did. You’re paying for years you haven’t seen yet, at the exact age when quarterbacks either level off or fall off a cliff — and there’s no reliable way to tell which in advance.

And Seattle has lived the other side of this. They watched what happened when a championship roster got expensive all at once. They paid Leonard Williams ninety million at thirty-two. They paid Devon Witherspoon a hundred and thirty-two million. And then Kenneth Walker the Third walked out the door and is now the first player since Jim Brown in 1963 to start a season the way he has — in Kansas City, playing against them tonight.

That’s the cost of being a champion. Every good player gets expensive at the same time, and the cap doesn’t grow because you won.

So here’s the honest summary of Sam Darnold’s future, and I want you to have it clean.

He is not trying to leave. There is zero evidence of that, and five separate times I’ve looked.

But he is one season away from a decision Seattle has given itself four extra weeks to make, on forty-five and a half million dollars it does not owe him, under a policy that forbids negotiating before March, with a market that would cost fifty million a year to beat.

That is the real cliffhanger. And it’s genuinely scarier than the fake one, because it’s on paper, it has a date, and nobody’s feelings can change it.

Here’s what actually moves the needle, in order.

One: a second championship. Everything else is noise next to that. Graziano said it, and he’s right.

Two: the interception rate from here. Seven in five starts, ten turnover-worthy plays. If that number comes down over the back half of the season, the conversation changes completely. If it doesn’t, March gets very easy for Seattle.

Three: the offensive line. And this is why I keep hammering it. More than half of Darnold’s passes in Denver came out in under two and a half seconds — and he got hit more inside that window than he ever had. He is throwing faster than ever and getting hit anyway. Anthony Bradford is back tonight and the starting five is whole for the first time in five weeks. If the pressure rate drops and the picks drop with it, Darnold just made himself tens of millions of dollars.

Four: Drew Lock’s contract situation. Because the cheapest version of the future for Seattle is a backup who’s shown he can win games. Macdonald already said it out loud: “Drew is a tremendous quarterback. He’s a starting-level quarterback.” A front office that believes that has less reason to pay fifty million.

Let me close.

I was asked five times to tell you Sam Darnold wants out. He doesn’t, and I won’t.

But I’ll tell you the true version, and it’s a better story.

Sam Darnold is playing for forty-five and a half million dollars and a deadline. Not this Sunday’s forty-five million — next March’s. Every third down he converts and every ball he keeps away from a safety between now and February is an argument in a negotiation that hasn’t started yet, with a front office that wrote itself four extra weeks to listen to it.

And the only argument that wins outright is a parade.

His own words on where his head is: “Everything that happens in your life prepares you for the next moment. But staying in the moment is sometimes the hardest thing to do... just being exactly where our feet are.”

Exactly where our feet are. Tonight that’s Lumen Field, against the 5-0 Kansas City Chiefs, with the right guard back and the man who won his Super Bowl MVP wearing red.

Go through, not around.

Twelves, two questions, and these are the good ones. First: if Sam Darnold finishes this season at a 77 passer rating but Seattle makes a deep January run — do you pay him forty-five and a half million for 2027? Yes or no, no hedging. And second, the harder one: would you rather have Sam Darnold at fifty million a year through his early thirties, or Drew Lock at a fraction of it plus a first-round pick to spend somewhere else?
