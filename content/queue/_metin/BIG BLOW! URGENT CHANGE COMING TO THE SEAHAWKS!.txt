There is a big blow this week, twelves, and it landed on exactly one man.

Anthony Bradford is on the bench, and he is four months away from free agency.

That’s the real story, and almost nobody is doing the math on it. So let me do it, with the actual contract numbers — and then I’m going to tell you something about Anthony Bradford that complicates this whole thing, because I’m not in the business of telling you only the half that feels good.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seahawks and Bears tonight, 8:15 Eastern, Lumen Field, Monday Night Football. Seattle is 4-3, defending Super Bowl champions. Let’s get into it.

First — the facts of his situation, because they’re specific.

Anthony Bradford was a fourth-round pick in 2023. Number 108 overall, out of LSU.

He is in the final year of that rookie contract, on a salary of about $3.67 million. And this isn’t news that broke this week — Bob Condotta of the Seattle Times reported back in August that there was no “hint” of an extension, and that Bradford was likely headed to free agency next March.

So Anthony Bradford knew, in training camp, that 2026 was his audition.

Then here’s how his season has gone: he missed five games. He came back from a knee injury to start against the Kansas City Chiefs. Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards, allowed zero sacks, and held the ball for thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds. Mike Macdonald volunteered — unprompted — that “our offensive line probably played their best game of the season this past Sunday night.”

And four days later, Seattle traded for Quenton Nelson, and Anthony Bradford lost his job.

Now here’s why the timing is genuinely brutal, and it’s about how offensive linemen get paid.

Guards don’t get paid for potential. They get paid for recent starting snaps. When a team is deciding whether to hand a 24-year-old guard a multi-year contract in March, the single thing that moves the number is how much good film exists from the season that just ended.

Bradford’s film stopped growing in Week 7.

He has nine or ten games left in his contract year and he’s going to spend them in a helmet on the sideline. Every week he doesn’t play, the pile of 2026 tape stays the same size while every other free-agent guard’s pile gets bigger.

And he has no leverage whatsoever. He’s not getting tagged — nobody franchise-tags a backup guard. There’s no extension coming; Condotta told us that in August. He is going to walk into the open market in March with a resume that ends in October.

That’s the blow. And it’s real.

But now I have to be honest with you, because there’s a second set of numbers and they change the picture.

Pro Football Focus graded Anthony Bradford the third-worst pass-blocking guard in the entire NFL in 2025.

And they ranked him 71st out of 79 qualified guards overall.

And they gave him poor grades in his first two seasons as well.

Twelves, I’m not going to pretend I didn’t find that. Seattle won a Super Bowl last season with a right guard that the advanced numbers considered one of the worst pass protectors in football.

And that is the actual reason this trade happened.

It wasn’t personal. It wasn’t a message. It was the longest-standing weakness on this roster finally getting fixed — and it got fixed for a third-round pick and a fifth-round pick.

Think about the last five weeks through that lens. Sam Darnold was sacked fourteen times in six games. Five against San Francisco. Five against Denver. Seattle ranked twenty-ninth in the league in rush EPA. Everybody screamed for a running back.

The problem was interior offensive line play, and the numbers had been saying so for two years.

And yet — here’s the complication, and this is why I respect Anthony Bradford.

He has gotten better every single year, and he’s done it by beating people out.

As a rookie in 2023 he started ten of fourteen games, filling in for an injured Phil Haynes. In 2024 he won the starting job outright over Christian Haynes — the third-round pick Seattle drafted to replace him — before a knee injury limited him to eleven games. And in 2025 he was a full-time starter on a Super Bowl-winning football team, and he played the third-most regular-season snaps of any Seattle offensive player.

Third-most. On a champion.

That is not a man who was given anything. He has been handed a job once and had to win it twice. And the week he finally came back healthy in 2026, his line had its best game of the year.

So which is it? Both. Both things are true at once. Anthony Bradford is a genuinely limited pass protector by the advanced numbers and a tough, improving, durable starter who helped win a championship. Macdonald said exactly that, in his own language: “Two things can be true at the same time.”

Now the trade idea, and I have to label this properly.

The version going around is that Seattle could trade Bradford before the November tenth deadline and “easily recoup that fifth-round pick we shipped out.”

That is speculation, not a report. Nobody — not Schefter, not Rapoport, not Pelissero, not Condotta — has reported that Seattle is shopping Anthony Bradford. Zero sources. It’s a fan idea, and it’s a reasonable one to discuss, but you should know it’s an idea.

And when you test it against reality, it falls apart.

What is a backup guard actually worth eight days before a deadline?

He’s on an expiring deal, so the acquiring team gets ten games and nothing else. Most of his $3.67 million base salary has already been paid out, so there’s barely any cash left to inherit — but there’s also no future to buy. And the public evaluation of him is third-worst pass-blocking guard in the league.

Nobody is giving up a fifth-round pick for ten games of that. The honest market for Anthony Bradford right now is a late-round conditional at absolute best, and realistically nothing at all.

And here’s the argument that ends the conversation for me.

Charles Cross is out tonight. Seattle is starting Josh Jones at left tackle. Grey Zabel is playing right guard for the first time in his professional life. Quenton Nelson has been in the building for six days and described Seattle’s offense as “completely different” from what he knows.

Three of five offensive line spots are new tonight.

And Anthony Bradford is the only man on this roster with forty career starts at right guard.

You do not sell your insurance policy in the same week you create the risk. If Zabel’s move to the right side doesn’t take — and Macdonald himself called it “a process” that they have to “attack the heck out of” — then Bradford walks back in and plays the position he’s started forty games at. That is worth infinitely more than a conditional seventh-round pick.

And before anybody says Seattle has depth behind him: the other guard in the building is Beau Stephens, a 2026 fifth-round pick out of Iowa. He is a developmental player, not a contingency plan. Christian Haynes, the other option, got waived last week and Miami claimed him inside twenty-four hours.

Seattle’s guard depth is Anthony Bradford. Trading him would be replacing a safety net with a draft pick that lands in eighteen months.

And here’s the argument I have not heard anybody make, and I think it’s the best one. If Seattle lets Anthony Bradford walk in March, they might get a draft pick anyway.

Compensatory picks.

Here’s how they work, and it’s the most underrated mechanism in the sport. Each offseason the league awards extra draft picks to teams that lost more in free agency than they signed. It’s a formula built mainly on the size of the contracts involved — the bigger the deal your departing player signs somewhere else, the higher the pick you get back. They run from the third round to the seventh, a team can earn up to four of them, and they’re awarded the following spring.

So the real choice in front of John Schneider is not “a late pick now or nothing.”

It’s: a conditional late pick today — or keep the insurance for a championship run, let him sign a real contract in March, and potentially collect a compensatory pick for 2028 instead.

And remember the rule I gave you two weeks ago: you cannot trade a compensatory pick before it’s awarded. They don’t exist as assets until the league hands them out. Which means a comp pick isn’t a thing Seattle can spend at this deadline — but it is absolutely a thing Seattle can earn by doing nothing.

Keep the player. Keep the depth. Collect the pick later. That’s the move, and it isn’t close.

And let me put real stakes on his contract year, because the numbers in that market are staggering.

You already know the top of it: Quenton Nelson wanted to be the highest-paid interior offensive lineman in football, and that bar is Tyler Linderbaum at around $27 million a year. That’s the ceiling for an All-Pro.

Anthony Bradford is not in that conversation and never was. He’s currently earning $3.67 million. But the gap between the two ends of the guard market — between proven starter and useful backup — is enormous, and it is measured in tens of millions of dollars across a single contract.

And the thing that decides which side of that gap you land on is your most recent film.

That’s the cost of this bench, stated plainly. It isn’t pride. It’s the difference between a multi-year starter’s contract and a one-year prove-it deal — and it got decided for him by a phone call between two front offices that he had no part in.

Which is why what Macdonald said matters more than it sounds. When a head coach says publicly that a benched player “earned a significant role in our team — a starting-level role,” he isn’t being nice. He is putting a sentence on the record that Anthony Bradford’s agent can hand to thirty-one other teams in March.

Now two corrections, and one of them is mine.

Correction one, on the transcript going around: Grey Zabel is not a rookie.

He’s in his second season. He was Seattle’s 2025 first-round pick, and he has started all twenty-four games of his career at left guard. He also played all five offensive line positions at North Dakota State.

That matters, because “rookie slides to a new position in prime time” sounds terrifying, and “second-year first-round pick with twenty-four starts who played every spot in college moves one position over” sounds like a normal Tuesday. Macdonald’s own words: “He’s had a lot of experience playing on the right side, so that’ll help with the learning curve.”

Correction two is mine, and I owe you this one.

In my last two videos I said Chicago would be playing tonight without its starting running back. That came from Kyle Monangai being ruled out with a thumb and toe injury, and I ran with it.

That was too strong. Chicago’s leading rusher this season is D’Andre Swift, with 476 rushing yards and seven touchdowns. Monangai being out matters, but the Bears’ primary back is playing.

So the run-stopping job tonight is harder than I told you. Seattle’s streak — thirty-six straight games without allowing a hundred-yard rusher — is going to be tested by a real NFL starter, not a backup. I got that wrong and I’m fixing it on the record, because if I’m going to correct other people’s transcripts every week, I have to correct my own.

Now how this team has actually treated Anthony Bradford, because this is the part that matters to me most.

Mike Macdonald, asked directly what he tells him:

“It’s not an easy situation. I thought Anthony played really good football for us, and he’s earned a significant role in our team — a starting-level role. So it’s one of those situations with AB that is not easy. Really no way to sugarcoat it.”

And: “I think he believes this when we say this is how much we love him, his teammates — the guys love him in the locker room. He’s a fantastic teammate. We don’t expect him to be jumping for joy in this situation, because that’s not realistic. But he’s going to be a great teammate and he’s going to stay ready. And we know how this league works. You’re a play away from him playing every play again.”

And: “He’s a tough cookie. The guys will have his back, and we’re behind him as well. But it’s not easy.”

“No way to sugarcoat it.” No cliché, no insult, no pretending.

Leonard Williams said he’s been advising both Quenton Nelson on joining a new organization and Anthony Bradford on the situation. The same veteran, helping the man who arrived and the man who got displaced, in the same week.

And Sam Darnold went out of his way at his own podium: the offensive line has welcomed Nelson “with open arms” and “they’ve been handling the situation with grace.”

Three different men, in three different press conferences, made sure Anthony Bradford’s name was said with respect. That’s not an accident. That’s a culture.

And one wrinkle nobody has mentioned: Anthony Bradford is listed as questionable tonight with a knee.

So the man who lost his starting job four days ago is also on the injury report. If he’s inactive, that’s a bad week stacked on a bad week. If he plays, he plays as the backup, on the knee, with free agency four months out. Either way, that’s a hard Monday.

Now the title, and here’s my verdict.

“BIG BLOW” is accurate — it’s just a blow to one player, not to the team. The Seahawks got dramatically better. Anthony Bradford got dramatically worse off. Both at the same time.

But “urgent change coming” and “emergency call” and “news just broke”? No. Nothing broke. There was no emergency call. Macdonald announced the line shuffle at a Wednesday press conference six days ago and explained it calmly for ten minutes.

The one genuinely urgent change is happening today, and it isn’t Bradford: it’s Jadarian Price. The healthy rookie first-round pick is still on injured reserve, and activating him requires clearing a spot on the 53-man roster — a spot Seattle no longer has, because Nelson took it.

Macdonald’s words: “We got some things going on roster-wise that we’re working through right now.” And when pressed: “We’re just going to let the transaction speak for itself on that one.”

That transaction lands this afternoon. Somebody’s name is about to be in a sentence nobody wants to be in.

My pick stays Seahawks 24, Bears 20.

Chicago is 26th in run defense DVOA and allows 4.4 yards a carry, against a Seattle offense that just ran forty-one times for 153 yards. Seattle’s defense is first in the NFL in opponent yards per play and allows the fewest explosive passes in football — the direct antidote to Caleb Williams, who has thrown for 300-plus in back-to-back weeks.

The thing that beats us is pressure and turnovers. Chicago is fourth in pressure rate with ten takeaways, they just added Jeffery Simmons for two first-round picks, and four of Sam Darnold’s eight interceptions have come with a defender in his face — with a backup at left tackle. For the record, ESPN’s own model has this at 55 percent Seattle. That’s a coin flip with a lean, not a formality.

Let me close.

A fourth-round pick from LSU won a starting job twice, played the third-most offensive snaps on a Super Bowl-winning team, got graded one of the worst pass-blocking guards in football, came back from a knee injury to help produce the best offensive line game of Seattle’s season, and lost his job four days later to a six-time All-Pro — four months before free agency.

All of that is true. That’s the NFL, and it’s harder than the highlight reels let on.

And if I’m him, here’s what I hold onto: “I was replaced by a surefire Hall of Famer, on a team that just won a Super Bowl, and my head coach said publicly that I’d earned a starting-level role.” That’s not a red flag on a resume. In March, that’s a paragraph that gets you paid.

Twelves, two questions. First, the one the other channel asked, and it’s a good one: would you trade Anthony Bradford for a late pick, or keep him as insurance for a championship run? I keep him, every time — Seattle has three new starters on that line tonight and he’s the only guard on the roster who’s started forty games.

And second: do you want Seattle to re-sign him in March? Because that’s the real question. A backup guard who’s beaten out two draft picks and won a ring is a useful man to have around — if the price is right.
