More than half of Sam Darnold’s passes in Denver came out in under two and a half seconds.

And he got hit more in that window than he ever had in his career.

Seahawks Nation, stop and read that twice, because it destroys the single most popular opinion about this football team.

For two weeks the entire internet has said Sam Darnold is holding the ball too long. He wasn’t. He was getting rid of it faster than he ever has — and getting hit anyway.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. It’s game day. Kansas City tonight. And I’ve got two real press conferences to go through — Darnold on Thursday, Macdonald on Friday — plus the final injury news, including the one piece of genuine good news this offense has been waiting five weeks for.

But first I have to be straight with you about something, because I owe you that more than I owe anybody a headline.

There is no indication whatsoever that Sam Darnold wants to leave this team. None. Not a report, not a hint, not a source.

And I’m not guessing — I have his press conference from Thursday in front of me. Here is what he actually said, in his own words, three days ago:

“Really happy with where we’re at.”

“I’m always trusting Flurry. He’s a very smart dude that’s seen a lot of football... I trust every single play that he calls.”

And the detail that settles it completely: after the San Francisco loss, Sam Darnold went to his offensive coordinator and asked to hone in on the plays the offense runs best. He initiated that. He walked into Brian Fleury’s office with an idea about the playbook.

A quarterback who wants out does not do that. A quarterback who wants out keeps his head down, protects his own numbers, and says nothing. He does not volunteer to redesign the offense he’s leaving.

So I’m not making that video. And honestly, what he actually said is a better video — because the man answered the exact question in the title. He told you what Seattle has to fix. Let’s go get it.

Here’s what Sam Darnold revealed, in one word: consistency. And then he got specific, which is the part that matters.

He said: “The biggest thing for us is just consistency. Consistency day in, day out.”

Fine. Every athlete says that. But then he explained precisely what he means, and it’s the most useful sentence anybody in that building has said all month:

“That’s just sustaining drives. It’s like having six or — you know, six, seven, eight plays that we’re stringing together and then not being able to really convert a third down on that eighth play.”

Read that again, because it is a diagnosis, not a cliché.

Seattle’s problem is not that the offense can’t move the football. Seattle moves it fine. In Denver they outgained the Broncos 353 to 168 — more than double — and lost.

The problem is the eighth play. Seattle strings together six, seven, eight good snaps, gets into scoring range, and then fails on one third down. Drive over. Field goal instead of a touchdown, or a punt instead of a field goal.

And when you lose three games by two, three and three points, the eighth play is the entire season.

That’s why Seattle is 3-3 while outgaining people. It’s not a yards problem. It’s a finishing problem. And his quarterback named it without being asked to.

Now the stat that reframes the pressure problem, because this is the biggest thing in the whole press conference.

A reporter laid it out for him directly: in the Denver game, more than half of Darnold’s passes were thrown in under two and a half seconds — and he was hit more in under two and a half seconds than he ever had been.

Let me explain why that number matters so much.

Two and a half seconds is the industry benchmark for a quick-game throw. It’s the slant, the hitch, the stick route, the quick out. Three-step drop, ball gone. If a quarterback is getting more than half his passes out that fast, he is not holding the ball. Period. That debate is over.

So what does it mean that he was still getting hit at a career-high rate inside that window?

It means the protection was breaking before the play had a chance to exist. Being hit at 2.4 seconds isn’t a quarterback problem — the ball is already gone or going. That is a blocking failure, and it’s the kind that no scheme and no quarterback can fix.

So when you hear “Darnold took five sacks again” — and he did, in each of the last two games, fourteen on the season, with a 41.4 percent pressure rate in Denver — understand that he was also throwing faster than he ever has. Both things are true. And only one of them is his fault.

And here’s how Darnold answered it, which I thought was impressive.

He didn’t blame the line. He talked about what he controls: “When that time clock starts to go off... being careful with the football. Sometimes that’s throwing the ball away, and other times that’s knowing — when I’m under pressure — even maybe even taking a sack in those situations.”

A quarterback volunteering to take sacks. That’s a man choosing a punt over an interception.

And then the line of the week: “Not making a bad play worse is kind of the goal moving forward.”

“Not making a bad play worse.” That’s the lesson from the overtime interception, stated as a rule instead of an excuse. He threw deep to Rashid Shaheed when Cooper Kupp was sitting at the sticks, and he’s now told you his new rule is: when it’s already broken, don’t try to unbreak it with one throw.

He also said: “I’m just going to continue to play decisive and try to get the ball out.” Which, given the 2.5-second number, is exactly what he was already doing.

And now the question Macdonald refused to answer — and Darnold answered it.

Remember last week? A reporter asked Macdonald why Drew Lock kept running onto the field to help relay play calls. And Macdonald said: “we’re having a hard time sometimes communicating... we’ll look and see what the recourse of that is if — but, um — we end up — I’ll just leave it at that.”

He cut his own sentence in half. I told you that was the most interesting moment of the week.

Well, on Thursday, somebody asked Sam Darnold the same question — noise or headset? And Darnold answered it:

“No, it was — I mean, a little bit of combination of both.”

Both. Crowd noise and the headset.

And he went further: “Just headset stuff kind of happening around the league.” And: “I think as the season goes on, it’s going to continue to get better. But I think the biggest thing is just having a plan for when it does happen.”

So there it is. The communication problem was real, it was partly equipment, it’s apparently not unique to Seattle, and the fix is a contingency plan rather than a repair. The quarterback said in twenty seconds what the head coach wouldn’t finish saying.

And there’s one more thing Darnold said that I want to put in front of you, because it’s the best scouting report anybody’s given on tonight’s opponent.

Asked what the hallmark of the Kansas City defense is, he said something I haven’t heard a quarterback say before:

“They play really fast. And I think a lot of times they attack offenses and they don’t allow the offense to attack them. And I think that’s a hallmark of a great defense.”

“They don’t allow the offense to attack them.”

Think about what that means. Most defenses react. You line up, you motion, you snap, and they respond. A great defense flips that — it makes you spend your snap solving its problem instead of posing your own.

And then Darnold described Steve Spagnuolo in a way that lines up exactly with what Macdonald said two days earlier — and neither man was in the room for the other’s answer:

“It seems like, to an offense or to someone just watching, it might seem like they do a lot. But you can tell that within their scheme, I feel like they keep it really simple and they allow their defense to play fast — but it looks like they’re doing a lot of things out there.”

Now put that next to Macdonald, Wednesday: “You can mistake it by saying that they’re just kind of throwing paint against the wall, but I think really the opposite is true, on how targeted he is.”

The head coach and the quarterback, independently, gave the same answer: it looks complicated and it isn’t. Spagnuolo’s defense is simple on the inside and chaotic on the outside, which is the hardest kind to prepare for — because you can’t attack a weakness you can’t find on tape.

And Darnold’s counter to it was the self-scout: “A huge, huge point of emphasis these last few days was looking at our tendencies, and what defenses are realizing, and how they’re playing us.”

Tendencies. That’s the real work of a break week. Not new plays — finding out what you do too often, and from which formations, because that’s what the next opponent has already noticed.

Now Friday’s news, and there’s a lot of it. Starting with the good.

Anthony Bradford is playing. Macdonald said it flatly: “AB will play.” And asked whether Bradford could go the whole game: “Yeah, that’s the plan.”

Five games on injured reserve with knee and hip injuries, and the starting right guard is back for a Sunday night game. And Macdonald framed it as more than one position: “It’s nice to get the starting five back together.”

He also pointed at the pairing that matters: “How much could Anthony being back help a guy like Abe Lucas?” Macdonald: “Those guys have played a lot of snaps next to each other. I know they have a good relationship.”

That’s the right side of the line, reunited. And given everything I just told you about the 2.5-second number, this is the single most important thing that has happened to Seattle’s offense in a month.

And Macdonald’s verdict on the man: “AB’s earned this job for a reason. He’s played good football for us, especially during training camp.”

Now Charbonnet, and Friday clarified it completely.

A reporter asked whether Seattle was “just protecting him from himself.” And Macdonald’s answer was essentially yes:

“I think we’re going along with the plan. The plan was to get a great week’s worth of work and see where we’re at... we’re going to take it into next week. It’s part of the plan.”

And then, asked directly if there was a setback: “No setback. No. If anything, it’s going as good as we could hope it could go.”

“As good as we could hope it could go.” That is not a disappointed coach. That is a staff executing a schedule. He’s eight months from ACL surgery, he took a full week of practice, and the next step is next week — not this week.

And the confirmation we were waiting on: Elijah Arroyo’s season is over.

Macdonald: “I think Elijah’s will be season-ending, unfortunately. I know he just had surgery recently and said it went well.”

And what Seattle loses: “The things that he’s able to do, we got to figure out ways to get that skill set on the field, whether it’s through his tight end room or through another room.” And then, simply: “It stinks.”

“Whether it’s through the tight end room or another room.” That’s him telling you the answer might be a receiver or a back doing Arroyo’s job, not a tight end replacing him.

The rest of Friday’s board:

Nick Emmanwori is questionable with a hip — and it’s brand new. Macdonald: “It happened in practice today. The hip is new today.” That’s the chess piece who plays safety, slot, linebacker and rusher, now a game-day question.

Drew Lock is questionable with an elbow, which has been on the report all week.

Connor O’Toole questionable. Chazz Surratt out. Drake Thomas out.

And Jadarian Price: “Next week, I believe, is when his window can open.” So the rookie back is a November story.

And my favorite exchange of the whole week, because it’s just funny. A reporter asked what shade of questionable Emmanwori was. Macdonald: “I don’t know what to tell you. It’s Friday tempo. The best way to describe it is — it’s questionable. I’m not trying to be... it’s just questionable.”

Now let me give you the football lesson from Friday, because Macdonald taught something genuinely great about protection.

A reporter noted that people obsess over the offensive line and asked how much a good pass-protecting running back matters.

Macdonald: “You can throw them right in there with the offensive line. A lot of times they end up with more one-on-ones than the line does. It’s incredibly important.”

“More one-on-ones than the line does.” Think about that. A guard usually has help — a center or tackle beside him. A running back picking up a blitzing linebacker has nobody. He’s alone, often against a man with a running start, and if he loses, the quarterback eats it immediately.

And then the image I loved: “You want the full menu of protections to be available to be made in the kitchen. It helps when you can call those protections and feel great about it.”

The full menu, made in the kitchen. Meaning: if your backs can’t protect, your coordinator loses entire pages of the playbook. You can’t call the seven-step concept if nobody can block the blitzer. So a back’s pass protection doesn’t just prevent sacks — it determines how much offense you’re allowed to have.

Which is exactly why Charbonnet and Price matter beyond carries, and why Emanuel Wilson and George Holani carry more responsibility tonight than a stat sheet will show.

And Tyrice Knight deserves his moment, because he’s the man standing in for Drake Thomas.

Macdonald: “Tyrice has done a really nice job. It’s seamless. He goes in — he’s got a little different style of play than Drake, which is cool. Really productive. And I know him and EJ have a great rapport as well.”

“EJ” is Ernest Jones the Fourth, who was ranked eighth among NFL linebackers coming into this season. So the pairing in the middle tonight is Jones and Knight, with Thomas and Surratt out.

And Macdonald added the part coaches don’t always say: “He’s a guy that deserves to play. We’ve tried to find ways to do it when Drake is up, but now that he’s had his opportunities, he’s definitely taking advantage of it.”

Against Kenneth Walker the Third. 655 rushing yards, eight total touchdowns, first man since Jim Brown in 1963 to start a season like this. Tyrice Knight’s night just got very large.

And finally — the thing that actually answers the “does Darnold want out” question better than any quote could.

Macdonald was asked about his philosophy of players meeting across position groups — a quarterback sitting with offensive linemen, that sort of thing. And listen to this answer:

“We’re one team, man. We’re not a collection of position groups or a collection of phases. It’s — we’re the Seahawks.”

And then the detail: “There are independent meetings that guys are holding to get people from cross-sectional groups... they’re doing that on their own throughout the week, in both phases. Which I really appreciate — and that’s a player-driven thing.”

Players organizing their own meetings, across position groups, on their own time, in the middle of a two-game losing streak.

That is not a locker room coming apart. Teams that are falling apart leak to reporters and stop showing up early. This one is scheduling extra meetings nobody asked it to schedule.

And Macdonald’s close on it: “I think that only helps you get more aligned.”

Let me close.

Three and three. Tonight, Kansas City — 5-0, fourth in the league in offensive EPA per play, second in EPA per rush — comes to Lumen Field with Kenneth Walker the Third, the MVP of Seattle’s Super Bowl.

And Seattle plays them with Charbonnet out, Thomas out, Surratt out, Emmanwori questionable, Lock questionable — and both Kansas City starting tackles in trouble, with Rashee Rice out and Travis Kelce limited all week.

But Seattle gets its starting five back on the offensive line. And the quarterback everybody wants to run out of town just told you he was throwing the ball in under two and a half seconds and getting hit anyway, that the fix is the eighth play of a drive, and that he trusts every call his coordinator makes.

That’s not a man looking for the exit. That’s a man doing the homework.

Darnold’s own words on how you handle a stretch like this: “Everything that happens in your life prepares you for the next moment. But staying in the moment is sometimes the hardest thing to do... just being exactly where our feet are.”

Exactly where our feet are. Tonight, that’s Lumen Field.
