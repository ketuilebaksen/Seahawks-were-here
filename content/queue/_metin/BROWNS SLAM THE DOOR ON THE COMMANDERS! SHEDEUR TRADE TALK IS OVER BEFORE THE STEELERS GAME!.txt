Mary Kay Cabot settled it. No trade before November tenth. And the JJ McCarthy deal just priced the whole market.
 
All week we said the same thing, over and over, to seven different versions of the same rumor: when this becomes real, a reporter with a name and a track record will put it in print.
 
It happened.
 
Mary Kay Cabot of cleveland.com, as connected to this beat as anyone in the business, reported that the Browns have no plans to trade Shedeur Sanders or Dillon Gabriel before the November tenth trade deadline.
 
That's a report. Named journalist, specific claim, organizational sourcing, a date attached. It is a completely different object from a fan-site framework that gained a source every time it got retold.
 
And on the same news cycle, the New York Giants, the team at the center of the loudest version of the rumor, solved their quarterback problem by trading a 2027 fifth-round pick to Minnesota for JJ McCarthy.
 
Those two facts together tell us more than the entire week did. Let's work through them, including the part where we have to correct ourselves.
 
First, what a real report looks like.
 
Quick compare, because it's instructive.
 
The rumor version: "sources close to the situation," "multiple insiders are saying the same thing," an identical trade framework repeated in seven videos, and at one point "record-breaking contract numbers" that the rookie wage system makes impossible.
 
The report version: a beat writer, by name, at a publication, stating what the team's plans are, with the deadline date in the sentence.
 
That's the whole difference, and now everybody can see it side by side. We're not taking a victory lap, we're pointing at the contrast, because the next time a rumor cycle starts, this is the test.
 
And one small thing the loud version got wrong: including Dillon Gabriel in that report wasn't the Browns padding the statement. It's a beat writer covering the quarterback room properly. Gabriel is a young quarterback on a cheap deal too. He counts.
 
Now the part where we were wrong.
 
Here's where we hold ourselves to the standard we've been applying to everybody else all week.
 
We said the price should start in the second round. We said it twice, once when Washington was the only suitor, and again when New York joined, arguing that two buyers create a real bidding dynamic.
 
The JJ McCarthy trade says we were too high.
 
Look at what just moved. McCarthy was a first-round pick. He has ten NFL starts. He went six and four in them. And his price was one 2027 fifth-round pick.
 
If a former first-rounder with ten starts costs a fifth, then a former fifth-rounder with seven starts does not cost a second. The market for young quarterbacks with uneven tape is much softer than we estimated, and the honest read is that Shedeur Sanders' realistic trade value sits somewhere in the fourth-to-fifth round range, with conditions, maybe a little better.
 
Which means Tony Grossi's skepticism, which we engaged with respectfully a few days ago, aged better than our own number did. He argued that a player 31 teams passed on doesn't suddenly command a premium. The one actual transaction of the week proved his point and disproved ours.
 
We said all week that we'd tell you when we were wrong. That's us being wrong.
 
The "cash cow" theory, checked.
 
Now the argument at the center of this take: that Cleveland is keeping Sanders purely as a revenue generator. A cash cow. Jerseys, viewership, content, attention, money, not football.
 
Let's check it, because the mechanics don't support it.
 
Jersey and merchandise money is pooled. NFL licensed merchandise revenue runs through the league and is shared equally among all 32 teams. When a Shedeur Sanders jersey sells, Cleveland does not get a special check. Every other team gets the same slice the Browns do. That's the single biggest hole in the theory.
 
Content creator revenue doesn't reach the team at all. The people making videos about him, including everyone in this cycle, keep their own ad revenue. None of it lands in Berea.
 
And local revenue requires him to play. Tickets, concessions, local sponsorship, those move when a player is on the field putting people in seats. He hasn't taken a meaningful snap. The ticket market for the home opener, as we covered, was soft, not inflated.
 
So what's actually left? Television money, which is national and shared. National attention does have value to a franchise, but it's diffuse, it's mostly league-wide, and it isn't the kind of line item a general manager protects by refusing trade calls.
 
Here's what we think is actually happening, and it's less cynical and more boring:
 
One, insurance. Deshaun Watson has two Achilles surgeries behind him and just ran the ball eleven times in a game. You do not trade your backup quarterback in that situation.
 
Two, the price. Nobody was offering what Cleveland thinks he's worth, and the McCarthy trade just showed why: the market is cheap. Teams don't sell assets into a soft market when they have no reason to sell.
 
Three, they might actually think he's good. That possibility keeps getting skipped because it's the least dramatic explanation available.
 
The one play in the red zone.
 
And now the legitimate grievance in all of this, because there is one and it deserves to be taken seriously.
 
By this account, Sanders was put in for a single play in the red zone and then Watson came back on the field.
 
If that's what happened, it isn't development. One snap is a cameo. A quarterback learns nothing from one play that he couldn't learn from a walkthrough, and it doesn't tell the coaching staff anything either. You can't evaluate a passer on one red zone snap any more than you can evaluate a receiver on one route.
 
Fair caveat, because we don't know the reason: it could have been a specific designed play, a personnel package the staff had installed for that situation, or a moment when the staff wanted a look at something. Nobody in Berea has explained it.
 
But the principle is the one we've argued all week. Cleveland's real problem isn't the trade market, it's information. And one snap is not information.
 
If the Browns are keeping him through November tenth, and they've now said so through a reporter who doesn't get things wrong, then the obligation that comes with keeping him is finding out what he is. A package. A series. Situational snaps. Meaningful reps when a game tilts in either direction. Something a coaching staff can actually grade.
 
Keep him or play him is a false choice. Keeping him is exactly why you should be learning something.
 
What the Giants' choice really says.
 
The angriest part of this take is that New York chose McCarthy over Sanders, and that McCarthy's reported line last season, under 58 percent completions, 12 interceptions to 11 touchdowns in ten starts, is not better than Shedeur's rookie year.
 
That's a fair reading of the numbers, and it's also not what the Giants were choosing between.
 
They weren't comparing two players. They were comparing two prices and two availabilities. Cleveland said no. Minnesota said yes, for a fifth. A general manager whose starter is out for the season takes the quarterback he can actually get.
 
And in fairness to McCarthy, who is a young player being used as a prop in an argument about somebody else, a first-round pick with a high ankle injury and a chaotic first year of starts is exactly the kind of quarterback who can look completely different in a new building. The numbers aren't good. The bet is reasonable.
 
The lesson for Cleveland isn't that the Giants disrespected Shedeur Sanders. It's that the quarterback market is cheaper than anybody in this rumor cycle claimed, which, again, is the thing we got wrong.
 
Who was actually in it, and who wasn't.
 
Since this cycle produced a lot of team names, here's the clean list, sorted by how much was ever behind them.
 
Washington. The most credible of the group, and for an obvious reason: Jayden Daniels dislocated the same left elbow for the third time in under a year, and Marcus Mariota is a bridge. A real need, real draft capital, and reported interest. Still never a named report of an actual offer.
 
New York. Became a real situation the moment Jaxson Dart's knee ended his season, with a head coach who had reported interest in Sanders back in his Baltimore days. And New York is the one that resolved, with McCarthy, for a fifth.
 
The Jets. Floated, and worth being skeptical about. Reported ownership-level coolness on Sanders during the pre-draft process makes that link the weakest of the three.
 
Atlanta, Las Vegas, Tampa Bay, Detroit, Indianapolis, Dallas. All named at some point in the last two weeks, in videos, columns or June reporting. None by a reporter attaching a source to a current conversation.
 
That's eight franchises attached to one backup quarterback inside a fortnight. One of them made a quarterback trade, and it wasn't for him.
 
Where we stop.
 
"The Browns drafted him for reasons that had nothing to do with football." That's an assertion about private motives, and it's the same move we've refused in every direction all season, including when it was aimed at Monken in August.
 
"Holding him hostage." He's a player under contract, like every other player in the league. A team declining trade offers for a player it controls is the most ordinary thing in professional sports. You can criticize the reps. The contract isn't a hostage situation.
 
"Washington was willing to give up two picks." Unconfirmed. And now priced out of relevance by an actual trade.
 
What this means going forward.
 
So here's where the week ends, with the noise cleared out.
 
No trade before November tenth. That's from a named reporter and it's as settled as anything gets in September.
 
The market is soft, a fourth or fifth, not a second. Cleveland has nothing to gain by selling and nothing to lose by waiting until March.
 
And the whole question moves inside the building now. Not "who wants him," but "what is Cleveland going to learn about him between now and January?" That's a question for Todd Monken and Andrew Berry, and it's the only one that still matters.
 
And there's a football game Thursday. Pittsburgh, short week, division game, both teams winning. Cleveland's passing offense produced 144 yards in the home opener. That number, not the trade deadline, is what decides this season.
 
The bottom line.
 
Here's the Ktl Report read.
 
A real report finally arrived, from Mary Kay Cabot, and it says no trade before November tenth, for Sanders or Gabriel.
 
We were wrong on the price. The JJ McCarthy trade, a former first-rounder with ten starts for a 2027 fifth, prices this market far lower than our second-round estimate. Fourth or fifth is the honest range.
 
The "cash cow" theory doesn't hold, because merchandise money is shared league-wide and a player who doesn't play doesn't sell tickets. Insurance and a soft market explain it better.
 
The one-play cameo is a fair complaint. One red zone snap isn't development, and keeping him is exactly why the Browns should be learning something real.
 
And the Giants chose price and availability, not quality. Cleveland said no; Minnesota said yes for a fifth.
