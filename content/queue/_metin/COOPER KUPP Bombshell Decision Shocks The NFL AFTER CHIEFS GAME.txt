Cooper Kupp spent the week before the biggest game of Seattle’s season reading about a college football coach who died thirteen years ago.

Seahawks Nation, I’m not making that up. He volunteered it at the podium, unprompted, after a 20-3 win over the Kansas City Chiefs. He said — and I’m quoting — “this week I’ve been, for whatever reason, diving into” the philosophy of a man who coached small-college football about thirty-five miles from Lumen Field.

And when I went and looked up who that man was, I understood exactly why Cooper Kupp is the way he is.

I’ll come back to it, because it’s the best thing anybody said all week. But first the football — because Cooper Kupp’s press conference gave away three things no other player or coach told us, and the first one made my jaw drop.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seahawks 20, Chiefs 3. Seattle is 4-3. Let’s go.

Thing one: “Sam’s fifth read.”

You know the number by now. Seattle came into this game among the worst third-down offenses in football, and on Sunday night they went eleven for their first fifteen — finishing eleven of sixteen — and a lot of those were long-yardage situations.

A reporter asked Cooper Kupp what he was seeing. And here’s his answer:

“I think we distributed the field well. I think Sam did a great job getting through progressions and taking advantage of the looks that that defense was giving us. Guys just playing fast, decisive football. And some of those conversions are Sam’s fifth read — getting them, and getting the ball in one of the playmakers’ hands and letting them go use their legs to get the first down.”

“Some of those conversions are Sam’s fifth read.”

Twelves, stop. The fifth read.

On a passing play, a quarterback has a progression — a ranked order of where to look. Most NFL throws are the first or second read. A good quarterback gets to three. Getting to the third read regularly is what separates starters from backups.

The fifth read is the checkdown’s checkdown — the last man in the progression, the guy you throw to when everything else is covered. To get there, you have to survive in the pocket long enough to look at four other options first, and then still throw it accurately. Against Steve Spagnuolo. On third and long.

And here’s where it connects: Sam Darnold was not sacked once. Zero sacks, hit twice. That is why there was a fifth read. You cannot get to a fifth read in two and a half seconds. Bradford came back at right guard, the pocket held, and the quarterback got to look at five men.

A week earlier the internet wanted that quarterback benched.

Thing two — and this is a masterclass. Cooper Kupp just explained the two ways that being doubled can destroy a receiver.

A reporter set it up beautifully: Cooper, you know what it’s like to be the focal point of an opposing defense and still shine and have a record-setting season. What do you make of what Jaxon’s doing — over 200 yards more than anybody else in the league?

And that stat is real, and bigger than he said. Jaxon Smith-Njigba leads the NFL with 867 receiving yards. Second is Chris Olave of New Orleans at 636. That’s a 231-yard lead — clear of the entire league.

Kupp’s first answer was about focus: “He’s playing one play at a time and he’s doing an unbelievable job of it — knowing that there’s going to be plays where it’s an uphill battle and they’ve got those doubles on him, and teams are going to continue to find ways to try to take him out of the game. But his ability just to stay engaged, one play after the next... it’s really hard to do. It truly is a really hard thing to do, to stay engaged through a whole game when a defense is trying to take you away.”

“They’ve got those doubles on him.” Confirmation from inside the huddle: teams are double-teaming him and he’s still 231 yards clear.

Then the follow-up was the great one: What do you have to guard against when that happens?

And Cooper Kupp — a man who has lived this — named two separate failure modes. This is the best coaching anybody gave us all week:

“Well, I think it’s the complacency, right? It’s like, well, they’re going to take me away. You start to kind of take a step back and you just say, well, they’re taking me out of the game. And it’s hard to get into a rhythm sometimes that way.”

“And the other side of it is you start pushing. You start feeling like you have to play outside of what the offense is.”

Two ways to break. Opposite directions.

Failure one: you quit competing. The defense has decided to erase you, so you stop running hard, stop selling routes, stop expecting the ball. You disappear with the defense’s permission.

Failure two: you try too hard. You freelance. You break your route because you need a catch. You stop being a piece of the offense and start being a man chasing numbers. “Playing outside of what the offense is.”

And his answer for how Jaxon avoids both: “He’s done an unbelievable job just staying engaged and exactly executing his job — and knowing that Sam’s going to do a great job going through his progressions. If you’re in the right place at the right time, creating separation, Sam will give you the ball.”

“If you’re in the right place at the right time, Sam will give you the ball.”

That’s trust as a technique. Not a slogan — an actual method. The reason Jaxon Smith-Njigba doesn’t freelance when he’s doubled is that he believes his quarterback will find him if he does his job correctly. And on Sunday night, that belief was rewarded on the fifth read.

And the historical company Jaxon is now keeping is absurd.

He is the first receiver since 1966 with at least 850 receiving yards and eight touchdowns through his team’s first seven games, per ESPN. Since 1966 — before the first Super Bowl was played. He joins Michael Irvin in 1995, Marvin Harrison in 2000, and Adam Thielen in 2018 as the only Super Bowl-era receivers with 100-plus yards in six of their team’s first seven games. And his 867 yards are the third-most through seven games in the entire Super Bowl era — behind only Tyreek Hill in 2023 (902) and Lance Alworth in 1968 (871).

He is four yards behind Lance Alworth. Four. One route against Chicago.

And the man explaining how to carry it is Cooper Kupp, who won the triple crown in 2021. That’s the most valuable veteran presence in football, and Seattle has it in its own huddle.

Thing three: Cooper Kupp made a tackle, and then told on himself about it.

Twelves, remember this play from Mike Macdonald’s press conference? Seattle got an interception, and Macdonald went out of his way to praise the men who blocked and chased on the return — George Holani and A.J. Barner on the sideline, and Charles Cross running down the field: “To get the guy to cut back so George could tackle him — that’s 12-as-one football, man.”

Well, a reporter asked Cooper Kupp about his part in that same play — because Kupp and Holani slowed the ball carrier enough for Cross to arrive. His answer:

“You know, George probably gets the credit for that one. I don’t know how effective I was tackling there.”

I love this man. A Super Bowl MVP, and his contribution to a hustle play is a self-deprecating joke.

But then he got serious:

“That’s a big-time play in terms of momentum at the half. Being able to keep them out of field goal range — and we talk about it all the time, every yard matters. Every yard matters.”

“Every yard matters.” That is the exact phrase Mike Macdonald used from his own podium about that same play. Two different men, two different rooms, same five words. That’s not coincidence. That’s a sentence that gets said in that building every single day.

And then Kupp did something you almost never hear after a seventeen-point win. He criticized his own team’s effort on a play that worked:

“We would like to do a better job of, if it is going to happen like that, that you get that guy on the ground — because you don’t want to give them an opportunity to score.”

They stopped the return. They kept Kansas City out of field goal range. They scored to start the half. And he still said we’d like to do that better.

Then the philosophy underneath it: “We talk all the time about giving our defense every chance. If we give our defense a chance, we know they’re going to go out there and make a play for us.”

That’s not a compliment — that’s an operating assumption. The offense’s job, in Kupp’s description, is to not put the defense in a bad spot, because if you don’t, the defense wins the game for you. 213 yards. Eleven first downs. No red zone trips. Three points.

Now Sam Darnold — because Kupp was asked directly about the criticism, and his answer was about a skill nobody talks about.

The question: There’s obviously been criticism of Sam the last couple of weeks, and everybody says he stayed the same. Is that what paid off tonight?

“He did an unbelievable job. Unbelievable job out there. His command — his ability to get plays in and out, just his command of the huddle, at the line of scrimmage, getting things fixed, getting guys organized.”

Notice what he led with. Not the throws. The command. That’s the quarterback as traffic cop — eleven men lined up right, protections adjusted, somebody moved two yards left before the snap. The least glamorous and most important part of the job, and it never shows up in a box score.

Then the mechanics: “Playing decisive on all those third downs. He’s really clicking through his progressions and going through it one at a time, and playing in rhythm with his feet and his eyes all in one.”

“His feet and his eyes all in one.”

That’s a gorgeous phrase and a real diagnosis. A struggling quarterback’s eyes and feet come apart — he’s looking at one receiver while his feet are set for a different throw, so when he decides he has to reset, and he’s late. “Feet and eyes all in one” means the body is already aimed where the eyes are going. That’s what rhythm is.

And he closed it the way a teammate should: “That’s who he is. That’s what we expect from him. And that’s who he’s going to continue to be, because that’s who Sam is.”

Macdonald has called him “part of the solution” three weeks running. Jaxon said “that’s why we love him.” Kupp says “that’s who Sam is.” Nobody inside that building ever wavered. Remember that the next time the internet decides somebody is finished.

Now the defense — and Kupp said something about them I haven’t heard anybody else say.

A reporter laid out the numbers: eleven first downs, the fewest in a Mahomes start, no red zone trips.

“That was some lights-out defense. It’s really cool to watch those guys go to work. I see it every day in the way that they compete in practice. We get the pleasure of going up against them, and it’s always a great challenge.”

“We get the pleasure of going up against them.” Seattle’s offense practices against that every week. That’s a hidden advantage nobody counts.

But then he explained why they’re good, and it wasn’t scheme:

“First off, they really love one another. And a defense like that, that plays for one another, plays off of each other so well — that’s what makes defenses great.”

“They really love one another.”

Remember what a national analyst said about this same defense this week — that the impressive part isn’t the length and speed, it’s “the way that they’re getting them to play together”? And the freeze frame with ten, eleven hats in the picture on a Kenneth Walker carry?

The analyst saw the eleven hats. Cooper Kupp told you why there were eleven hats. Guys run to the ball for people they love. That’s not soft; it’s the mechanism.

And two lines about where this team actually was before Sunday.

First, a reporter pointed out: “This team hasn’t lost two games in a row since before you were here.”

Cooper Kupp signed in Seattle in 2025. So Seattle had not lost two straight in over a season. That’s why Jaxon said “I don’t know the last time we’ve been a part of that.” It wasn’t a cliché. It was unfamiliar.

Kupp’s answer: “Regardless of what happened before, it was about going out there and playing Seahawks football. We’ve been talking about it and talking about it, and putting in so much work to see it not happen has been tough — but we just continue to be at the grindstone... and it was great to see that materialize finally.”

“Putting in so much work to see it not happen has been tough.” That’s the most honest sentence of the night. Not we were fine. Not it’s a long season. Tough.

And then the line I can’t stop thinking about. A reporter said: “You guys have been chasing a complete game like this probably since February.”

Since February. Do the math. February is the Super Bowl. This franchise won Super Bowl LX — and that reporter just said, and Cooper Kupp did not disagree, that Sunday night was the first complete game Seattle has played since the night they won the championship.

Eight months. That’s how long the defending champions went without feeling like themselves. And they got it back against the last unbeaten team in the AFC.

Kupp: “It feels good — but it feels good because this is part of the journey. This is part of the vision. This is what we work for. This is who we are.”

Now — Frosty Westering. The part I promised you.

Here’s the quote in full, and it’s remarkable:

“We’ve talked about this over and over this week: this is an about-us game. It’s about going out there and playing our football. And this week I’ve been, for whatever reason, diving into more of what Frosty Westering — a legendary coach here — he used to talk about. A team comes in to beat us, but our job is to be us. We come step on the field, just be who we are. And that’s been Mike’s message from day one. It’s about us and who we are.”

So who was Frosty Westering?

Forrest “Frosty” Westering coached Pacific Lutheran University — in Tacoma, about thirty-five miles from Lumen Field — for thirty-two seasons. Career record around 305-96-7, which puts him in the tiny club of college coaches with 300 wins. Three NAIA national championships — 1980, 1987 and 1993 — plus the NCAA Division III title in 1999. College Football Hall of Fame. He got the Amos Alonzo Stagg Award in January 2013 and died three months later at eighty-five.

But the wins aren’t why Cooper Kupp was reading about him. Frosty Westering is famous for how he ran a football team.

His preseason camps weren’t football camps — they were three-day retreats with skits and songs instead of drills. His teams used “attaway” cheers. After every game, win or lose, they held an “afterglow”: food, hugs and feelings, with both teams. His players were taught to knock an opponent down and then help him up. His slogan was “Every Man A Lute,” and his players gave around two thousand hours a year to Tacoma-area schools.

And what he taught is sometimes called the “double win”: play to the absolute edge of your own potential, and let the scoreboard be the scoreboard. His book was called “Make the Big Time Where You Are.” He once said the journey “was the greatest thing in life, whether we won or lost.”

Twelves, do you understand what just happened?

In the week after his team lost two in a row for the first time in over a season, the most accomplished receiver on the roster went and studied a small-college coach from Tacoma who believed football was a means to an end. And he brought back one sentence: “Another team comes in to beat us. Our job is to be us.”

Then he connected it to his head coach: “That’s been Mike’s message from day one. It’s about us and who we are.”

Think about how many of Macdonald’s sentences that explains. “12 as one.” “We’re still a championship caliber team and that’s what we’re chasing.” “You can’t control starting fast — you can control your mentality.” Macdonald has been preaching Frosty Westering’s gospel without saying his name.

And Kupp finished it: “It doesn’t matter who’s across from you. It’s about executing your job. How do you do your job over and over and over again? And when you handle that side of things, the results are going to end up in a good place most of the time anyway.”

“The results are going to end up in a good place most of the time anyway.”

Process over outcome, said by a man with a Super Bowl MVP trophy, after beating a 5-0 team by seventeen.

Now let me be straight with you about this video’s title, because I always am.

There is no “bombshell decision” from Cooper Kupp in this press conference. No announcement, no retirement hint, no trade request, no ultimatum. He announced nothing. He answered eight questions about his teammates and quoted a college coach who died thirteen years ago.

And that’s better. A receiver who won a triple crown is standing in the same huddle as a man doing something nobody’s done since 1966, teaching him how not to break under a double team. That’s not a bombshell. That’s what the bombshells are made of.

Where we are: Seattle is 4-3, defending Super Bowl champions. Chicago comes next, November second. The deadline is November tenth — and Adam Schefter has Seattle among four teams “expected to consider” a Jonathan Taylor trade. Watch Cross’s ankle, Shaheed’s knee, Lock’s elbow, and Jadarian Price’s return window.

Let me close. The head coach gave us “play in waves.” Jaxon gave us “I invite it” and took the blame for an interception. National television gave us “dark side defense.”

And Cooper Kupp gave us the fifth read, the two ways a doubled receiver breaks, a self-deprecating joke about his own tackling, and a coach from Tacoma who believed your job is to be you.

That’s a locker room that isn’t in trouble — and hasn’t been for one single day.

Twelves, two questions. First: which of Kupp’s two failure modes worries you more — a star who goes complacent because the defense erased him, or a star who starts pushing and plays outside the offense? Mine’s the pushing. The complacent guy you can coach; the freelancer breaks the whole play.

And second: “Our job is to be us.” Is that the sentence of Seattle’s season? I think it might be — and I think Mike Macdonald has been saying it in different words since the day he got here.
