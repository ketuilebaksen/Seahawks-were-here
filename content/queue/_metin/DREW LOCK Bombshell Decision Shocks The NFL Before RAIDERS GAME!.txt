“This injury is what it is. It’s going to be ready to go by Sunday, and I’m going to go out and play NORMAL.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s Drew Lock, and that’s the decision. Not managed. Not limited. Normal.

And then he explained something about his oblique injury that I have never heard a quarterback explain on camera — and it’s the single best piece of news Seattle has gotten all week.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four, Las Vegas is Sunday, Sam Darnold is out, and Drew Lock just told us he’s fine. Let’s go.

First, the thing that changes everything — WHICH SIDE the oblique is on.

Asked how much he’s feeling it while throwing:

“Not too much. Honestly — talked about it being on the BACK SIDE, which is way better for a thrower instead of the front side. So grateful for that.”

“Way better for a thrower.”

And then a reporter asked him to explain the difference, and twelves, this is a genuine coaching clinic:

“Mechanic-wise, like, yeah, you’ve got to use a little bit of the backside. But when you’re rotating and pulling through, it’s all THIS — and that’s really when the torque happens. The speed of the arm comes through with that. So allowing it to be back here, mechanically speaking, it is a little better.”

Let me break that down, because it matters enormously.

A throw is not an arm motion. A throw is a rotation.

For a right-handed quarterback, the front side is the left side of the torso — and that’s the side that decelerates and pulls everything through. That’s where, as Lock put it, “the torque happens.” That left oblique is the hinge the whole throw rotates around, and the arm speed is generated by it.

The back side — the right side — is the side you load and push off of. It’s involved, but it is not the engine.

So an oblique strain on the throwing-arm side is a sore muscle. An oblique strain on the FRONT side attacks the exact mechanism that generates velocity.

And Drew Lock knows the difference from experience — because he’s had the bad one.

“I have. When I was in New York.”

And what was that like? “That was on the front side. So yeah, that was not fun for a couple weeks. Took a couple weeks off. It was in the preseason though, so I missed the last two preseason games. Had the little three or four day weekend before the first week, and then I was back to being the backup.”

“That was not fun for a couple weeks.”

Twelves, there’s your comparison. The front-side oblique cost him two weeks and two preseason games. The back-side oblique has him saying “not too much” and planning to play normal.

Same injury name. Completely different problem. And now you know why.

And here’s the part that makes it land: a few weeks ago a national analyst looked at Lock’s elbow and said “as anybody knows who’s had tennis elbow, that’s annoying — it can hang around for a quarterback.” He was right. Tennis elbow is tendinitis, and that’s the confirmed diagnosis.

But the oblique — the newer, scarier-sounding one — landed on the harmless side. That’s luck, and Seattle needed some.

Now — the state of his week, and the tone is completely different from last week.

“Good. Feeling good. Yeah, feeling good. Kind of like I talked about after the game — I feel like we did a great job handling the week last week, and here we are. We just got a little something added to it and I think we’re doing the same. We’re doing a great job getting ready. Good stuff in the morning before meetings. Be nice and warmed up heading into practice today, and we’ll go out there and roll it.”

“We just got a little something added to it.”

That’s how a professional describes a second injury. “A little something added.”

And catch the detail about the mornings: “good stuff in the morning before meetings. Be nice and warmed up heading into practice.”

That’s the medical plan at work. He’s doing treatment and activation BEFORE meetings, so by the time practice starts, the tissue is warm instead of cold. Cold muscle tears. Warm muscle works.

That’s the same staff Lock credited by name after the Arizona win — “the training staff, the strength staff, Coach Mike, everybody handled it so great.”

And on how much he’ll actually throw: “that’s what we’re deciding today or yesterday — like, hey, let’s pull this back a little bit, give you another day to rest.”

So they’re STILL rationing. But listen to why he’s comfortable with it:

“I don’t feel like I’m missing too much in prep, because of the fact that for the last couple years, that’s how I’ve been able to get ready for games. IT’S THE ONLY WAY I’VE BEEN ABLE TO GET READY FOR GAMES.”

“It’s the only way I’ve been able to get ready for games.”

Twelves, that is eight years of being a backup quarterback turned into an advantage, and I want you to really sit with it.

A normal starter who suddenly can’t practice is in crisis. His entire preparation method just got taken away from him.

Drew Lock has never had that method. He’s spent years preparing without reps — tape, walkthroughs, and what he called “re-watching practices.” Watching practice film of a practice he was standing at, so he could see it from the quarterback’s angle instead of the clipboard’s.

So when Seattle tells him “we’re pulling your throws back,” that isn’t an obstacle. That’s Tuesday.

And he was honest about the trade-off too: “it’s definitely a good blend of both. You want to be out there, you want to get every single rep.” Then: “it allows us to be a little more fluid as to how I’m feeling day by day.”

“Fluid.” They’re not locked into a plan. They’re reading his body each morning and adjusting.

And remember what happened last time: roughly twenty throws, all on one day, and nineteen of twenty-eight for 175 yards, a touchdown, and no turnovers.

NOW — the funniest and most revealing thing in this press conference, and it’s about Jaxon Smith-Njigba.

A reporter asked whether defenses are showing him pre-snap looks designed to scare him off throwing to JSN — and whether Seattle has different rules for him, like, if he’s getting this coverage we’ll still go there anyway.

And Drew Lock laughed and said: “Yeah. Jax would love that. JAX WOULD TELL YOU THAT IF HE’S DOUBLED, HE’S STILL OPEN. That’s kind of been the funny line this year.”

“If he’s doubled, he’s still open.”

Twelves, that is the greatest thing a thousand-yard receiver has ever said about himself, and apparently he says it constantly.

But then Lock gave the professional answer, and it’s the right one:

“No — for me personally, and I think for everybody involved, OUR READ IS OUR READ. If Jax is number one and he’s covered, we go to number two. If number one’s covered and it’s not Jax, we go to two — and Jax is for some reason three, and Jax is open? We’ll throw him the ball. That’s how I’ll play back there. Just go through reads, go through progressions, knowing you’re hot, and play good football.”

“Our read is our read.”

And twelves, I want to explain why that discipline matters so much, because it’s the difference between winning and throwing interceptions.

A progression is an order. First read, second read, third read, checkdown. And the order exists because of the coverage — it’s built so that if the defense takes away option one, option two is open BY DESIGN.

The moment you break the progression to force a ball to your best player, you’re not reading the defense anymore. You’re hunting. And hunting is how a safety who’s been sitting on your favorite receiver all afternoon gets to make a play on the ball.

So Drew Lock’s answer — “our read is our read” — is exactly what you want from a quarterback whose number one target leads the league in target share. He’s not going to feed Jaxon Smith-Njigba. He’s going to read it, and because JSN is that good, he’ll be the answer plenty of the time anyway.

And notice “knowing you’re hot.” That’s the blitz term — knowing which defender is unblocked and where the quick answer is if he comes. On a twenty-throw practice week, that awareness is everything.

Now — how you actually beat a defense that’s obsessed with one man.

A reporter asked what forces teams out of doubling Jaxon Smith-Njigba — production from other guys? A specific route?

And Lock’s answer was honest and a little philosophical:

“Now, that’s going to be a good question for a defensive coordinator whenever you get in front of them. They’re still HUMANS. They still feel games. If they’re on the sideline, they feel momentum shifting, they feel a guy getting the ball.”

“They’re still humans. They still feel games.”

That’s a wonderful observation and it’s true. Coverage decisions aren’t made by a computer in a vacuum. A coordinator watching his safety get beaten twice changes his mind. Momentum is a real input.

And then: “They want to double Jax, they’ll double Jax. They want to take him away from a game, then we have other players that are going to get the ball and make it work. It’s really on THEM how they want to attack us that week, and it’s our job to RESPOND.”

“It’s our job to respond.”

And twelves, Seattle already proved it can. Against Arizona, Mike Macdonald said it plainly: “to put the ball away in the red zone WHEN THEY TAKE JAX AWAY — to have another option down there is really helpful.”

They took Jax away. AJ Barner caught two touchdowns with a bad hand. Seventeen to three.

NOW — the best football explanation of the press conference, and it’s the oldest idea in the sport done right.

Asked how much the run game affects what coverages a defense can play:

“Yeah, I feel like that’s the classic, right? Run the ball to be able to throw it. Run the ball, run the ball — forcing them to get into SINGLE HIGH coverages, three or man, and that opens up the pass game. Then you start hitting the pass game, they’ve got to play SHELL, and you come back to the run. It’s the classic complementary football between run game and pass game.”

Twelves, that’s the entire sport in six sentences, so let me make sure you have it.

Single high means one safety deep. Which means the OTHER safety is down near the line of scrimmage helping against the run. That’s the defense respecting your running game — and it leaves only one man guarding the deep middle of the field. That’s when your passing game opens up.

Shell means two safeties deep. That’s the defense respecting your passing game — and it means one fewer body near the line. That’s when your running game gets easier.

A defense cannot do both at the same time. Physically cannot.

So the whole game is a lever: run it until they drop a safety down, then throw it over the top. Throw it until they lift that safety back, then run it down their throat.

And look at how perfectly this matches what Cooper Kupp told us after the same game: “when you can keep defenses in this place where they are playing two-high, it’s a great opportunity to build around the ball. And it puts the onus on us as receivers to make sure we’re getting down the field blocking.”

Quarterback and receiver, two separate press conferences, describing the exact same lever from both ends. That’s an offense that knows what it is.

Now third down, and there’s a real number attached.

Seattle converted around fifty percent on third down against Arizona. And Lock’s answer went straight to the down before it:

“Critical for us being good on first and second down. Speaking toward our third down last week — just guys making plays with the ball in their hand, mixing in whether it was a run, a quick pass, a deep drop back. Having ALL ASPECTS of our offense available on third down was big for us, and it keeps defenses on their toes. But mostly — let’s keep being efficient on first and second down and giving us advantageous situations on third.”

“Having all aspects of our offense available on third down.”

That’s the prize, twelves, and here’s what it means.

Third and three is a different universe from third and nine. On third and three you can run it, throw it quick, run play-action, hand it to a tight end on a push. The defense has to defend everything.

On third and nine, you have one choice and they know it. They can rush four, drop seven, and wait for you.

So being good on third down is mostly a lie — it’s being good on FIRST and SECOND down, which is what gets you to third and manageable.

And remember Anthony Bradford’s teammate Cooper Kupp said almost exactly this about the run game: “you stay ahead of the six, and you’re at second and six — that’s a big difference than second and ten.”

Same math. Different voice.

Now Jadarian Price, and Lock’s answer went bigger than one player.

Asked whether the plan with a man who makes people miss is just to get him the ball:

“Yeah — that, and to be honest with you, that’s how I feel about just about everybody on this team. Let’s get the ball in these guys’ hands, keep it in our hands, see what they do with it. And in my opinion, THERE’S NOT A GUY ON OUR OFFENSIVE SIDE OF THE BALL THAT I DON’T FEEL COMFORTABLE DOING THAT TO. JD was fantastic at it last week. Moving forward he can continue to do that, but we’ll see who it is this week — and I have full confidence it could be any one of our skilled players.”

“There’s not a guy on our offensive side of the ball that I don’t feel comfortable doing that to.”

And that ties straight to what Macdonald said: “it doesn’t have to be some extraordinary play to create explosives. Let’s just be efficient with our decision-making and get it in our players’ hands and let them do the rest.”

Same philosophy, coach and quarterback. You don’t hunt explosive plays. You distribute the ball efficiently and let good players make them.

AND NOW — my favorite answer, because it’s about Zach Charbonnet and it’s pure joy.

Asked whether something looked or felt different about Charbonnet and Price last week:

“Yeah. I think when CHARBS does his classic like ‘YOU CAN’T TACKLE ME’ thing and shoves people to the ground, it’s pretty fun to watch. And that’s kind of like a — oh, THERE it is. That’s what we know him as.”

And then: “the Carolina clip from years ago was kind of one of his first big ones. Man, when you see more and more of that happening — including the LOOK IN HIS EYE when you come back to the huddle. Yeah, there’s definitely a feel.”

“The look in his eye when you come back to the huddle.”

Twelves, think about where Zach Charbonnet was eight days before that.

He tore his ACL in January. He hadn’t taken a single live football rep in eight months. He played Monday night against Chicago and got held under thirty yards, and a national analyst’s word for him was “rust.”

And on Sunday his quarterback saw the look in his eye and thought: there he is.

That’s not a stat. You can’t put “the look in his eye” in a box score. But the man who takes the handoff from him says it’s back, and that’s the only scouting report that matters.

And his teammate confirmed it in his own words — Lock said last week they were “fighting through contact. First guy wasn’t tackling.”

NOW — the answer that tells you how this organization actually works, and it goes back years.

A reporter asked Lock to break down what’s giving him that confidence — the coaching staff? The building? The preparation?

And his answer surprised me:

“Ironically, it goes back to when I got traded here — the first couple talks that I had with Pete and John about how much confidence they had in me moving forward, about how excited they were to get me here. So to get set off on that foot right as I get here...”

And then the key part: “I know we’ve had different coaches in the quarterback room, offensive coaches, head coaches — IT’S ALL BEEN THE SAME. It’s all felt that way. There’s ‘we want you to go be YOU. We believe in you. Go out and execute, and don’t ever think anything — just go be yourself.’”

“We’ve had different coaches... it’s all been the same.”

Twelves, that is the most valuable thing you can say about an organization.

Drew Lock has been here through a coaching change. New head coach, new offensive coordinator, new quarterbacks coach. And he’s telling you the MESSAGE never changed.

That’s not luck. That’s institutional. The building has a way of treating people, and it survives staff turnover.

And then he extended it past himself: “when you keep getting that pressed upon you, whether it’s me or Jadarian Price or Tory Horton — that builds a confidence. And this building has done a phenomenal job, from what I’ve seen for myself and other players that sit in those chairs, that they’re going to be confident going into Sundays because they get preached that to them.”

“Because they get preached that to them.”

That’s “we hired you to be Seahawks” from the coaching side. Same philosophy, applied to players. One message, whole building.

Now let me give you the two human moments, because they’re great.

On playing through injury, he said something genuinely insightful: “most players can attest to playing through something. It takes a lot of focus during the week — like, extra thoughts throughout the week, they’re just not there. You’re thinking about getting better, thinking about getting out of practice, doing the best you can at practice and getting ready for Sunday. There’s just that extra thing to think about, and IT KIND OF TAKES YOUR BRAIN AWAY FROM ALL THE NONSENSE that may try to drag you in.”

“It takes your brain away from all the nonsense.”

An injury as a filter. There’s no spare capacity left for the noise — for the quarterback controversy talk, for the videos, for the takes. The rehab eats all of it.

And on adrenaline: “when you get out there, your adrenaline starts rolling, you’re PLAYING FOR MORE THAN JUST YOURSELF, and it kind of just goes away.”

“Playing for more than just yourself.”

And then the trash-talk story, which is the funniest thing I’ve heard all season.

Asked if he enjoys it when defenders get chatty:

“Yeah, no, I do have fun with that. I’d say earlier in my career I had more fun with it. TOLD JOEY BOSA HE WAS TIRED ONCE when I was playing for the Broncos. Then he proceeded to swim a tight end and tackle in the backfield for a three-yard loss and get in my face. THAT WAS LIKE THE LAST TIME I’VE EVER REALLY TALKED TO A DEFENSIVE LINEMAN DURING A GAME.”

[He told Joey Bosa he looked tired. Joey Bosa then threw a tight end aside, made a tackle for loss, and came and explained himself personally.]

And Lock’s conclusion: never again. That’s a man who learned a lesson one time and kept it for a decade.

And then: “love seeing AB get a little riled up, you know? It’s cool.”

Anthony Bradford, getting riled up at right guard, on the job he just got back. Of course Drew Lock loves that.

Finally — the question that produced the line of the week.

A reporter asked whether playing through injuries makes it easier to stay within yourself, like the saying in golf.

“Yeah, I get what you’re saying. It does. I guess you can mentally get to a point where, like — I’m going to stay within myself, not play HERO FOOTBALL, so to say. We’re always trying to avoid playing hero football, because THAT’S WHEN BAD THINGS HAPPEN.”

“Hero football is when bad things happen.” Write that down. That’s the whole explanation for zero interceptions this season and three straight games without a turnover.

But then he refused the premise, and this is the decision:

“But as far as my situation this week — I’m not looking at it as staying beneath myself. Like, this injury is what it is. It’s going to be ready to go by Sunday, and I’m going to go out and play NORMAL.”

“I’m going to go out and play normal.”

And on how he actually felt after Sunday: “Pretty good Sunday, pretty good Monday. And then by about five or six o’clock you start feeling some things. I thought I was getting a sore throat or something, but it was really just the neck, the head going back — NOT USED TO THE HITS. So, just classic football feelings after a game. Nothing too crazy.”

“Not used to the hits.” Because he hadn’t played in weeks. A man gets hit for the first time in a month and mistakes it for a sore throat. That’s football.

And last — his family posted some wonderful news this week. I’m not going to make that into content, twelves. I’ll just say this: eight years in this league, through a trade, a benching, a demotion to third string, and two injuries at once — and good things are happening for him at home in the same week he’s starting an NFL football game.

Congratulations to him and his family. Genuinely.
