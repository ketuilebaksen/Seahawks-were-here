“Never let me tell you how to run a route again.”

That’s Drew Lock, talking to Jaxon Smith-Njigba, after throwing for a win that put Seattle at five and four.

And twelves, the story behind that sentence is the best thing I’ve heard a Seahawk say all season. I’m going to save it for the end, because you deserve to hear all of it in order.

Let me be straight about this title first, like always: there’s no decision being announced here. But there IS real news in this press conference — Drew Lock went into the medical tent during this game and came back out — and there’s a behind-the-scenes look at how Seattle pulled this off that nobody else is going to show you.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seventeen to three. Five and four. Six starters out. Let’s go.

First, the injury — and his version is better than the official one.

Mike Macdonald told us he was having Lock evaluated for an oblique. So they asked Lock directly.

“Yeah, I don’t know. We’ll get it looked at tonight. I was able to finish with it, but we’ll get it looked at and go from there. But not too bad.”

“Not too bad.” Of course. He’s a quarterback. They all say that.

But then he told us exactly when it happened, and the story that follows is fantastic.

“Yeah, it was a scramble — or not a scramble, it was a movement. It was a keeper. I think it was third-ish quarter maybe.”

So: a designed quarterback keeper. Not a hit. Not a sack. A movement throw where his torso did something his torso didn’t want to do.

And then this:

“Went into the tent, had it looked at, came back out. Just let me see how it feels throwing the ball. I was like, ‘Wait — I’ve already thrown the ball. You’re going to be all right.’ Went and threw it. I mean, you feel it, but it’s nothing that was going to keep me on the sideline.”

Twelves, read that again.

They put him in the medical tent and told him to test it by throwing. And Drew Lock’s reaction was: I’ve already thrown the ball. With this injury. In this game. I’m fine.

He had already answered the medical question by playing through it before anybody asked.

And “it’s nothing that was going to keep me on the sideline” is the whole sentence. Not “the doctors cleared me.” “Nothing was going to keep me on the sideline.”

Now, the elbow — and his answer wasn’t about himself at all.

“Uh, good. I mentioned it out on the field, I believe, but the staff here — the training staff, the strength staff, Coach Mike, like everybody handled it so great today, this week. Jalen Milroe. I think it was really well done. Just hats off to this building, to this team, for being able to stick with me throughout this week and get us here to Sunday as healthy and as ready as we could be.”

“For being able to stick with me throughout this week.”

He thanked the training staff, the strength staff, his head coach, and his backup quarterback — before he said one word about how his own arm felt.

And now the part I actually wanted from this press conference, because it’s a window into the job that we almost never get.

A reporter called him out, gently: you downplayed how limited you were on Thursday, but your teammates said you only had so many throws. How hard was it getting ready without full reps?

“Yeah, the walkthroughs are obviously huge. Everybody understanding that I wasn’t going to throw during those. With us playing on a Monday night, being able to do a walkthrough practice Wednesday — not throw there.”

And then: “I think it all — like I said, the building, coaching staff, strength staff, training staff, all having a piece in on this, was fantastic. It really worked well.”

But here’s the sentence that tells you who Drew Lock is:

“And to go back to probably something I’ve said before — I feel like I’ve found my way as a guy that doesn’t get reps as a backup to be ready for a game. So when we got to this week, it’s not something that I wasn’t used to, to say the least. Just mentally being as prepared as I can with tape, walkthrough reps, re-watching practices, and being ready to come out here and spin it around.”

“I’ve found my way as a guy that doesn’t get reps.”

Twelves, that is eight years of being a backup quarterback compressed into one sentence.

Think about what that job actually is. You don’t get the first-team reps. Ever. You stand behind the starter, you watch, and then one Sunday somebody tells you you’re playing and nobody cares that you haven’t taken a live snap in a month.

And Drew Lock just told us he has built an entire personal system for that. Tape. Walkthrough reps. Re-watching practices — not watching them, re-watching them. Watching practice film of a practice he was standing at, so he can see it from the quarterback’s angle instead of the clipboard’s.

That’s not talent. That’s a professional who figured out how to be ready without the thing everyone else needs.

And then he was asked a brilliant question, and his answer was even better.

“Does it take away that practice is overrated?”

And Lock said: “No. But you can kind of click into the ‘well, we don’t ever really fully practice on Thursday nights, and we go out and play on Thursday night games’ — and kind of talk to yourself that way.”

That’s a real mental trick, and it’s a good one.

Every team in this league plays Thursday night games. And on a Thursday night game week, nobody practices properly. Monday is recovery. Tuesday you walk through in shorts. Wednesday you fly. Thursday you play. That’s it.

So the entire league already proves, several times a year, that you can play winning football without a real practice week.

Lock just used that against his own doubt. “We do this on Thursdays all the time. This is a Thursday.”

And then he gave us the number, and twelves — it’s smaller than you think.

Asked how much he threw this week compared to a normal starting week:

“Yeah, the only balls I threw were on Thursday. And we did a little bit of a split there, but say I took the majority of the reps Thursday. And that was it.”

One day. He threw a football on one day this week.

He did not throw Wednesday. He did not throw Friday. He did not throw Saturday.

And then a reporter noticed something nobody else would have: it looked like he barely threw in pregame warmups either.

“Yeah, I know. They had — again, a great plan. Discussed basically all day Saturday on how we wanted to handle this. And yeah, it was executed perfect. Hats off to everybody. And I waited to throw until I finally got the shoulder pads and helmet on.”

“I waited to throw until I got the shoulder pads and helmet on.”

Twelves, this is a genuinely fascinating detail and I want to make sure you appreciate it.

A normal quarterback pregame is a long, escalating routine. You come out ninety minutes early in a t-shirt. You start at ten yards and work your way out to sixty. You throw dozens and dozens of footballs before you ever put the pads on.

Seattle deleted that entire phase.

They spent all of Saturday building a plan whose central idea was: don’t spend a single throw before it counts. Every football he threw on Sunday, he threw in pads, with a helmet on, in warmups that mattered or in the game itself.

That’s a throw budget. And they spent it entirely on football.

And then he told us where the template came from — and this part gave me chills.

Asked whether it was similar to what Sam Darnold dealt with in the playoffs:

“It was basically the same template. Like a three-minute tweak in the middle of it, but that was it.”

And then: “Yeah, they’ve been through it before. And again, going back to your question — seeing Sam do it in a previous year, not practice... I was practicing for him. Then him being able to come out on a Sunday and play really, really good football all throughout the playoffs was something I could hang my hat on and say, I could model myself this week on what he did.”

Twelves, follow the full circle on this.

Last year, Sam Darnold couldn’t practice in the playoffs. So Drew Lock took the practice reps FOR him. Lock ran the offense all week so Darnold didn’t have to — and then Darnold played great on Sunday.

This year, the roles flipped. Darnold is hurt. Lock is starting. And Lock used the exact plan he helped Sam Darnold execute — the same template, with a three-minute adjustment.

“I was practicing for him.”

That’s a quarterback room, twelves. Not a competition. A room. Last January, Drew Lock’s job was to protect Sam Darnold’s arm. This November, the plan they built to do it is the plan that got Drew Lock to five and four.

And remember what Lock said about Darnold’s cracked rib last week — “an absolute soldier.” And what Macdonald said about both of them: “I don’t know how you get any tougher.”

These two men keep making each other better and neither one of them will say a bad word about the other. That’s rare, and this fanbase should appreciate it.

On whether he felt dialed from the start: “No, I’d say I felt pretty good going into it. The arm felt fresh. Felt good to start the game, and that’s kind of all the confidence I needed to be able to go out and not think about it and just play football.”

“The arm felt fresh.” Of course it did. He’d thrown on one day all week. That’s not an accident — that’s what the plan was FOR.

Now the running backs, and this answer made me laugh out loud.

Asked about Zach Charbonnet and Jadarian Price playing together for only the second time all year:

“Yeah. I’ll probably get in trouble for a couple reps — handing it off, not doing a fantastic keeper out, because I was looking back and watching Zach and JD run sometimes.”

The quarterback admitted he botched his own fake handoff mechanics because he was watching his running backs instead of selling the play.

And then he explained why:

“They were fighting through contact. First guy wasn’t tackling. Line was making great holes. Tight ends. I mean, it was classic run game from Zach and JD tonight. Along with our offensive line just playing their butts off, man. It was so fun.”

“First guy wasn’t tackling.”

Twelves, that phrase is the most important run-game statistic nobody tracks.

When the first defender to arrive can’t bring the back down, everything changes. The three-yard run becomes six. The second-level defender has to come up and now he’s out of his gap. And by the fourth quarter, a defense that has to send two men to tackle one running back is a tired defense.

That’s how Seattle closed this game out. Not explosive runs. First-contact runs that didn’t go down.

And remember what a national analyst said about these two men after the Bears game? His word was “rust.” He said Charbonnet looked rusty and Price looked “hesitant.”

One week later the quarterback’s word was “classic.”

And then the drive stat that tells the story.

A reporter pointed out that on the first touchdown drive, Jadarian Price and Zach Charbonnet combined for fifty-three of the fifty-five yards.

Fifty-three of fifty-five. The running backs essentially drove that entire series by themselves.

And Lock’s answer went bigger than that drive:

“Yeah, those guys are the workhorses of this team. I think it says a lot about the running back room in general, knowing that we didn’t have Charbonnet early in the year. Then we lost JD to the IR. With the guys behind them being able to step up, make plays when their number was called — to then get Zach and JD back... Zach having a couple games under his belt now after a very serious injury, you’re just going to keep seeing him come more and more to life. Hats off to that room. All of them have had their number called at some point, and in my opinion they’ve all been ready.”

“All of them have had their number called at some point, and they’ve all been ready.”

That’s the running back room’s whole season in one line. Charbonnet gone with a torn ACL. Price to injured reserve. Backups carrying the load for weeks. And now the two of them are back and the room is whole for only the second time all year.

Now the team answer, and he used Macdonald’s own words.

Asked how gratifying it is to win like that while missing so many men:

“Yeah, Mike preaches it. The ready squad — it’s seventy guys ready to roll at any time. It’s kind of just the mentality that this building has. So we knew that whoever it was stepping up — playing their first game, playing their hundredth snap, whatever it was — guys were going to be ready.”

“The ready squad. Seventy guys.”

Macdonald said it after the game too: “we said this game was like the power of our seventy.” The quarterback said it unprompted in his own press conference.

When the head coach’s phrase comes out of a player’s mouth without anybody feeding it to him, the message has actually landed. That’s culture working.

And he credited the staff: “coaches have to do a great job of putting together a plan on both sides of the ball for guys that haven’t played maybe all year. Is awesome.”

And then: “I keep going back to it, but this building is so special. This coaching staff — going into games, you feel confident as you’ve ever been. And we can just keep doing that. Keep progressing, keep getting better. Sky’s the limit.”

“Sky’s the limit.” From a man who threw a football on one day this week.

Now the aggressive drive before halftime — and his answer is about the unglamorous part of quarterbacking.

Asked if going downfield there was a plan or something he saw:

“No, I think it’s just flow — feeling the game, feeling it out. And as it always is, it’s my job to go out and execute and operationally get us up to the ball, keep the false starts minimal. And when he calls it downfield, I’m going to take it and drop back, I’m going to take it. Quick game, we’ll go from there.”

“Operationally get us up to the ball. Keep the false starts minimal.”

Twelves, that is the least glamorous and most important job description a quarterback has ever given on camera.

And it’s exactly what Mike Macdonald spent all last week talking about. His words: “we need to be better operationally... how we take the line, how we motion and shift, how we get in and out of plays.”

The coach said operations were the problem. The quarterback described his own job as operations. And on Sunday there was no sack and a four-minute closing drive.

Now the oblique and adrenaline — and this is a fascinating little piece of being an athlete.

Asked whether any throws hurt after the injury:

“No, it’s a crazy experience of being out there and just not feeling anything. Adrenaline — how important it is. It just kind of goes away. You kind of don’t think about it until you come sit down on the sideline. You’re like, ‘Oh yeah, that’s still there.’”

“You kind of don’t think about it until you come sit down on the sideline.”

And then the professional part: “I didn’t feel like there was anything I couldn’t do as far as throwing goes. So I relayed that to the sideline and we just kept moving forward.”

He reported it accurately and kept playing. That’s it. No drama.

Now — the thing I think actually won this football game, and he put it perfectly.

A reporter noted that’s now three games without a turnover, and asked whether that’s what it comes down to with this defense.

“I know that’s a big thing for me going into every week. Knowing that we can control the game with the run, operate on third downs well, keep the ball in our hands, allow our defense to have long fields to defend — not giving the team the ball on the thirty or the twenty, whatever it may be, even at the fifty. Playing complementary football.”

And then: “We have the best punter in the world. So getting to fourth down, it is what it is, when you have Michael Dickson being able to punt and pin guys deep and allow our defense to have a ninety-yard cushion, so to say.”

“We have the best punter in the world.”

Twelves, I love that this came up, because it’s the most underappreciated competitive advantage Seattle has.

Here’s the math. Starting field position is worth points. A drive that starts at your own eight yard line scores far less often than one starting at the forty. And Michael Dickson is the man who decides which one your opponent gets.

So Drew Lock’s version of winning football is: don’t turn it over, run it, convert third downs, and when the drive dies, hand it to the best punter alive and make them go ninety yards.

Arizona scored three points. Three. That’s the whole plan working.

And look at how this ties to Cooper Kupp’s complaint after the Bears game: “way too many three and outs, way too many short drives, just putting our defense back out on the field.”

On Sunday, Seattle did the opposite. Long drives, no turnovers, a four-minute close, and a punter burying them when it ended. That’s complementary football, named by the offense that failed at it eight days earlier.

AND NOW — the story. The one I promised you at the top.

A reporter asked how mental reps allowed him to hit tight windows over the middle without practice time.

And Drew Lock said: “I’ll actually give you the real truth.”

“That first drive — similar route to Jax, I kind of threw it in front of him a little too much. And we had a conversation on the sideline. I was like, ‘Look, dude, I was ready for you to kind of rip through there and run into that.’ And he’s talking to me about the safety and the backers and the holes. He’s feeling like he’s going to sit up a little bit. I was like, ‘All right.’”

“He did exactly what he told me he was going to do those next couple times we end up running that route.”

“And I went up to him after that drive and I said, ‘Never let me tell you how to run a route again. You keep doing what you’re doing. Tell me.’”

Twelves.

Let me walk you through what just happened there, because it’s the best football story of this season.

First drive. Drew Lock throws a route to Jaxon Smith-Njigba and leads him too much — he threw it where he expected JSN to be running. Incomplete, or close to it.

They meet on the sideline. And the quarterback tells the receiver what he wanted: rip through the window, keep moving, run into the ball.

And Jaxon Smith-Njigba says no.

Not rudely. He explains. He talks about the safety. He talks about the linebackers. He talks about the holes in the coverage. And he tells Drew Lock: based on what I’m seeing out there, I’m going to SIT UP a little bit — I’m going to stop in the window instead of running through it.

And he was right. He ran it his way the next couple of times and they connected.

And then Drew Lock — a quarterback with eight years in this league, in the middle of a game he’s starting — walked up to his receiver and said: “never let me tell you how to run a route again. You keep doing what you’re doing. Tell me.”

“TELL ME.”

Twelves, that is not a small thing. That is the quarterback handing over authority.

Because here’s the football reality: on an option route, the receiver is the only person on the field who can actually see the coverage from the inside. The quarterback sees shapes and leverage from eight yards behind the ball. The receiver sees the safety’s eyes, the linebacker’s hips, and exactly how much grass is in front of him.

And on those middle-of-the-field routes, the whole thing depends on the quarterback and the receiver agreeing — in advance, without words — on whether the man is going to sit down in the hole or run through it. Guess wrong and it’s an incompletion. Or worse, it’s a linebacker’s interception.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba told his quarterback what he was going to do. He did it. And the quarterback said: from now on, you tell me.

That is how trust gets built in real time. And it explains why the tight windows started getting completed after the first drive.

And can I just say — “never let me tell you how to run a route again, you keep doing what you’re doing” is the single greatest compliment a quarterback can give a receiver. Cooper Kupp called Jaxon Smith-Njigba “one of if not the best receiver in the league right now.” Drew Lock just said the same thing with different words: I’m not coaching you. You coach me.

Let me bring it home.

Here’s what you just learned about how Seattle won seventeen to three with six starters out.

A quarterback who threw a football on exactly one day this week. A pregame plan built all Saturday around not wasting a single throw. A template borrowed from the man he was practicing for last January. A medical tent visit in the third quarter that he answered by pointing out he’d already thrown. A running back room whole for the second time all year, combining for fifty-three of fifty-five yards on the first touchdown drive. Seventy men described as a ready squad. Three straight games without a turnover. The best punter in the world burying the field. And a receiver who told his quarterback how the route was going to be run — and was right.
