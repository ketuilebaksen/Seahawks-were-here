“We got him in a trade with the Colts right before the deadline, and I LOVE that move. He’s THIRTY years old, and he’s still ONE OF THE TOP INTERIOR LINEMEN IN THE LEAGUE.”

And then the line that puts real weight on these seven games: “the thing is, HE’S COMING OFF AN INJURY — so WHAT HE DOES IN THESE FINAL SEVEN GAMES IS PROBABLY GOING TO DECIDE HOW BIG THAT NEXT DEAL LOOKS.”

Twelves, let me lay out the actual stakes here, because they’re bigger than people realize.

Seattle gave up a 2027 THIRD-ROUND PICK and a 2028 FIFTH-ROUND PICK for Quenton Nelson.

And right now, as it stands today, they have gotten ONE GAME of football out of him. One. He played every offensive snap against Chicago, and then needed a knee procedure to clean out cartilage.

So if he walks in March, Seattle traded two draft picks for one game.

And that’s why this is a two-way negotiation and both sides have leverage. Nelson is thirty. This is likely his last big contract. If he comes back and dominates for seven games, his price goes UP. If the knee lingers, his price comes down — and so does Seattle’s willingness to commit multiple years.

And here’s the thing I keep coming back to: John Schneider’s whole model is built on exactly this. Acquire a man on an expiring deal, get him in your building, and extend him before anybody else can bid. That’s not a bug in the trade — that was the PLAN.

And the strongest argument for paying him came from Mike Macdonald himself: “he’s one of those guys that’s going to be a FORCE MULTIPLIER for our group — not just our offensive line, but really our whole offensive unit and our football team. SO IT’S WORTH THAT INITIAL INVESTMENT.”

“Worth that initial investment.” A head coach saying that in public about a man with an expiring contract is a message to the building, not to us.

And the vision for the line: “keep him, and the hope is that GREY ZABEL BECOMES A GOOD RIGHT GUARD next to him.”

Twelves, that’s the long game. Nelson at left guard. Zabel at right guard. Charles Cross at left tackle. That’s an interior you could build around for years — but ONLY if Nelson signs.

But the balanced verdict is the right one: “when Myers is ON, he’s ONE OF THE BEST KICKERS IN THE LEAGUE. I really think there’s INTEREST ON BOTH SIDES here. And he could even FINISH HIS CAREER IN SEATTLE. A KICKER YOU CAN TRUST IS WORTH A LOT.”

Twelves, I want to make the case for valuing a kicker properly, because fans systematically undervalue them.

Think about how many of Seattle’s games this season have been decided by three points or fewer. They lost at Washington by TWO. They lost at Denver by THREE. They lost to San Francisco by THREE.

That’s three games — and if Seattle had won all three, this team would be NINE AND ONE right now instead of six and four, and we wouldn’t be having a single conversation about the playoff race.

That’s what a kicker’s job actually is. Not highlights. Margins.

And three misses in ten games is not a crisis. It’s not perfect either, and in a contract year that’s exactly the kind of thing that shows up in negotiations.

Seven games to make his case.

NAME NUMBER THREE: JALEN SUNDELL — and twelves, this one has a mechanism that’s genuinely great news.

“His situation is a little different. He’s an EXCLUSIVE RIGHTS FREE AGENT, which means SEATTLE IS THE ONLY TEAM THAT CAN NEGOTIATE WITH HIM. So yeah, I’d say they’re keeping him.”

And let me explain exclusive rights free agency, because it’s the best possible version of a contract situation.

Twelves, there are tiers here and they matter enormously.

An UNRESTRICTED free agent can sign anywhere. You have no control. That’s Quenton Nelson’s situation.

A RESTRICTED free agent can negotiate elsewhere, but you get to MATCH the offer. That’s Ty Okada’s situation, which I covered last week — you keep him unless somebody prices you out.

An EXCLUSIVE RIGHTS free agent cannot talk to anybody else at all. Not one team. If Seattle offers him a qualifying contract, his only options are sign it or don’t play football.

That’s the tier where the team has ALL the leverage.

And why it matters: “why wouldn’t they keep him? THE GUY HAS PLAYED EVERY SINGLE SNAP THIS SEASON AS OUR STARTING CENTER. EVERY SNAP.”

Twelves, EVERY SNAP. Let that land.

Seattle’s offensive line this season has been a disaster area. Charles Cross out multiple games. Abe Lucas hurt. Quenton Nelson traded for and then surgically repaired. Grey Zabel moved to right guard and then back to left. Anthony Bradford benched and then restored. Bobby Hart playing meaningful snaps. Josh Jones filling in at tackle.

And through every single bit of that chaos, ONE MAN never left the field. The center.

And twelves, the center is the position where that matters MOST. He makes the line calls. He identifies the front. He declares the Mike linebacker. He’s the one who has to communicate the protection adjustment when a blitzer shows late.

Mike Macdonald told us about this exact problem — that his own defense tries to create it: “we’re not just going to line up and snap the ball on the quick every snap... HOW WE TAKE THE LINE, how we motion and shift, how we get in and out of plays.”

The center is the man who runs all of that. And while four of the five spots around him rotated, HE was the constant.

Nobody is going to make a highlight video about a center playing every snap. But Seattle’s run game taking a step — Zach Charbonnet going for ninety-four yards on twenty carries behind three replacement starters — happens through the middle of that line.

And the money: “he could earn up to FIVE MILLION DOLLARS on his next deal. So he’s got EVERY REASON TO KEEP PLAYING HIS BEST FOOTBALL DOWN THE STRETCH.”

Twelves, five million for a starting center who has played every snap is, frankly, a bargain in today’s league. If that’s the number, you sign it and you don’t negotiate.
