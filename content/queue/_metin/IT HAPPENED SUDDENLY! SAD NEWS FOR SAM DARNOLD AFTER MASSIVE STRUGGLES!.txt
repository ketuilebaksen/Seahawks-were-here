“I should have just gotten it down to Coop.”

That’s Sam Darnold. Out loud, to reporters, hours after losing a football game in overtime.

And then he kept going: “Coop ran a really good route right under the sticks, and I feel like he would have caught it and got the first down.”

Seahawks Nation, if you want sad news about Sam Darnold, that’s the sad news. Not a benching. Not a trade. A quarterback standing at a podium, naming his own teammate by nickname, describing the route that man ran correctly, and admitting he didn’t throw him the ball.

Because there IS sad news here. It’s just quieter and much more human than a benching.

Let’s go to the throw.

Overtime. Seattle has the ball first. Third-and-seven from Seattle’s own twenty-eight. And here’s where I have to correct the document, because it gets this backwards in a small way that matters.

The document says Darnold “got greedy” and that Cooper Kupp was open underneath. Half right. What the reporting actually says is that Darnold got greedy trying to hit Rashid Shaheed — the deeper, bigger play — and what he regretted was not coming down to Kupp underneath.

So it’s not that he chose Shaheed over nobody. He chose the thirty-yard throw over the eight-yard throw, and the eight-yard throw was open.

Ja’Quan McMillian intercepted it. Denver took over at the Seattle 43. Three plays, eight yards, and Wil Lutz hit a 53-yard field goal. Game.

Now let me teach you the two football ideas sitting inside that one play, because once you have them you’ll see this throw every single Sunday for the rest of your life.

Idea one: the hole shot in cover two. Macdonald’s own description of the play was “I think he’s trying to throw a hole shot in cover two.” Cover two means two safeties split the deep field in half and the cornerbacks sit underneath. That leaves one soft spot — the gap between the corner underneath and the safety over the top, along the sideline. That window is called the hole. It’s maybe two yards wide, it closes as the safety rotates, and it has to be thrown before the receiver arrives, which means the quarterback is committing to a patch of grass instead of to a man. Hit it, it’s thirty yards. Miss by a beat and the safety is standing exactly where your football is going.

Idea two — and this is the beautiful part — “sitting at the sticks.” Darnold’s own words were that Kupp “ran a really good route right under the sticks.” The sticks are the first-down markers. A receiver sitting at the sticks has done the least glamorous and most valuable job in football: he’s gotten himself exactly as far as you need, turned around, and made himself available. No highlight. No separation. Just — here, right here, this is first down.

On third-and-seven in overtime, you do not need thirty yards. You need seven. And Sam Darnold knew that twenty minutes later at a podium, which is the cruelest possible timing for knowing something.

That’s the sad news. A good quarterback, in a huge moment, took the big thing instead of the right thing, and then described his own mistake more precisely than any analyst could.

And his follow-up line is the one I actually respect most: “So just got to continue to go through my progressions and trust it.”

Trust it. That’s a man telling you the problem isn’t his arm and isn’t the scheme — it’s that in a tight moment he stopped believing the simple answer was good enough. Every single one of us has done that version of it in our own lives.

Now here’s the quote that should have been the headline, and the document had it and softened it.

Cooper Kupp — the receiver who was open, the man with every right to be frustrated — said this:

“We are not doing a good enough job offensively to allow Sam to play in rhythm.”

And then: “But it’s not really fair for him to shoulder all of it when we all definitely have a role in what’s gone on.”

Read that first sentence again, because it’s extraordinary. The open receiver did not say “I was open.” He said the offense isn’t good enough to let the quarterback play in rhythm.

That is a veteran who has won a Super Bowl MVP taking the heat off his quarterback by pointing at the structure around him. And the word he chose — rhythm — is exactly the right one. A quarterback in rhythm takes the snap, hits his third step, and the ball is gone. A quarterback out of rhythm is hitching, resetting, climbing the pocket and then deciding. That’s where greedy throws live: in the extra second you never wanted.

So let’s put the real numbers on it, both the ugly ones and the ones that explain them.

The ugly first, because I’m not here to spin for anybody. Darnold has seven interceptions in five appearances. That is tied for the most in the National Football League, level with Drake Maye and Justin Herbert. All seven have come since he returned from the glute injury. Against Denver: 27 of 51, 278 yards, no touchdowns, two interceptions, a 52.6 passer rating.

And it was actually three turnovers in that game, not two — a lost fumble on top of the two picks. The document gets that right and most coverage skips it.

He’s also taken 14 sacks this season. And the document says Denver had 17 quarterback hits in that one game — I couldn’t independently verify that figure, so I’m attributing it to them rather than handing it to you as fact, but five sacks is confirmed and the shape of it is obviously right.

And one more thing I’ll put on the table because honesty cuts both ways: in 2025, the championship season, Darnold threw 14 interceptions and lost 11 fumbles. So this is not a new flaw that appeared in October. It’s the same quarterback. The difference last year is that everything around him was better.

Which brings us to the numbers that explain it. In Denver, Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks. Under that pressure he went 6-of-19 for 47 yards. Nineteen of his fifty-one attempts were thrown with a man arriving. Five sacks against Denver, five sacks the week before against San Francisco.

And Anthony Bradford, the starting right guard, has been out since September, with a second-year player and a rookie — Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens — rotating in his place and both allowing multiple pressures on Thursday.

That’s what Cooper Kupp means by rhythm. You cannot be in rhythm when two out of every five snaps has a stranger in your face.

The document also mentions that Darnold ranks 24th of 30 in QBR with the second-highest interception rate among qualified quarterbacks. I wasn’t able to verify those two specific rankings myself, so I’m crediting them to the document rather than stating them as confirmed. But nothing about them sounds wrong.

Now, the Drew Lock question — because the document raises it fairly and it deserves a real answer, not a dismissal.

Lock’s numbers while Darnold was hurt are not small. He played most of two games — the New England opener after Darnold went down, and Week 2 against Arizona — and he went 35 of 48 for 422 yards, four touchdowns and zero interceptions. Seattle went 2-0.

Four scores, no picks, two wins. That is a genuine argument, and anyone making it is not being stupid.

And Macdonald didn’t dismiss it either. That’s what makes Friday so interesting. He said: “We have a great backup quarterback in Drew.” And: “Drew is a tremendous quarterback. He’s a starting-level quarterback.”

Then he said no anyway: “But I think these are solutions that we can find with Sam as our quarterback. I believe that.”

So why? Here’s the honest reasoning, and it isn’t loyalty.

Drew Lock would inherit the identical situation. Same missing right guard. Same 41 percent pressure rate. Same run game that’s fallen from 123.3 yards a game to 107.2. Same first-year coordinator. Same bracket on Smith-Njigba. Nothing about a quarterback change touches a single one of those four things.

And then the uncomfortable part, which I’d want said to me if I were arguing the other side: 48 pass attempts is not a sample. It’s a great two weeks. Darnold has thrown 51 passes in one night. A quarterback’s turnover problems don’t reveal themselves in two games — they reveal themselves in twelve. The honest read on Lock’s four-touchdown, zero-interception stretch is that it was excellent and that we have no idea whether it survives a full month of being hit five times a game.

That’s the choice, stated fairly: a known quarterback playing badly in a bad environment, or an unknown quarterback entering the same bad environment. Macdonald picked the first one and said out loud that he’d rather fix the environment. I think that’s defensible. I also think it’s a decision with a deadline on it, and I’ll say so if the pressure rate drops and the interceptions don’t.

And here’s what “fixing the environment” actually looks like in a ten-day break, because that phrase gets used a lot and almost never explained.

One: get the ball out faster. More quick game — three-step throws where the ball is gone before the rush matters. The slant, the hitch, the stick route. You cannot sack a quarterback who has already thrown.

Two: screens. A screen is a run play wearing a pass play’s clothes — it turns an aggressive pass rush into a liability, because the harder the defensive line charges, the more open grass there is behind them.

Three: play-action. But understand the catch, because this is the one people get wrong. Play-action is a loan against your run game. It only works if the defense believes you’ll hand it off. At 107.2 rushing yards a game, Seattle’s credit is running thin — which is exactly why Charbonnet’s deadline on the twenty-second isn’t a separate story from Darnold’s interceptions. A real run game is what buys the play-action that buys the clean pocket.

Now let me give the document real credit, because the rest of it is genuinely good work and I enjoyed reading it.

It picks winners out of that loss and it picks them correctly.

Rashid Shaheed had one of the best bounce-back games you’ll see. He fumbled in the first half and it cost Seattle points — the kind of mistake that can swallow a player’s whole night. And I loved the detail about the uniforms, so let me give you the history behind it. Seattle wore the royal blue throwbacks, Denver wore orange crush, and the reason that pairing exists at all is that the Seattle Seahawks played in the AFC West from 1977 until 2001. Denver was a division rival for twenty-five years. Seattle moved to the NFC West in 2002. So if that matchup looked and felt older than it should have — it was.

So where does that leave us, honestly?

The document’s own conclusion is “a little of both, but decisive,” and I think that’s close to exactly right. Here’s my version.

Seattle is 3-3, and the three losses have come by two points, three points and three points. Eight points, total. This is not a broken team. It’s a championship roster losing the last two minutes of football games.

And the two causes are the same in every single one: turnovers and pressure. Those are not separate problems. Pressure creates the turnovers. You fix the second one and the first one shrinks on its own.

And the fixes are in the building. All of them. This week.

Charbonnet. October twenty-second. Three days. The twenty-one-day practice window that opened October first closes that day. Either he goes on the fifty-three-man roster or he’s gone for the season. And remember the distinction: activating him is paperwork; playing him is football. He may be on the roster and still not ready.

Bradford. Eligible off injured reserve. Macdonald on Friday: “has a really good chance of being back this week.” That’s the single most important thing that can happen to Sam Darnold’s season, and nobody’s making videos about a right guard.

Elijah Arroyo. Shoulder. Still no public result from the imaging. Macdonald: “He has a shoulder... he obviously wasn’t able to finish.” Watch for it.

And then Kansas City, October twenty-fifth — where Kenneth Walker the Third, the Super Bowl MVP who now plays for the Chiefs, arrives with 537 rushing yards in four games. The entire Seattle team has 530 in five.

Let me close.

The sad news about Sam Darnold isn’t that he’s losing his job. He isn’t. His head coach backed him in public while openly calling the backup a starting-level quarterback, which is about as strong a vote as a coach can cast.

The sad news is eleven words: “I should have just gotten it down to Coop.”

That’s a man who will carry one throw with him through a ten-day break. And the most Seattle thing about it is that Cooper Kupp — the open guy — spent his podium time defending him. “We are not doing a good enough job offensively to allow Sam to play in rhythm.”

That’s a locker room, by the way. That’s what one looks like from the outside. Teams that are actually falling apart do not sound like that.
