Let me walk you through the ledger, because it's beautiful and brutal at the same time.

Per Over The Cap's projections, reported out by Brady Henderson of ESPN, Seattle is in line for twelve selections in the 2027 draft. That's seven native picks, four projected compensatory picks, and a conditional seventh-rounder from the trade last summer that sent backup offensive tackle Michael Jerrell to Atlanta.

Compensatory picks in plain English: when you lose a good free agent and don't replace him with somebody equally expensive, the league gives you a draft pick back. A consolation prize for being outbid. The better the player and the bigger the contract he signed elsewhere, the higher the pick.

Here are Seattle's four, and read the names slowly:

A fourth-round pick for Boye Mafe, who signed with the Cincinnati Bengals.

And three separate fifth-round picks — for Kenneth Walker the Third going to Kansas City, Coby Bryant going to Chicago, and Riq Woolen going to Philadelphia.

An edge rusher, a running back, a starting nickel and a starting corner. Four real players, all gone in one offseason. It was described at the time as a brutal free agency for this franchise, and it was.

And the entire thing converts into four extra draft picks in a draft that Adam Schefter described like this: "It is going to be a quarterback-deep, strong draft, which is going to add to the value of those picks."

Sit with that last part, because it's the strategic key and it's subtle.

A quarterback-deep draft doesn't just help the teams that need a quarterback. It raises the price of everybody's picks — because quarterback-needy teams start trading up, and when teams trade up, somebody has to be selling. Seattle will be holding twelve chips in a seller's market.

That's why we're talking about college football in October on a Seahawks channel. Not because 2027 is more fun than Sunday. Because Seattle's 2027 is already, quietly, the most interesting draft position in this division.

Number one. Julian Sayin, quarterback, Ohio State.

Against Illinois on September twenty-sixth, he went twenty-seven of thirty-six for 367 yards and four touchdowns, and he ran for one more. Ohio State won 42-19. He was named a Manning Award Star of the Week and put on the Davey O'Brien Great Eight list.

Now I know the first objection, because I had it too: it's easy to throw for 367 yards when Jeremiah Smith is running your routes and five future NFL linemen are protecting you. That's a fair point and I'm not dismissing it.

But here's the number that has nothing to do with his receivers, and it's the one that made me sit up.

Julian Sayin has completed 75.1 percent of his passes for his entire career. He is the most accurate quarterback in the history of Ohio State football — and that figure would be an NCAA record for anybody with at least 875 career attempts.

An NCAA record. Not a good season. The most accurate anybody has ever been at that volume.

He's also at 43 career touchdown passes, tenth in Ohio State history, and 4,900 career passing yards, fourteenth — and Illinois was his eighth three-hundred-yard game, tied for third in program history.

A great receiver gives you yards. Accuracy at that level over nine hundred throws is the quarterback.

Seattle angle, honestly: Seattle is not drafting a quarterback in round one. Darnold's deal runs through 2027 and Jalen Milroe is a third-rounder they still believe in. Sayin is not a Seahawk.

But he's the single biggest reason Seattle's twelve picks matter. He's the guy somebody trades a fortune to go get — and every fortune spent at the top of a draft flows through the teams who are selling.

Number two, and this is the one that actually matters to this football team. Da'Marion Bothwell, running back, Mississippi State.

You'll see him listed everywhere as "Fluff." His given name is Da'Marion. He's five-foot-ten, 230 pounds, out of Oneonta, Alabama.

And his high school tape is absurd. As a junior he ran for 3,112 yards and 41 touchdowns on 256 carries, caught 22 passes, returned a punt for a score — and then played defense, where he had 66 tackles, 19 for loss and five sacks. He was a three-star recruit, which should embarrass somebody.

He went to South Alabama, made third-team All-Sun Belt in 2024, then transferred to Mississippi State. And in the upset over Missouri: 122 rushing yards, 55 receiving yards, and a touchdown both ways.

Here's why I want you to write his name down.

A 230-pound back who catches the football, projected as a day-three pick. And Seattle is going to have three fifth-round compensatory picks — one of which exists because Kenneth Walker left.

You lose a running back, the league hands you a fifth-rounder, and you spend it on a 230-pound running back who scored two different ways in an SEC upset. That's not a coincidence. That's the business model.

Number four, and the actual best story of the week. Austin Romaine, linebacker, Texas Tech.

Forget the stat line. Here's what happened.

September twenty-sixth, Texas Tech versus Sam Houston, Texas Tech up 7-0. Linebacker Brock Gowlas deflects a pass at the line, Romaine catches it and takes it fifteen yards for a touchdown. Sam Houston kicks off. On the very first play after the kickoff, a different Sam Houston quarterback throws a first-down pass, Romaine intercepts it and takes it thirty-one yards for another one.

Twenty-one to nothing. Seven seconds apart. Two pick-sixes on consecutive throws, off two different quarterbacks.

He is the first player in FBS football to score on consecutive interceptions since DeVon Edwards of Duke in 2013, and the first Texas Tech player with two pick-sixes in a game since Kevin Curtis and Antwan Alexander against Oklahoma State in 2000. Texas Tech won 49-14 and he took Big 12 weekly honors.
