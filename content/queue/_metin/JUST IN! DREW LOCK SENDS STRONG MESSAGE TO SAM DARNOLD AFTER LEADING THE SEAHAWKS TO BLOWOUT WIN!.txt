Today we're going through Drew Lock's full postgame press conference after the 31-7 win in Arizona — because there is so much in here that tells you why this team is 2-0, why the streak is at twelve, and why this quarterback room might be the most underrated story in the NFL.

Let's start with how he described the game, because it was the perfect summary.

He was asked how it felt, and he said it was a complete game from both sides of the ball — and special teams as well. And he gave the defense the credit first. "Our defense is very good. Giving us the ball in some advantageous situations." Then he talked about how weird that first quarter was. He said he thinks the offense only had something like five plays. Stop, get the ball, score quick, defense back out there, three-and-out, defense back out there again — and they just kept chugging along.

That's the rhythm of this team, isn't it? The defense and the offense feeding each other. Every time Arizona got the ball, it came right back. And on Seattle's second offensive play, that's when the 82-yard touchdown happened.

And here's the detail from Lock about that play that I absolutely love. He was asked whether it was designed as a go route the whole time. And he said no. It was not a go route. Jaxon Smith-Njigba turned it into one.

Lock said he never expected JSN to take an inside release on that corner — that's just not a typical thing. But if the corner is going to play him that far outside and wants to take away vertical, JSN just went inside and made it vertical anyway. Lock said he was a little surprised by how JSN did it — he thought he was going to do it one way and it happened another. And then he said the line that tells you what it's like to play with the best receiver in football: "He's going to find ways to get open. It might not be the most conventional, but that's what makes him a ball player, and that's what makes him one of the best receivers — Offensive Player of the Year last year. That's just what he is."

And then — this is the part that made me respect Lock even more — he criticized himself. On an 82-yard touchdown. He said he probably could have gotten the ball out a step sooner, but sometimes with JSN you have to see how he comes out of his break. An 82-yard touchdown and the quarterback's first thought is "I could've been a step faster." That's the maturity he was talking about.

Now touchdown number two, and this is where you really see how much Lock learned this week.

He walked the media through the whole thing. Seattle ran a motion pushing a defender out wide to the right. Arizona was showing him a two-high shell — which he said was actually disappointing, because he wanted to go to a specific route and the shell made it harder. Then he lifted his leg to start the snap count, and both safeties started creeping down. Arizona ended up bringing zero — an all-out blitz, no deep safety help — with a couple of what he called "poppers," late blitzers who pop into the rush.

And he explained the rules like a coach. Against zero, it's his job to get the ball out, because you simply don't have enough bodies to block everybody. But when the poppers come, his job becomes avoiding them. JSN ran a fantastic route, Lock got the throw off through the rush, and Arizona ended up giving him — in his words — "the look I was personally hoping for." And it worked. Touchdown.

Think about what that means. A backup quarterback, on the road, read an all-out blitz before the snap, adjusted his plan mid-cadence, stood in against free rushers, and delivered a touchdown. That's not managing a game. That's playing quarterback at a high level.

And now the best story in the whole press conference. Sam Darnold.

Lock said that on Thursday, Seattle had a walkthrough that was all third down, all pressure. The coaches were "heating us up" — throwing blitzes at him, making him pick them up, flip protections, throw hot. And Lock admitted something honest: "I was swimming a little bit."

So what did he do? He walked straight up to Sam Darnold and said, "Sam, can you meet me upstairs and talk me through some of these protections some more?" Darnold was waiting for him while Lock did his media session, and then they went through it together. And Lock said it "drastically helped." Hearing it from the coaches, hearing it from Sam, going home and studying it, until he'd wrapped his head around the whole thing.

Seahawks Nation, think about that for a second. The injured starter — the Super Bowl-winning quarterback — sitting upstairs with his backup, walking him through blitz protections so the backup can go win a game in his place. That's what this locker room is.

Lock was asked if every quarterback would do that. And he said you'd like to think every quarterback room would. "But Sam is a special breed of guy." And then he said that if the situation were reversed, he'd have done the same thing for Sam — and that being able to receive that help now, after years of being the one supporting the starter, has been awesome. "I really appreciate him for everything he's done."

And when he was asked whether Darnold understands this better because he's been a backup himself, Lock said yes — Sam knows what it's like standing back there, watching, learning, staying ready for whenever it's your turn to play again. He knows the life.

That's two quarterbacks who have both been doubted, both been backups, both been told they weren't the answer — and they're pulling each other up. No wonder there's no quarterback controversy in Seattle. There's a quarterback brotherhood.

And he didn't stop with Darnold. He credited Brian Fleury and the offensive staff for getting him ready, and he talked about the whole quarterback room helping him all week. And then he said something every Seahawks fan should hear. He said he always talks about how much he loves this building and that it's the reason he came back — and that this week, it "kind of just played out." Feeling the love from everybody. The skill guys. The offensive line. "It's just a great group of guys."

Remember, Drew Lock left Seattle after 2023, went to the Giants, and then chose to come back. On Sunday he told you why.

Let's talk about that offensive line, because Lock could not stop talking about them. "Holy cow," he said. "It's awesome playing behind those guys." They picked him up all week, talked through everything with him. And he said the line had to adjust to his cadence this week — think about that, a whole offensive line re-learning the rhythm of a new quarterback's voice in a few days.

And then the drive that ended the game. Lock said all he had to do on that drive was say "set, hut," and hand it off to "Eman" — Emanuel Wilson, who finished with 21 carries for 91 yards. And Lock said Wilson did a phenomenal job.

He connected it to the culture, too. He said pushing piles is something Mike Macdonald talks about constantly. Finishing forward, never backwards. And he said you could look at every single play on that closing drive and see the team trying to do exactly that — and almost always succeeding. And then he gave a shout-out to Jaxon Smith-Njigba again, for a little screen to the right on that drive where JSN made a guy miss, got vertical, and stretched out for a first down to keep it alive.

And the fourth-quarter touchdown fit that exact same identity — AJ Barner punched it in on a tush push. A straight-ahead, push-the-pile, finish-forward play. Macdonald's philosophy, in one snap.

Next, Lock was asked about something I think a lot of fans wondered: when you know all week that you're the starter, do you fight the urge to overthink everything?

His answer was really thoughtful. He said yes — you definitely step out of your normal routines. He watched more film. He studied a little extra. He looked at things he normally wouldn't look at during a week as a backup, because he felt like there were things he needed more clarity on. But he said it wasn't a negative thing — he actually enjoyed that process. It's just what's mandatory when you're stepping in to lead a team for four quarters.

And when he was asked how he handled the looks in the game that the coaches had thrown at him in practice, he was honest again: "Early in the week, not so great. By Sunday, not too bad." And he said that's exactly the progression you want. Make the mistakes early, have them fixed by game day. That's the whole week in one sentence — the swimming on Thursday, the meeting upstairs with Sam, and then the zero-blitz touchdown on Sunday.

He also got a question about JSN lining up at the X receiver spot and getting "clouded" — that's when a defense rolls a corner and a safety over the top to double him. And Lock said he notices it at the line when it's a three-by-one set and JSN is isolated, but that the coaches have done a fantastic job moving JSN around — different spots, motions, shifts — so defenses can't just lock him down. As Lock put it, you don't come out and play that well without a great plan in place.

And there was a lighter moment too. Lock was asked what he thought when Rashid Shaheed fumbled a kickoff. He laughed and said it was one of those moments where you turn and start walking the other way — and then the whole sideline gets excited, and he's like, "Oh my gosh, Chazz picked it up." Chazz Surratt recovered it, and Seattle capitalized. Heck of a play, he said.

And finally, the celebration. On his last touchdown pass — the third one to JSN — Lock had an emphatic celebration, looking toward the sideline. And he explained it. He said that one felt really good because it was a pivotal moment. It made it 24-7. A three-score game instead of a two-score game. He said there was just a lot of emotion in getting that drive done.
