That’s Drew Lock’s passer rating in his most recent start. The one before that was 113.3.

Put the two games together: thirty-five of forty-eight, 422 yards, four touchdowns, zero interceptions. Seventy-three percent completions. And the Seattle Seahawks went 2-0.

Seahawks Nation, that is the most under-discussed stat line on this roster, and I’m going to spend real time on it today. Because Drew Lock matters enormously to this football team — more than almost anybody realizes — and on Friday, Seattle listed him as questionable with a right elbow.

Three quick things about the title, and then I’m going to make the strongest honest case for Drew Lock you’ll hear anywhere.

One — and this one’s just geography: Seattle plays in the NFC, not the AFC. The NFC West. So “AFC” won’t survive thirty seconds in a comment section.

Although — fun fact, and it actually came up two weeks ago: the Seahawks played in the AFC West from 1977 until 2001. Denver was a division rival for twenty-five years. Seattle moved to the NFC in 2002. So “Seahawks” and “AFC” used to belong in the same sentence — just not since the Bush administration.

Two: Drew Lock is the backup. Sam Darnold is the starter, and Macdonald shut that door as firmly as he’s shut anything. Asked directly about a quarterback change: “No, no.”

Three: he’s questionable. Right elbow. He may not dress tonight.

So no, I’m not going to sell you “AFC super monster.” But here’s the thing — the real Drew Lock story this week is more urgent than the headline, not less. Because his availability genuinely matters tonight, and almost nobody is talking about it.

Let me start with the case for him, because it’s strong and it deserves to be made properly.

Drew Lock started Seattle’s first two games while Darnold was out — Week 1 against New England and Week 2 at Arizona. Here’s what he did:

Thirty-five completions on forty-eight attempts. 422 passing yards. Four touchdowns. Zero interceptions. Two carries for thirteen yards. Passer ratings of 113.3 and 139.1.

A 139.1 passer rating. For context, anything over 100 is a very good day. Anything over 120 is a dominant one. 139 is the kind of number that wins a player of the week award.

Seattle beat New England 13-10 and beat Arizona 31-7. Two games, two wins, four scores, no turnovers.

And his head coach has been unusually direct about him. Not vague coach-speak — actual praise:

“We have a great backup quarterback in Drew.”

And: “Drew is a tremendous quarterback. He’s a starting-level quarterback.”

A head coach called his backup a starting-level quarterback, in public, in the same press conference where he confirmed he was keeping the other guy. That is not a thing coaches normally do, and it tells you what they think of Drew Lock inside that building.

And now the honest caveat, because I’m not going to oversell him either.

Forty-eight pass attempts is not a season. Sam Darnold threw fifty-one passes in one night in Denver. Lock’s two-game stretch was genuinely excellent, and we have no idea whether it survives twelve games of being hit five times a week — which is exactly the environment Darnold is currently playing in.

That’s precisely why Macdonald’s reasoning on sticking with Darnold holds up: Drew Lock would inherit the identical situation — same pressure rate, same run game, same first-year coordinator — and a quarterback change touches none of it.

So Drew Lock is not the answer to Seattle’s offense. He is something arguably more valuable: the best insurance policy in the National Football League.

And let me give you the career arc, because it makes those two games mean more than they look.

Here’s Drew Lock’s last three seasons in Seattle, and I want you to watch the shape of it.

2024: 1,071 passing yards, six touchdowns, five interceptions, plus 133 rushing yards and two rushing touchdowns. A real workload — a man getting actual snaps.

2025: fifteen passing yards.

Fifteen. For the entire season. That’s the year Seattle won the Super Bowl. Drew Lock spent a championship campaign holding a clipboard, throwing fifteen yards all year, and watching another man lift the trophy.

2026: two starts, 422 yards, four touchdowns, zero interceptions, 2-0.

Go from fifteen passing yards in a title season to winning the first two games of the title defense. That is a specific kind of professional discipline, and it’s the entire argument for why teams pay real money for backup quarterbacks.

Because here’s the thing about the job: you prepare every single week like you’re starting, and you almost never start. For an entire year Lock did the full install, the full film, the full walkthrough — and threw fifteen yards. Then Darnold went down on the first drive of a season opener and he had to be ready inside of one quarter.

And he was. Thirteen-ten over New England, then thirty-one to seven over Arizona.

That’s not luck. That’s a man who did the unglamorous version of the job for a full year and then cashed it in when it counted.

And here’s the part almost nobody understands about what a backup quarterback actually does all week — which is why his elbow matters even if he never takes a snap tonight.

A backup quarterback has two jobs, and the second one is invisible.

Job one is obvious: be ready to play.

Job two is that he runs the scout team. All week, in practice, the backup quarterback stands in and plays the role of the opposing quarterback so the defense can rehearse against something that looks like Sunday.

Think about what that means this week. Seattle’s defense needs to practice against Patrick Mahomes. Against the RPO game. Against under-center play-action. Against a quarterback who gets the ball out decisively on early downs — which is exactly what Macdonald described this week.

Somebody has to be Patrick Mahomes in a Thursday practice. And if your backup quarterback’s throwing arm is sore, the quality of that rehearsal drops — which means your defense goes into Sunday night having practiced against a worse imitation of what it’s about to face.

So Drew Lock’s elbow doesn’t just affect the fourth quarter. It affects the whole week of preparation that leads into it.

That’s why “questionable, right elbow” on a backup is a bigger line item than it looks on a sheet of paper. The second quarterback shapes how well your defense is trained, every week, whether he ever plays or not.

And here’s why that insurance policy is the real story tonight.

Look at what’s happened to Seattle’s quarterback over the last two games. Sam Darnold has been sacked five times against Denver and five times against San Francisco. He’s taken fourteen sacks on the season. In Denver he was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks and went 6-of-19 for 47 yards under that pressure.

That is a quarterback getting hit on two out of every five snaps.

Now add this: Seattle listed Drew Lock as questionable with a right elbow on Friday.

And it’s not the first flare-up. Trace the arm:

October twelfth and thirteenth — Lock was a limited practice participant with a forearm injury, and he did not play in the Week 5 loss to San Francisco.

October fourteenth — the forearm no longer carried a designation and he was expected to be available as the primary backup at Denver.

October twenty-third — questionable, right elbow.

That’s a throwing arm that has now been on the report twice in two weeks.

So here’s the sentence nobody is saying out loud: on a night when Seattle’s starting quarterback is getting hit five times a game, the most important insurance policy on the roster has a sore throwing arm.

That’s not a hot take. That’s the final injury report.

And let me explain why it matters more than a normal backup designation, because the roster mechanics here are genuinely important.

An NFL team dresses a limited number of players on game day. Quarterback is the only position where losing two men in one night can end your game outright.

The league allows teams to designate an emergency third quarterback who can enter the game only if the first two are unavailable due to injury — a rule written specifically because teams kept finding themselves with nobody left who could throw. But that emergency option is exactly that: an emergency. It’s not a plan. It’s a parachute.

So if Lock can’t dress tonight, Seattle plays a Sunday night game against a 5-0 team with its starting quarterback behind a line that’s allowed five sacks in two straight weeks — and no real second option behind him.

That’s the quiet risk in this game, and it’s the honest reason Drew Lock matters tonight. Not as a monster. As a seatbelt.

Now — the part you came for. Can Seattle win this game? Yes. And I’ll give you the real reasons, not the hopeful ones.

Reason one, and it’s the biggest: Kansas City may be starting two backup offensive tackles.

Jaylon Moore is OUT with a concussion. And Josh Simmons was reportedly placed on injured reserve this week with a back issue. That’s potentially both starting tackles, on a Sunday night, on the road.

And who’s lining up across from them? DeMarcus Lawrence and Derick Hall, in front of a defense that held the Denver Broncos to 168 total yards eight days ago. Seattle outgained Denver 353 to 168 in that game — more than double.

If Seattle’s edge rushers win one-on-one against backup tackles, Patrick Mahomes does not get to play the game he wants to play. That is the single most important matchup on the field tonight.

Reason two: Kansas City’s passing game is compromised.

Rashee Rice is OUT with a hamstring. Travis Kelce was limited all week with an ankle. That’s two of Mahomes’s most trusted targets either gone or hurting, in the same game, behind two backup tackles.

Reason three: Anthony Bradford is back, and he carries no game designation.

He was upgraded to full participation on Thursday after five games on injured reserve with knee and hip injuries. And Macdonald said something on Monday that he almost never says: “I know normally I’m pretty coy on this one, but it’s pretty straightforward. If he has a good week, he’s going to play a significant amount, if not the whole game.”

The week went well. The right guard is back. And he’s described as “a physical presence for us at right guard, both in run and pass.”

That is the direct answer to the 41 percent pressure rate. Not a trade. Not a quarterback change. A guard.

Reason four: L’Jarius Sneed is questionable. If Kansas City’s corner is limited or out, that’s where Jaxon Smith-Njigba feasts — and he’s at 717 receiving yards in six games, roughly 49 percent of all of Seattle’s receiving yards, the highest share of any player in the National Football League.

Reason five: the matchup Kansas City can’t scheme around — Seattle’s defensive front against a line that’s been rebuilt on the fly.

Macdonald said something this week about his defensive line that’s worth holding onto, because it’s the group this game rests on: “When we’ve played great, our best football, those guys are on fire and they set the tone for the rest of the defense.” And: “I think they’re the first ones to say, like, when we’re not meeting up to our standard, they could play better as well.”

That’s Leonard Williams — three years, ninety million dollars, twenty-one sacks since 2024, the most of any defensive tackle in football — plus Jarran Reed inside and Lawrence and Hall outside. A veteran front that polices itself.

And the thing that makes it dangerous tonight: Macdonald also explained that Seattle deliberately plays a light box and two-high safeties specifically to “take stress off our D-linemen.” Which means those four men get one-on-one opportunities by design — against two backup tackles.

Reason six, and this is the one that should give you actual confidence: Seattle has not been beaten this year. They’ve been out-possessed at the margins.

Three losses, by two points, three points and three points. Eight points total. Thirty-three to thirty-one at Washington. Thirty-two to twenty-nine against San Francisco. Seventeen to fourteen in overtime at Denver.

That is not a team that’s gotten worse. That’s a team that’s lost three coin flips. And coin flips at home, on Sunday night, in front of Lumen Field, land differently.

Zach Charbonnet is OUT. Activated Thursday, two full practices, ruled out Friday — and Macdonald confirmed no setback. He’s eleven months off an ACL and Seattle chose the long game.

Drake Thomas is OUT (ankle). Chazz Surratt is OUT (calf). And Uchenna Nwosu, Connor O’Toole, Nick Emmanwori, DeMarcus Lawrence and Drew Lock are all questionable.

Count that again: two linebackers out, two more questionable, and the safety who does linebacker work also questionable.

And the man they’re defending? Kenneth Walker the Third. 655 rushing yards, six rushing touchdowns, two receiving touchdowns in five games. 128 yards and two scores last Sunday. The first player since Jim Brown in 1963 to open a season with 600-plus rushing yards, six rushing scores and multiple receiving scores.

And the cruelest detail: Brashard Smith, Kansas City’s backup running back, is OUT with a hamstring. Which means more Kenneth Walker, not less. Fewer snaps off. More carries.

Against Seattle’s streak of 35 straight games without allowing a 100-yard rusher — the longest in franchise history — defended by a linebacker room missing at least two men.

And Kansas City is 5-0, ranked fourth in the league in offensive EPA per play and second in EPA per rush, with a defense allowing 16.3 points a game. Patrick Mahomes practiced in full on Thursday, one year off his own ACL tear.
