“Drew is a tremendous quarterback. He’s a starting-level quarterback.”

That’s Mike Macdonald, yesterday, Friday afternoon, talking about his backup.

And then he kept the other guy.

Seahawks Nation, that is the decision. It’s real, it’s dated, it happened less than twenty-four hours after Seattle’s third loss of the season, and it came out of the head coach’s mouth as directly as a head coach is ever going to say anything.

Asked whether the Seattle Seahawks were considering a quarterback change: “No, no.”

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. The defending Super Bowl champions are 3-3 after losing 17-14 in overtime in Denver, Sam Darnold leads the National Football League in interceptions, and the head coach just publicly called his backup a starting-level quarterback and publicly kept him on the bench anyway.

The two numbers that explain why Macdonald made it anyway — numbers that were not on your television screen Thursday night. And then the sentence Mike Macdonald stopped himself in the middle of, which I think is the single most important thing that happened in that press conference.

Let’s start with exactly what was said and when.

Thursday night, Denver. Seattle loses 17-14 in overtime. Darnold throws two interceptions in the fourth quarter and overtime, the second one ending the game. In the press conference afterward, Macdonald is asked the question every coach gets asked — at what point do you start to question the player instead of the calls?

And here is what he said, and I want you to hear the precision in it:

“I wouldn’t use the term — we’re not questioning people. We’re evaluating. We’re finding solutions.”

And then: “I’m responsible for the performance of the team.”

And then: “I believe in our guys. I believe in all of our guys.”

Friday morning and afternoon, October sixteenth. Macdonald goes on the radio and does his media availability, and the question comes directly: are you considering a quarterback change?

“No, no.”

And then he goes further than he had to. Much further.

“We have a great backup quarterback in Drew.”

“Drew is a tremendous quarterback. He’s a starting-level quarterback.”

“But I think these are solutions that we can find with Sam as our quarterback. I believe that.”

And on Darnold himself: “He’s definitely part of the solution rather than the opposite.”

And the sentence that tells you everything about how this organization thinks:

“We just won a world championship with him. We believe in the guy. We see him every day.”

Now, pay attention to the architecture of that, because it’s unusual.

Macdonald did not say Darnold played well. He didn’t say the interceptions were fine. He said this, Thursday night, about the offense: “Run game, quarterback’s getting hit too much.” He said the primary concern was “why the ball’s in jeopardy so much on offense.” He said, when somebody suggested Darnold wasn’t decisive: “I don’t know. It just wasn’t a performance that was good enough to win the game.”

So he’s not defending the play. He’s defending the diagnosis. He is saying: the quarterback is not the variable. And if the quarterback isn’t the variable, then changing the quarterback cannot be the solution.

That’s the decision. Not “Sam is great.” “Sam isn’t the problem.”

Those are two completely different claims, and only one of them can be tested. So let’s test it.

Here’s the honest case against Mike Macdonald, and I’m going to make it properly, because a channel that only makes the case it likes isn’t worth your subscription.

Sam Darnold has thrown seven interceptions. That is the most in the National Football League, tied with Drake Maye and Justin Herbert. He’s completing 60.4 percent of his passes at 6.9 yards per attempt, with seven touchdowns against those seven picks.

Compare that to the man who won the championship last year: 67.7 percent, 8.2 yards per attempt, twenty-five touchdowns to fourteen interceptions — and, the detail I keep coming back to, zero interceptions in the entire playoff run. Zero. All the way through.

And since Darnold came back from the glute injury he suffered on the first drive of the season, Seattle is 1-3.

Now stack the team number on top. Seattle has nine turnovers, tied for the fourth-most in the National Football League, four of them lost fumbles. Four turnovers alone in that 32-29 loss to San Francisco. The panel on the broadcast Thursday night put the number since the start of last season at thirty-eight — I can’t verify that figure myself, so I’ll attribute it to them rather than hand it to you as fact. But the shape of it is right. The defending Super Bowl champion is giving the football away at a rate that only three teams in football can match.

And here’s the part of the argument that actually has teeth. Drew Lock was not bad.

Lock played most of two games while Darnold was hurt — the Patriots game after the opening drive, and Week 2 against Arizona. His line: thirty-five of forty-eight, 422 yards, four touchdowns, zero interceptions.

Four touchdowns. No interceptions. That is not a clipboard holder. That’s a man who has a legitimate, non-crazy argument, and Macdonald himself validated it out loud by calling him a starting-level quarterback.

So the case against the decision is: your starter leads the league in giveaways, your team is 1-3 with him, your backup threw four touchdowns and no picks, and you yourself just said the backup is starting caliber. Explain the decision.

And Macdonald can explain it. With two numbers that nobody put on your screen Thursday night.

Number one: Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks against Denver.

Forty-one percent. On two out of every five snaps, somebody was on him before the play finished.

Number two — and this is the one: under pressure, Darnold went 6-of-19 for 47 yards.

Read that again. Nineteen of his fifty-one attempts came with a defender arriving. Almost forty percent of his throws were made from a collapsing pocket. And he completed six of them.

He was sacked five times in Denver. He was sacked five times the week before against San Francisco. And on the season he has seventy-one dropbacks under pressure — tied for the tenth-most in the entire league.

Now let me teach you the thing that makes this more than a stat.

A quarterback’s interception rate is downstream of his launch point.

When a quarterback throws from a clean pocket, he gets to finish his read, reset his feet, and put the ball where he wants it. When he throws with a defender arriving, three things go wrong at once: his eyes drop, his feet go, and — this is the killer — he has to decide faster than the route has developed. That’s how a throw becomes a guess. And a guess thrown into a zone becomes a gift.

You can bench the man. You cannot bench the pocket.

And Macdonald said this himself, on Thursday night, when a reporter tried to hand him the easy answer and blame the offensive line. His response:

“You can say it’s on the offensive line. It’s on our offense. It’s both.”

And then: “The quarterback’s getting hit way too much. So everybody sees that.”

He refused the easy out twice. He wouldn’t scapegoat the line and he wouldn’t scapegoat the quarterback.

And the most credible voice on that broadcast backed him. Robert Turbin — who carried the football for the Seattle Seahawks — said Denver’s defensive line “overwhelmed the offensive line for the Seattle Seahawks for the majority of the game,” and that Denver showed why it’s “one of the top two or three defenses in the NFL.”

And he was specific about how. “They beat this offensive line with speed.” Nick Bonitto and that Denver edge group winning the outside shoulder, getting around the corner, and — Turbin’s words — Darnold “not being as mobile as some of these other quarterbacks.”

That last bit matters more than it sounds. A quarterback coming off a glute injury, playing at altitude, against the fastest edge rushers he’ll see all month. Asked whether Darnold might still be feeling that injury, Macdonald gave the most honest answer available: “I don’t know the answer to that. I don’t know the answer to that.”

Twice. He said it twice.

Okay. Now the part of the press conference I can’t stop thinking about.

A reporter asked about something he’d noticed during the game: Drew Lock kept having to run onto the field to help communicate a play.

The backup quarterback. Physically sprinting onto the field. To deliver the call.

And here is Macdonald’s complete answer, exactly as he gave it:

“Yeah, we’re having a hard time sometimes communicating. So, we’ll look and see what the recourse of that is if — but, um — we end up — I’ll just leave it at that.”

“I’ll just leave it at that.”

Let me explain why that stopped me.

In the NFL, the play comes into the quarterback’s helmet by radio from the coordinator. One helmet, one voice, one channel. The quarterback hears the call, repeats it in the huddle, and the eleven men execute it. That entire chain has to happen in about twenty seconds.

If the backup quarterback is running onto the field to relay a play, that chain is broken somewhere. Either the radio is failing, or the call is arriving too late, or the call is arriving in a form that takes too long to say.

And a head coach who answered every other question in that room fully — who talked about the offensive line, the interception, the defense, his own responsibility, his quarterback’s injury — stopped himself in the middle of this one.

I’m not going to invent what he was about to say. This is not a report. Nobody has said this. No insider, no source, nothing. This is me, sitting here, looking at a man cut his own sentence in half on camera and noticing which sentence it was.

And it was the one about the voice in the quarterback’s helmet.

Which brings me to the thing I think the whole broadcast missed, and honestly it’s the reason I wanted to make this video.

On that same show, right after the game, Emory Hunt and Robert Turbin spent an entire segment diagnosing Bo Nix. And their diagnosis was not his arm, and it was not his legs.

It was the voice in his helmet.

Denver opened the season with a different man calling plays and then went back to Sean Payton. And Hunt’s explanation of what that does to a quarterback was genuinely excellent. He talked about the encouragement — the “hey, look this way first,” the “no worries on that last play.” He said it’s a different element entirely when a different guy starts the season with you. He said it creates self-doubt, and doubt in the play call, and — his phrase — “the belief is a little bit minimized.”

Then they moved on. And nobody in that studio said the obvious next sentence.

Seattle changed the voice in Sam Darnold’s helmet too.

Klint Kubiak left to become the head coach of the Las Vegas Raiders. Brian Fleury is in his first year as the Seattle Seahawks’ offensive coordinator.

Same diagnosis. They aimed it at Denver’s quarterback and never turned it around.

And once you see that, look at everything else that changed around Sam Darnold since the parade:

Kenneth Walker the Third — the Super Bowl MVP — is in Kansas City. Seattle’s run game has fallen from 123.3 yards a game to 107.2, seventeenth in the league. And Macdonald told you himself what that costs the passer: “run game, quarterback’s getting hit too much” — in the same breath, as one thought, because they are one thought.

Anthony Bradford, the starting right guard, has been out since September with knee and hip injuries. In his place, Seattle has rotated a second-year player and a rookie — Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens — and against Denver, both of them allowed multiple pressures.

So count the changed variables. New offensive coordinator. No Walker. A rookie-and-second-year rotation at right guard. And a communication system the head coach won’t discuss on camera.

Four changes around the one man everybody wants to change.

And I want to be careful here, because this is the line between analysis and excuse-making: none of that throws an interception. Sam Darnold threw those seven passes. He said so himself, and he said it better than anyone else could have. His words after the game: “I just think it’s BS for me to keep turning the ball over.” And: “It’s not fair to our defense, not fair to our special teams group, not fair to the entire team.”

That’s accountability with no lawyer in the room. Respect it.

But understanding why something is happening is not the same as excusing it. And the reason Macdonald’s decision is defensible is simple: benching the quarterback doesn’t touch a single one of those four variables. Drew Lock would inherit the same coordinator, the same missing running back, the same right guard rotation, and the same radio.

So here’s what Seattle is actually doing with these ten days — and these are real decisions, with real dates, unlike the quarterback change everyone wants to talk about.

One: Anthony Bradford. He’s eligible to come off injured reserve, and on Friday Macdonald said he “has a really good chance of being back this week.” And then this, which is the most underrated quote of the week: “It’ll be part of our decision-making as we go forward.” That is the actual fix for a 41 percent pressure rate. Not a quarterback. A right guard.

Two: the right guard rotation itself. Haynes outsnapped Stephens in Denver and both gave up pressures. Macdonald said Friday he hadn’t even sat down with his offensive staff about it yet — because, in his words about the short week, “we were moving at warp speed.”

Three: Zach Charbonnet, October twenty-second. Five days. He’s practicing, he’s not activated, and on Friday Macdonald had no update. Seattle puts him on the fifty-three-man roster by that date or he reverts for the rest of the season. Twelve rushing touchdowns last year with zero starts, and he costs no draft pick and no cap space. If the run game is strangling the quarterback, that is the cheapest oxygen available.

Four: Elijah Arroyo. He left the Denver game and didn’t come back. Macdonald: “He has a shoulder. We’ll see what the extent is, but he obviously wasn’t able to finish.” And Friday: “He’s getting some imaging and testing as we speak, so no update yet.” Watch for that.

Five: Jadarian Price, who has to miss at least one more game on injured reserve, but Macdonald says he’s “doing great” and pointed at a return sooner rather than later after the next one.

Five real decisions. Every single one of them about the environment around the quarterback. Which is exactly what the head coach said the problem was.

Now let me give you the number that should genuinely frighten this team, because it reframes the entire loss.

Denver gained 168 total yards. Seattle gained 353.

Denver’s entire offense produced less than half of Seattle’s. Bo Nix threw for 107 yards, and his longest pass of the night traveled 7.6 yards in the air. One reporter in the press conference pointed out that after Denver’s two touchdown drives, the Broncos managed two first downs the rest of the game.

And here is the historical number, from Andrew Mason: the Denver Broncos had been 0-12 in franchise history in games where they gained fewer than 175 yards and the opponent gained at least 350.

Oh and twelve. Now one and twelve.

And league-wide, since 2010, teams in that exact statistical position had gone 2 and 109.

Two wins. One hundred nine losses. Seattle lost a football game that had been lost one hundred nine times out of one hundred eleven.

That is not a bad defense. Macdonald on his guys: “Really proud of our guys. Just the resolve. They executed at a really high level for what we were asking them to do today against a really good offense.” And he credited his staff for finding solutions after those first two drives.

That is a defense that did its job and got nothing for it. Which is why Darnold’s “it’s not fair to our defense” landed the way it did.

And then the number that defines this entire season: Seattle’s three losses have come by two points, three points and three points. Eight points, total. The 49ers are 5-0 and lead the division. Macdonald is 27-13 in three seasons as a head coach. Seattle lost three games in the entire regular season last year — and they’ve already lost three, by a combined eight points, and they are not a bad team.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba finished Thursday with twelve catches for 134 yards — and only two catches for eleven yards in the first half, while Denver bracketed him. He got his. Rashid Shaheed had 66 yards, a 42-yard catch, and the two-point conversion. George Holani ran in the touchdown. Jason Myers tied the game. Eleven unanswered points in the fourth quarter down 14-3 on the road at altitude on a short week. Macdonald wasn’t lying about the resolve.

He also wasn’t lying about the other part: “There’s no moral victories in the league. It’s a bottom-line business, a production business.”

Let me close with where I actually stand, and I’ll put a condition on it so you can hold me to it.

I think Macdonald got this right. Not because Darnold has played well — he hasn’t — but because the diagnosis is correct and the diagnosis is what you act on. The pressure rate, the 6-of-19 under duress, the five sacks twice in a row, the new coordinator, the missing MVP, the rookie guard, the radio nobody will talk about. Those are the variables. A new quarterback changes none of them.

And here’s the condition. Anthony Bradford is probably back this week. Charbonnet’s deadline is five days away. If Seattle gets its right guard back, activates its running back, drops that pressure rate — and the interceptions keep coming anyway — then Macdonald was wrong, the variable was the quarterback all along, and we’ll know it by the middle of November.

If that happens, I’ll sit right here and say it. That’s the deal on this channel.

I’m probably thinking a lot of the same things you guys are thinking. It’s just a matter of backing it up and making sure we’re going down the right path.”

That’s a head coach saying: I see what you see. I’m just not going to act on it until I can prove it.
