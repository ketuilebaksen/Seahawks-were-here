Start with what it actually takes to say that out loud.

Jadarian Price has two fumbles on thirty-two NFL touches. One of them handed Washington a touchdown on the second drive of a game Seattle lost by two points. And this isn't a mystery pattern either — at Notre Dame he fumbled five times on 295 career touches and lost four of them. Roughly one every fifty-nine touches. It came with him from college.

Meanwhile Kenneth Walker — the man Seattle let walk to Kansas City — fumbled three times in his entire Seahawks career, across more than eight hundred carries.

So Macdonald had every reason to protect himself. He could have said "we'll evaluate the reps." He could have said "everybody has to earn their carries." Those are the sentences head coaches use when they want to keep their options open and let the fan base do the executing for them.

Instead he committed, in public, with a microphone in his face, to a twenty-two-year-old who just cost him a football game. He said the fumble wasn't good business, and then he said the kid is going to be fine — twice — and that they're going to keep handing him the ball.

Here's why that matters, and it has nothing to do with being nice.

A running back who is scared of fumbling runs differently. He runs with two hands on the football and his eyes down and his feet slow, and he becomes exactly the cautious, indecisive back who gains two yards instead of twelve. If you want to destroy the explosiveness that made you spend a first-round pick on him, the fastest way is to make him play scared. Macdonald just removed that fear in the most public way available to him.

Against zero. Zero coverage. For anybody who doesn't live in this stuff — zero means no deep safety. Everybody's locked on a man, everybody else is coming. It is the most aggressive call in football. You are betting the game that you get there before the quarterback beats you, because if you're wrong there is nobody behind it.

So on third and nine, with the season's winning streak on the line, Mike Macdonald didn't flinch. He sent everybody. And Washington's quarterback read it at the line, checked into the right play, and a run went outside where nobody was left to contain it.

Every complaint I've read this week says this coaching staff was too passive. Should have kicked the field goal. Should have played it safer. And the actual, documented truth is that Macdonald went for the throat on the most important snap of the game and got beaten by an audible.
