Alright. Now the actual football lesson, because the document circles something real and never lands it: why kickers almost never get traded.

There are three reasons, and the third one is the one nobody explains.

Reason one: it’s the most replaceable position on a roster in-season. There are competent kickers sitting at home every single week of the NFL season. If your kicker gets hurt on Sunday, you can have a new one on the practice field by Tuesday. You never need to trade draft capital for a skill that’s available for free.

Reason two: the money doesn’t work in the direction people think. Kicker contracts are small in absolute terms but enormous relative to the value — seven point three million dollars buys you a starting-caliber guard or a rotational edge rusher. You don’t pay premium prices for the one job that has a free replacement market.

And reason three, which is the real one: a kicker is not one man. He’s a three-man timing operation.

Think about what actually happens on a field goal. The long snapper fires the ball back roughly eight yards, laces oriented, to a holder who catches it, spins the laces away, and places the tip at a precise angle — all in about one and a quarter seconds. And the kicker has started his approach before the ball arrives. He’s kicking at a spot he trusts will have a football in it.

That’s not three jobs. That’s one job performed by three bodies, rehearsed thousands of times until the timing is invisible.

Now drop a brand-new kicker into that in week seven. Different approach, different plant depth, different preferred lean on the laces, different rhythm. You didn’t upgrade a leg. You broke a rehearsed trio and asked it to re-learn itself in four practices before a Sunday night game.

And Mike Macdonald said something on Monday that’s exactly this idea, about a different part of the team: “You get, on average, sixty-something opportunities to snap the ball a game... It’s not that many snaps, okay? And so those are precious snaps.” And then: “How you deploy people, when you call it, how you get there, what you practice.”

Operation over acquisition. That’s the whole philosophy of this coaching staff, and it’s why Seattle’s answer to a close-game problem is never going to be a kicker.

And let me make the opportunity cost concrete, because “fifty-seven percent of your cap space” is abstract until you say what you gave up.

Seven point three million dollars, in the middle of an NFL season, is not nothing. Here is what that money can actually buy at the November tenth trade deadline:

A starting-caliber interior offensive lineman. The exact thing Seattle has been missing for five games while Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens both gave up multiple pressures in Denver.

A rotational edge rusher on an expiring deal, to take snaps off DeMarcus Lawrence and Derick Hall in a December that’s going to be cold and physical.

A veteran tight end on the active roster, instead of hoping a practice-squad elevation covers for Arroyo’s four-game absence.

Or a running back whose contract actually works — like Tony Pollard, who’s in the final year of a three-year, twenty-one point seven five million dollar deal with a six point seven four million dollar base, where Tennessee would save about seven point two five million and eat only about two million in dead money.

And here’s the mechanic that makes deadline shopping cheaper than people realize: in-season, you only take on the remaining prorated base salary. Every week that passes, the price goes down. A player with a six-million-dollar base salary costs roughly three million at the halfway point of the year, and less the week after that.

So the real sentence is this: you can have a guard, an edge rusher and a tight end for what one kicker’s cap number costs — or you can have the kicker, and keep the 29th-ranked rush offense.

That’s not a close call. That’s not even a decision a front office would spend a meeting on.
