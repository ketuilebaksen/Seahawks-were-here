Let’s count properly.

In four days, the Seattle Seahawks signed three football players. Two running backs and a safety. And every one of those stories is good.

But here’s the one nobody has told you about, Seahawks Nation.

On Tuesday, Seattle waived a safety. On Wednesday, they signed him back.

Twenty-four hours. Out the door and back through it. And they did it to make room for a running back who finished third in the entire country in yards per carry.

That’s not a clerical shuffle. That was a calculated gamble with thirty-one other front offices holding the other end of it, and John Schneider won it.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Today: the two signings and what they actually are, the roster trick nobody noticed, and then a fantasy going around about Seattle signing a linebacker from Kansas City — which I’m going to end with one number. Plus the final sheet before tomorrow. Let’s go.

Signing number one: Robert Henry Jr.

On October sixth, Seattle signed him off the Washington Commanders’ practice squad — straight onto the fifty-three man active roster. Not Seattle’s practice squad. The real one.

And the résumé is a lot more than “practice squad guy.”

At Lumberton High School in Mississippi, he was Class 1A Mr. Football twice and a four-time all-state pick, with 1,961 yards and twenty-nine touchdowns as a senior. At Jones College he was the 2022 NJCAA Offensive Player of the Year and the winner of the first Walter Jones Trophy ever awarded to the best junior college running back in America.

Then at UTSA in 2025: first-team All-American Athletic Conference, 1,045 yards, nine touchdowns, and 6.9 yards per carry — third in all of FBS. Third in the country.

And the game that made Seattle’s scouts sit up: August 30th, 2025, against Texas A&M. 177 yards and two touchdowns at over eleven yards a carry, including a 75-yard touchdown on the very first play of the second half. Texas A&M won 42-24 and spent the following week with their head coach calling his own run defense “very, very correctable.”

Macdonald’s scouting report Friday: “He’s got good vision. He’s got good burst, got good acceleration. A lot of a lot of good stuff.” And on whether that SEC tape matters, he landed on the quote of the week: “I’m not going to bet against John evaluating runners.”

Signing number two: Dontae McMillan.

Same day — October sixth — to the practice squad. And this one’s personal, because he’s from here.

Dontae McMillan went to Chief Sealth International High School in West Seattle. As a senior he ran for 2,019 yards and forty-four touchdowns. He was the unanimous Metro League Offensive Player of the Year, first-team All-Metro — and first-team All-Defense, because he played both ways.

Then Weber State and Eastern Michigan: fifty career games, 493 carries, 2,785 rushing yards, twenty touchdowns, plus forty-nine catches for 449 yards and three more scores. In 2025 he started all twelve games, had five separate hundred-yard games, and ran it 177 times for 1,014 yards at 5.7 a carry. All-MAC second team and his school’s Most Valuable Player.

Undrafted in April, signed with Baltimore on May first, broke a 63-yard run against the Commanders in the preseason, released August thirtieth.

And the detail that got me: he played with Rashid Shaheed at Weber State. A kid from Chief Sealth and Seattle’s starting receiver, teammates in the Big Sky, now in the same building.

Now let me be straight with you about the word “star,” because I won’t sell you something I can’t back up.

Between those two men, there are zero NFL carries. None. Everything I just read you happened in a Mississippi high school, a Seattle high school, junior college, the AAC and the MAC.

But have both of these men been stars at every level they have ever played? Absolutely. Two Mr. Football awards. A national juco player of the year. A unanimous league MVP. Two thousand-yard college seasons. And one of them was third in the United States of America in yards per carry.

That’s the honest version, and it’s still a good story.

Now the part nobody covered, and it’s the sharpest thing this front office did all week.

AJ Finley’s twenty-four hours.

Tuesday, October sixth: to create the fifty-three man roster spot for Robert Henry Jr., Seattle waived safety AJ Finley.

Wednesday, October seventh: Finley cleared waivers, and Seattle signed him right back — to the practice squad.

Now, I know how that sounds. Paperwork. Who cares.

Here’s why you should care. When you waive a player, every other team in the National Football League gets a shot at him. All thirty-one of them. And the man sitting out there on waivers was a twenty-five-year-old safety who, three weeks earlier, had made the first start of his career for the defending Super Bowl champions.

If one single team had put in a claim, he was gone. Seattle gets nothing. No pick, no compensation, no goodbye.

Nobody claimed him.

So Seattle read the market, took the risk, got their running back onto the active roster, and kept the safety anyway. They bought a roster spot for free. That’s not luck — that’s a front office that knows exactly how other teams value its own players.

And Finley’s story deserves a second, because what a month. He first came to Seattle during the 2024 season. He missed the entire 2025 season with a knee injury. He re-signed in the 2026 offseason, played three games, and got thrown into his first career start in Week 3 at Washington on two days’ notice when Julian Love’s calf tightened up — and Macdonald said afterward that the back end was organized.

First start of his life three weeks ago. Waived on a Tuesday. Back on a Wednesday. Still here.

That’s the National Football League, and he’s still standing in it.

Oh, and there was a fourth move: wide receiver Irv Charles was designated to return from injured reserve on Wednesday — a genuine standout special-teamer getting his clock started.

Alright. Now the fantasy, and I need to handle this properly, because there’s a long, very confident-sounding piece going around arguing that Seattle should bring in a veteran inside linebacker to wear the green dot and run the defense. And it names Nick Bolton.

So, the rule of this channel: This is not a report. Nobody has said this. No insider, no source, nothing. And to its credit, the piece admits it — it calls itself “a conditional exploration, not a report of negotiations.” At least it’s honest about that.

And before I take it apart, let me actually explain the thing it keeps saying, because “the green dot” gets thrown around constantly and almost nobody tells you what it is.

Look at the back of a defensive helmet. One player — exactly one — has a small green sticker on the back. That sticker means that helmet has a radio receiver in it, and the defensive coordinator’s voice goes directly into that man’s ear between plays. He hears the call, and then he turns around and gets ten other men lined up correctly before the ball is snapped, usually in under fifteen seconds, often while the offense is sprinting to the line trying to stop him from finishing.

The rules allow one green dot on the field at a time. So if your green-dot player comes off — hurt, or in a package where he isn’t needed — somebody else has to take the radio, and that man has to be able to do the whole job: hear it, process it, communicate it, and then go play football.

That’s why the idea of buying a veteran signal-caller sounds smart. It’s a real skill and it’s genuinely hard.

It also ignores the small matter of Seattle already having one of the best in the league. Which brings me to the thing the piece didn’t do, so let me do it for you.

Nick Bolton is under contract with the Kansas City Chiefs through the 2027 season.

Three years. Forty-five million dollars. Thirty million guaranteed, signed in 2025. His 2026 base salary is fourteen million, seven hundred fifty thousand dollars, and his 2026 cap number is nineteen and a quarter million. He does not reach free agency until 2028.

He is not available. There is no door to knock on. And for scale: Seattle has about twelve point eight million dollars in total cap space — which is less than this one man’s cap number. You could not fit Nick Bolton on this roster if Kansas City gift-wrapped him.

But forget the money for a second, because here’s the number that actually ends the conversation, and it’s my favorite thing I found this week.

Sports Illustrated ranked the ten best linebackers in football for 2026.

Ernest Jones the Fourth is number eight. Nick Bolton is number nine.

Eight and nine.

Seattle already has the better player.

So read the proposal back to yourself with the facts in it: spend nineteen million dollars you do not have, on a man under contract with another team until 2028, to upgrade a position where you currently employ somebody ranked above him.

That’s not a bold idea. That’s a sentence that falls apart the moment anybody checks it.

And Ernest Jones’ file is not some technicality. He won Super Bowl LVI as a rookie with the Rams and became a captain there. He got traded to Tennessee, then traded to Seattle, and then won Super Bowl LX here. Last season: 126 total tackles, four tackles for loss, and five interceptions — the most of any linebacker in the National Football League and second-most in the entire league at any position.

Five interceptions. From a linebacker.

And this season he’s sitting on 41 total tackles, the fourth-most in football through four games.

Now let me be fair to Bolton, because he’s genuinely excellent and I’m not going to trash a good player to make a point. Last season he had 154 total tackles and 86 run stops. He’s won Super Bowl LVII and LVIII. He led the entire NFL in postseason tackles during that second title run. And his own defensive coordinator, Steve Spagnuolo, gave him the best compliment a coach can give a player: “Do whatever Nick says.”

Nick Bolton is a terrific football player.

He’s also theirs.

And here’s the part that should make you smile: you’re going to see him anyway. Seattle plays Kansas City in a few weeks — with Kenneth Walker the Third in a Chiefs uniform, by the way. You’ll get your Nick Bolton game. He’ll just be wearing red and trying to tackle Emanuel Wilson.

Now let me show you why Seattle’s linebacker room does not need saving, using one football game.

Against the Chargers, here’s what that group did.

Ernest Jones the Fourth: twelve tackles, six solo, a sack, a tackle for loss, a pass defensed — and an interception he returned twenty yards. That’s a complete afternoon from a middle linebacker, one week after a rough day in Washington.

Drake Thomas intercepted a pass on the first play of the game. The first play. He finished with five tackles, four solo, a tackle for loss and a pass breakup.

Uchenna Nwosu had a sack and a forced fumble. Derick Hall recovered Nwosu’s fumble to help close the game out.

Four linebackers. Two interceptions, two sacks, a forced fumble and the recovery — in one afternoon. Local coverage called this group “one of the more special linebacker corps in the NFL” and said that if anybody deserved credit for saving that day, it was the linebackers.

And the thing I appreciate most about Jones is off the field: he spends his press conferences handing credit to the guys around him. That’s the green-dot quality the piece going around says Seattle needs to go buy. Seattle already pays for it.

Now the honest caveat, because you always get one here. Seattle’s linebacker depth is genuinely thinner tomorrow: Chazz Surratt is out with a calf, and Tyrice Knight spent the week working back from an ankle. That’s real.

But thin depth is a practice squad problem, not a nineteen-million-dollar problem.

And Seattle has been solving it that way all season, quietly, while nobody wrote a word about it. Over the last few weeks this front office has signed linebacker Jimmy Ciarlo to the practice squad. It signed defensive tackle Gary Smith III to the practice squad. It signed guard Evan Beerntsen to the practice squad. It signed safety Rodney Thomas the Second off the practice squad onto the fifty-three, then elevated him again. It elevated Maxen Hook and Velus Jones Jr. before the Washington game, then signed Velus Jones to the active roster before the Chargers game. It carries Trevon Diggs — a former Pro Bowl cornerback — on the practice squad and elevates him on game days.

That’s the actual machine. Not one nine-figure splash. Fifteen small, boring, correct decisions that mean the next injury doesn’t cost you a season.

You fix linebacker depth with a Jimmy Ciarlo signing. You don’t fix it by raiding the Kansas City Chiefs.

Okay. Tomorrow. Here’s the final sheet, and the news is good.

Leonard Williams has no injury designation. None. He’s off the report entirely after a Wednesday limited and a Thursday DNP. Macdonald: “Leonard is gonna play” and “we just wanted to do right by him.” That Thursday off was on purpose — a twelfth-year, three-time Pro Bowler who’s missed one game in three seasons doesn’t need Thursday. He needs Sunday.

Ty Okada will make his season debut with no designation, after four weeks out with the hamstring.

Seattle’s out: Julian Neal with a hamstring, Chazz Surratt with a calf. Questionable: George Holani with ribs, Irv Charles with a knee.

San Francisco’s out: Nick Bosa with the knee and calf, Kyle Juszczyk with a knee, Marques Sigle with an ankle, James Thompson with an ankle. Questionable: Upton Stout and Mykel Williams, who’s being activated off the physically-unable-to-perform list. Mike Evans is not on the report — he plays.

And the stat hanging over everything: San Francisco has not allowed a sack in four games. They’re the first team to open a season that way in twenty-one years — the last was the Indianapolis Colts in 2005 — and only the fifth team to do it since 1963.

Seattle is favored by three over the only undefeated team in the conference. Total of forty-seven and a half.

And one more thing to carry into tomorrow, because it matters: the last time these two teams met, Seattle won 41-6 in the divisional round. Forty-one to six. In this building.

Looking ahead: Jadarian Price is eligible to return November second against the Bears — he misses the 49ers, Denver on October fifteenth, and Kansas City. And Zach Charbonnet has to be activated by October twenty-second or he’s on PUP for the season.

Watch list, three things.

One: does Robert Henry Jr. touch the football? Five days in the building, third in the nation in yards per carry, zero NFL snaps.

Two: first down. Not third. If San Francisco lives in second-and-five, that sack streak survives and Seattle loses. Get them to second-and-nine and the whole thing changes.

Three: the penalty count. Fourteen for a franchise-record hundred and fifty yards against the Chargers, seven personal fouls. Hand a 4-0 team a hundred and fifty free yards and they’ll bury you with them.

Let me close.

John Schneider signed two running backs in one day — one of them third in America in yards per carry, one of them a kid from Chief Sealth High School — waived a safety to do it, correctly bet that nobody in the league would claim that safety, and signed him back twenty-four hours later.

Three players added. One roster spot spent. Nothing given up.

And while that was happening, the loudest idea on the internet was that Seattle should go spend nineteen million dollars it doesn’t have on a linebacker from Kansas City who is ranked one spot below the linebacker Seattle already has.

That’s the gap between how this front office actually works and how people imagine it works. Schneider doesn’t buy the famous name. He finds the guy nobody’s looking at, and then his head coach stands at a podium and says “I’m not going to bet against John evaluating runners.”
