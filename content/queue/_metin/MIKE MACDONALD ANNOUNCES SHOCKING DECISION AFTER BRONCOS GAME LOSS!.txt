Seven hundred and seventeen.

That’s how many receiving yards Jaxon Smith-Njigba has right now, six games into the season. Forty-nine catches. Seven touchdowns. A hundred and nineteen and a half yards every single week.

And the script I was handed for this video says — and I’m quoting it — that Smith-Njigba is “positioned to go on a blistering pace to blow past 700 receiving yards on the season.”

On the season.

Seahawks Nation, he blew past it on Thursday night. In week six. And the pace he’s actually on is two thousand and thirty-two yards, which matters because the all-time single-season record in the history of the National Football League is Calvin Johnson’s one thousand nine hundred sixty-four.

Nobody has ever gained two thousand receiving yards in an NFL season. Nobody. And it might be happening in Seattle right now, while the entire football world argues about the quarterback throwing him the ball.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. And we need a very specific kind of conversation today, because what landed in my inbox is unusual and I’m going to be straight with you about it.

Two things before we start.

First, the title. Sam Darnold is not gone. He is the starting quarterback of the Seattle Seahawks. Yesterday — Friday, October sixteenth — Mike Macdonald was asked directly whether the team was considering a quarterback change and he answered: “No, no.” Then he went further than he had to. He said Darnold is “definitely part of the solution rather than the opposite.” He said: “We just won a world championship with him. We believe in the guy. We see him every day.”

So “SAM DARNOLD GONE” isn’t an exaggeration of the news. It’s the opposite of the news. And I already made the video about that decision — it’s the last one up. I’m not serving you the same meal twice with different seasoning.

Second, and this one’s important: the document I was given is a game preview. It’s a preview of a game that has already been played. It ends by predicting — and again, I’m quoting — “a hard-fought 10-point road victory that pushes this franchise to four and two.”

Seattle lost 17-14 in overtime. They are 3-3.

So we’re not doing postgame analysis from a pregame script. We’re doing something I think is far more valuable: we’re going to grade the preview. What it got right, what it invented, and the enormous thing it missed — which is the 717 yards I just read you.

Because here’s what makes this worth your time. That preview got a stunning amount correct and still called the game completely wrong. Understanding how that happens will make you better at watching football than any hot take ever will.

Let’s go.

Part one: what the preview got right. And it starts strong.

The injury section is genuinely good work. I checked every name against the official Week 6 report, and it’s accurate.

Patrick Surtain the Second — ankle — ruled OUT. Denver’s All-Pro cornerback, the one defender capable of erasing an opponent’s best receiver, did not play.

Talanoa Hufanga — ankle — ruled OUT. Denver’s hardest-hitting safety, gone.

Riley Moss — ribs — questionable. And he was active for the game.

On Seattle’s side: Zach Charbonnet, knee, OUT. Drake Thomas, ankle, OUT. Both correct.

That’s five injury calls and five hits. Whoever wrote that section actually read the report, and I’ll give credit where it’s earned.

It even got the reasoning right on Charbonnet — that a Thursday game robbed him of roughly forty-eight hours of recovery a Sunday game would have given him, and that he’d looked smooth in practice. Both true.

Now here’s the one that surprised me, because I assumed it was invented and it isn’t.

The preview claims Seattle has “managed to hang 29 or more points on the board in four consecutive outings.”

I checked. Thirty-one against Arizona. Thirty-one at Washington. Thirty against the Chargers. Twenty-nine against San Francisco. Four straight games of twenty-nine or more points. Completely accurate.

And the exception it names is accurate too: the only game Seattle failed to clear that bar was New England, where — in the preview’s words — “Darnold went down after just five snaps, forcing Drew Lock into emergency duty to grind out an improbable victory.” Seattle won 13-10. Correct again.

And then the diagnosis, which is the best paragraph in the document:

“When pressure collapses his space and defenders invade his vision, Darnold’s decision-making unravels. He rushes his mechanics, forces throws into tight windows, and coughs up agonizing turnovers.”

That is exactly right, and we now have the numbers to prove it, which the preview didn’t have. Against Denver, Sam Darnold was pressured on 41.4 percent of his dropbacks. And under pressure he went 6-of-19 for 47 yards. Nineteen of his fifty-one throws came with somebody arriving. He completed six.

So: the injuries, the trend, the quarterback mechanism. The preview had the inputs. It had the right inputs.

And it got the game completely, catastrophically wrong.

Part two: the number that should humble every single one of us.

The preview’s conclusion was a ten-point Seattle win and a 4-2 record. The actual result: Broncos 17, Seahawks 14, in overtime. Seattle 3-3.

And look what happened to the one trend it correctly identified. That four-game streak of twenty-nine-plus points? Seattle scored fourteen. The streak died that night — and it was Seattle’s lowest-scoring game of the entire season with a healthy quarterback on the field.

Denver played without its All-Pro cornerback and without its starting safety, and Seattle scored fourteen points.

And then the detail that should genuinely frighten you. Denver gained 168 total yards. Seattle gained 353. Denver’s entire offense produced less than half of Seattle’s and Denver won. Bo Nix threw for 107 yards and his longest pass of the night traveled 7.6 yards in the air.

Andrew Mason dug out the historical number, and it’s brutal: the Denver Broncos had been 0-and-12 in franchise history in games where they gained fewer than 175 yards while the opponent gained at least 350. Now they’re 1-and-12. And league-wide since 2010, teams in that exact position had gone 2 and 109.

Two wins. One hundred and nine losses. Seattle lost a game that had been lost a hundred and nine times out of a hundred and eleven.

Here is the lesson, and it’s the most useful thing I can give you today: you can be right about every input and still be wrong about the output.

The preview said “the path to dominance is laid out in plain sight.” And it genuinely was — on paper. Denver’s secondary was missing its two best players. Seattle had scored twenty-nine-plus four weeks running. The matchup was real.

But a preview adds up advantages, and a football game is decided by variance across a very small number of possessions. You can win the matchup on every line of the spreadsheet and lose the game on one third-and-seven.

And that is this entire Seattle season in one sentence. Three losses, by two, three and three points. Eight points total, across three football games. Thirty-three to thirty-one at Washington. Thirty-two to twenty-nine against San Francisco. Seventeen to fourteen in Denver.

The defending champions have not been outplayed this year. They’ve been out-possessed at the margins. Those are different problems, and only one of them is fixable by panicking.

Part three: the war that doesn’t exist.

Now we get to the part of the preview that’s invented, and I want to spend real time here because it’s a specific kind of fake analysis and once you can spot it you’ll see it everywhere.

The preview claims there’s an “ideological clash taking place on the offensive coaching staff.” The theory goes like this: Klint Kubiak built his offense on maximum protection, routinely keeping seven or even eight blockers in before unleashing deep shots, giving the quarterback a safe pocket to breathe and scan. Brian Fleury, meanwhile, is said to be “far more aligned with the aggressive Kyle Shanahan school,” placing “unwavering faith in the concept that five linemen should hold their ground” and accepting the occasional sack as “the cost of doing business.”

It’s a great-sounding theory. It explains the sacks. It has two names, a philosophy and a villain.

And Brian Fleury said the opposite, on the record, in February, when he got the job.

His words: “It looks very similar to the one that just won the Super Bowl.”

And: “I do think one of the advantages of taking this job is there is gonna be a lot of continuity.”

And then the sentence that collapses the whole theory: “So, that’s going to be the goal, is to keep as much as we can, the same.”

Keep as much as we can the same. That is the brand-new coordinator, publicly, on day one, saying continuity is the plan.

And here’s the detail that finishes it off. Fleury and Kubiak come from the same coaching tree. They are both products of the Kyle Shanahan school. Fleury was the San Francisco 49ers’ tight ends coach before Seattle hired him.

So the preview invented a philosophical war between two men who went to the same school, one of whom publicly said his entire goal was to keep things the same.

Learn this pattern: inventing a philosophical disagreement to explain a statistical one. The sacks are absolutely real — five against Denver, five against San Francisco the week before. The pressure rate is real. But “two coaches with clashing ideologies” is a story someone wrote to make a stat feel like a drama. The real explanation is sitting right there: Anthony Bradford, the starting right guard, has been out since September, and Seattle has rotated a second-year player and a rookie — Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens — who both gave up multiple pressures against Denver.

That’s not an ideology. That’s a missing guard.

And here’s what Fleury actually changed, because this part is real and reported, and it’s more interesting than the fiction.

More motion at the snap. Brian Nemhauser of Hawk Blogger, watching Seattle’s minicamp, put it this way: “I believe motion is going to be a much bigger part of this offense.” And he said it already felt “different already than what I was seeing at this stage a year ago.”

Let me make sure that lands. A shift is a player who moves before the snap and then sets. Motion is a player still moving at the moment the ball is snapped. And motion does two jobs at once: it forces the defense to declare its coverage late, and — this is the part quarterbacks love — it tells the quarterback man or zone before the ball is even snapped, because if a defender follows the moving man, it’s man coverage. Motion is free information.

And the second real change: Fleury is a tight end developer by trade — that’s literally the job he came from. The plan built around that was multiple tight end sets: A.J. Barner and Elijah Arroyo together, run-game personnel with pass-game ability.

Which brings me to the cruelest sentence of this entire week.

The one structural change Fleury genuinely made was built on having two tight ends. And on Thursday night, he lost one of them.

Macdonald on Arroyo: “He has a shoulder. We’ll see what the extent is, but he obviously wasn’t able to finish.” And on Friday: “He’s getting some imaging and testing as we speak, so no update yet.”

That’s the actual story. Not a coaching civil war. An injury to the center of the plan that was actually installed.

Part four: a quick one, but it tells you everything about the source.

The preview lists the edge rushers who need to show up against Denver: DeMarcus Lawrence, Derick Hall, and Boye Mafe.

Boye Mafe plays for the Cincinnati Bengals. He left in free agency in March on a three-year deal reported around sixty million dollars. He hasn’t been a Seahawk since the parade.

That’s the tell I keep handing you: when a script names a player who isn’t on the roster, nobody involved checked a roster. It also calls Bo Nix a “rookie quarterback.” Nix is in his third NFL season. These aren’t opinions. They’re facts that were never looked up.

Part five. And this is why I actually made this video.

Because for all the things that preview invented, the thing it missed is the best story in Seattle right now, and almost nobody is telling it.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba has 717 receiving yards through six games.

Forty-nine catches. Seven touchdowns. 119.5 yards per game.

On Thursday night in Denver, he was bracketed in the first half and held to two catches for eleven yards. Denver committed to taking him away and for thirty minutes it worked. He finished the game with twelve catches for 134 yards.

That pace — 119.5 a game — projects to roughly two thousand and thirty-two receiving yards across a seventeen-game season.

The single-season record is Calvin Johnson’s 1,964 yards, set in 2012. It has stood for fourteen years. Nobody in the history of professional football has reached two thousand.

And here’s the number that’s arguably more remarkable, because it’s about a shape, not a total. Smith-Njigba is accounting for roughly 49 percent of all of Seattle’s receiving yards — which, per ESPN Insights, is the highest share of any player in the National Football League. The record for that is Ken Burrough’s 1975 season at about 50.6 percent.

Forty-nine percent. He is, functionally, half of a Super Bowl champion’s passing offense.

Now hold both of these facts in your head at the same time, because this is the whole argument.

Seattle’s quarterback leads the National Football League in interceptions with seven.

Seattle’s receiver is on pace to break the all-time single-season receiving record.

Same quarterback. Same throws. Same football.

You cannot post the second number without the first one also being true. Nobody gains 717 yards in six games by himself — somebody has to put the ball there forty-nine times. The forty-two yard throw to Rashid Shaheed came from the same arm as the overtime interception. The twelve-catch, 134-yard night happened in the same game as two picks.

And that is the strongest version of Mike Macdonald’s argument — stronger than anything he actually said at the podium. He said “these are solutions that we can find with Sam as our quarterback.” What he could have said: the man throwing to the receiver chasing Calvin Johnson’s record is not what stands between this team and January.

But I’ll give you the other side too, because a forty-nine percent share is a strength and a vulnerability wearing the same jersey.

If one man is half your passing offense, every defensive coordinator in football knows exactly who to bracket. Denver did it. Seattle had ninety-one total yards at halftime and trailed 14-3. And that’s exactly why the Arroyo injury matters more than it looks on a report — two tight ends was supposed to be part of the answer to the bracket. Barner and Arroyo force a defense to respect the middle of the field, which is what loosens coverage on your best receiver.

Take one away and you’re asking a 49-percent target to beat a double team while the quarterback holds the ball an extra beat behind a rotating right guard.

That’s not a philosophy problem. That’s a personnel problem with five names on it.

So what’s actually next? Real dates, real decisions.

October twenty-second. Five days. Zach Charbonnet’s deadline — activated to the fifty-three-man roster, or he reverts for the rest of the season. As of Friday, Macdonald had no update. And Seattle’s run game has fallen from 123.3 yards a game last season to 107.2, seventeenth in the league. The man scored twelve rushing touchdowns last year with zero starts, and he costs no pick and no cap space.

Anthony Bradford. Macdonald, Friday: he “has a really good chance of being back this week,” and “it’ll be part of our decision-making as we go forward.” That’s the fix for the forty-one percent pressure rate. Not a quarterback. A right guard.

Elijah Arroyo’s imaging results. Watch for those first.

Jadarian Price, at least one more game on injured reserve, but Macdonald says he’s “doing great.”

And one roster note the preview missed entirely: Chazz Surratt was also ruled out with a calf injury — which is why Seattle elevated Jimmy Ciarlo from the practice squad for linebacker depth, alongside receiver Irv Charles for special teams. Two elevations, which as you know by now is the weekly maximum.

Kansas City. October twenty-fifth. Nine days.

Let me close.

That preview got five injuries right, a four-game scoring trend right, the quarterback’s pressure problem right — and then predicted a ten-point win in a game Seattle lost by three in overtime. It invented a coaching war between two men from the same coaching tree. It put a Cincinnati Bengal on Seattle’s defensive line. And it walked straight past a receiver on pace to do something no human being has ever done in this sport.

That’s the lesson. Being right about the pieces isn’t the same as being right about the game. And being loud about the obvious story isn’t the same as finding the real one.

Seattle is 3-3. The defending champions have lost three games by a combined eight points. Their quarterback leads the league in interceptions and their receiver is chasing Calvin Johnson. Both sentences are true and neither one cancels the other.

Mike Macdonald said it better than I can: “There’s no moral victories in the league. It’s a bottom-line business, a production business.” And then: “Got the right people in the building. We just got to go to work.”

Go through, not around.
