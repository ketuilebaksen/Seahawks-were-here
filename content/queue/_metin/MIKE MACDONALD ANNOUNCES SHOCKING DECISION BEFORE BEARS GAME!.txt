Seahawks Nation, there is a decision this week, and it’s a real one. Mike Macdonald has to pick a man to protect Sam Darnold’s blind side on Monday night — and Charles Cross is doubtful.

That’s the headline. Charles Cross has a high ankle sprain. Ian Rapoport of NFL Network reports he could be back in one to two weeks, depending on rehab. He is listed doubtful for Monday.

And here’s why that’s the scariest sentence of the week: the Chicago Bears rank fourth in the NFL in pressure rate. And Sam Darnold has eight interceptions this season — four of them with a defender in his face.

Half of this quarterback’s interceptions have come when somebody got to him. And the man who protects his blind side probably isn’t playing.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is 4-3, defending Super Bowl champions. Chicago is 5-2. Monday night, November second, 8:15 Eastern, at Lumen Field, on ABC and ESPN. Seattle is favored by three.

And twelves, Seattle has to win this game. I’m going to tell you exactly why, I’m going to tell you exactly how they do it, and then I’m going to tell you my score.

First — why this is a must-win, and I don’t mean that as a figure of speech.

Reason one: Chicago is 5-2 and they’re in our conference.

This is not a cupcake. The Bears have the fourth-best record in the NFC. If Seattle loses at home on Monday night, you hand a direct wild-card competitor the head-to-head tiebreaker, and in January those tiebreakers decide who hosts and who travels. Conference games count twice: once in your column, once in theirs.

Reason two: San Francisco is still ahead of us.

The 49ers finally lost in Week 7 — Atlanta got them — but they’re still in front of Seattle in the NFC West. Seattle beating the Chiefs doesn’t matter if Seattle turns around and loses to Chicago. You don’t climb a division by alternating.

Reason three — and this is the one nobody talks about. The trade deadline is November tenth. Eight days after this game.

Twelves, think about what John Schneider is doing on Monday night. He is sitting in that stadium deciding whether this team is a buyer. Adam Schefter has already reported Seattle among four teams “expected to consider” a Jonathan Taylor trade.

If Seattle wins on Monday, this is a 5-3 defending champion with a top-two defense and a front office with eight days to go get something.

If Seattle loses on Monday, this is a 4-4 team that beat Kansas City once, and you don’t spend premium draft capital on a 4-4 team.

Monday night isn’t just a game. It’s the audition for the deadline.

Reason four: it’s a home Monday night game, in front of the twelves, as the defending Super Bowl champion. National television called Lumen Field one of the toughest places to play in football last week. You don’t get that reputation by losing prime-time home games to 5-2 teams.

Now let me be honest about Chicago, because I’m not going to insult them and neither should you.

Caleb Williams is playing the best football of his career right now. Since returning from a hamstring injury: 71.0 percent completion, 1,020 yards, five touchdowns and one interception in four games. Plus 128 rushing yards and two rushing touchdowns. He has thrown for over 300 yards in two straight weeks.

One interception in four games. He is not giving it away.

And here’s the number that should make you sit up: in the three full games Caleb Williams has started and finished this season, the Bears scored 59, 31 and 30 points.

Fifty-nine.

And Chicago has covered the spread in all three. They’re 5-2 against the number — the best cover record in the NFL. Seattle, meanwhile, is 2-3-2 against the spread. The market has been wrong about Seattle in both directions all year.

Two more things about the Bears defense you need to respect: they’re fourth in the league in pressure rate, and they’ve forced ten turnovers after leading the entire NFL in takeaways last season. They are a ball-hawking, quarterback-hunting defense, and Seattle’s left tackle is doubtful.

So that’s the case against us. Now here’s why I think Seattle wins anyway — and the numbers are not close.

Seattle’s defense is, by the measurements, the best in football right now.

Second in the NFL in defensive EPA per play. First in opponent yards per play. And they allow the fewest explosive passes in the entire league.

Read that last one again, because it is the direct antidote to Caleb Williams. A quarterback coming off back-to-back 300-yard games lives on explosive throws. Seattle allows fewer of them than anybody. You want to throw it over our heads? Last week Patrick Mahomes tried and finished with 165 passing yards, 213 total yards, eleven first downs and three points — the worst numbers of his entire career in a game he started.

And then there’s the matchup that decides this game. Chicago cannot stop the run.

The Bears rank 26th in run defense DVOA. 26th in ESPN’s run stop win rate. And they allow 4.4 yards per carry.

Twenty-sixth. Twenty-sixth. Four point four.

Twelves, do you understand what that is? That is the single best matchup on Seattle’s entire schedule. Last Sunday night, Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards against a defense that is genuinely good, and burned four minutes and forty-six seconds off the clock on one drive while Kansas City knew exactly what was coming.

Now take that offense and point it at the 26th-ranked run defense in football.

And one more: their defense allows the most yards per play in the NFL. They’re seventh in success rate, so they bend without breaking — but the chunk plays are there. And Seattle has Jaxon Smith-Njigba, who leads the league with 867 receiving yards, 231 clear of second place.

And both teams are down a left tackle. Braxton Jones is on injured reserve for Chicago. Cole Kmet is on IR too. Kyle Monangai is questionable. This isn’t a one-sided injury story.

So here’s the blueprint. Five steps. It’s the exact thing they did to Kansas City, and every single one of them is available on Monday.

Step one: make Caleb Williams one-dimensional.

This is what a national panel said about the Chiefs game, and it’s the whole philosophy: they couldn’t establish the run, so play-action stopped working, so Seattle didn’t need to blitz, so the front four pinned their ears back, and then it was a feeding frenzy.

Play-action is a loan against your run game. If Seattle takes away Chicago’s run — against a 26th-ranked run defense that has to lean on it even more because their own quarterback keeps carrying the ball — then Caleb Williams is throwing it thirty-five times against a defense that allows the fewest explosive passes in football. That’s not a fair fight.

Step two: possession is run defense.

Seattle held the ball thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds against Kansas City. That held the Chiefs to fifty plays. That held Kenneth Walker to fourteen carries.

You cannot score fifty-nine points in forty-eight snaps. Chicago’s offense is explosive — so don’t let it on the field. The way you beat a hot quarterback is to make him watch.

Step three: third down.

Seattle went eleven of sixteen against Kansas City after being among the worst third-down offenses in the league. Cooper Kupp said some of those conversions came on Sam Darnold’s fifth read. Third down is now a strength, and against a defense that bends, every conversion compounds.

Step four: feed Jaxon Smith-Njigba, and let him make history in the first quarter.

Twelves, this is my favorite stat of the week. Jaxon’s 867 yards are the third-most through seven games in the entire Super Bowl era — behind Tyreek Hill’s 902 in 2023 and Lance Alworth’s 871 in 1968.

He is four yards behind Lance Alworth. Four.

On his first catch Monday night, Jaxon Smith-Njigba passes a 1968 AFL legend and moves into second place in the Super Bowl era. Against a defense that gives up more yards per play than any other team in football.

Step five: run the ball, and finish in the red zone.

Seattle’s one real failure against Kansas City was the red zone — three trips inside the twenty produced six points. Macdonald said it himself, five times, in a press conference after winning by seventeen: “procedural stuff, red zone stuff, negative plays, how we rush, our red zone defense.”

Fix the red zone and Monday isn’t close.

Now the injury report in full, because the decisions here are the actual story.

Charles Cross: high ankle sprain. Doubtful. One to two weeks. Seattle has to put somebody else at left tackle against the fourth-best pressure-rate defense in football. That is the decision of the week, and it matters more than the trade deadline.

Rashid Shaheed: knee. Questionable. And the news is good. Macdonald, on Monday morning: “Any time the knee’s involved, you’re holding your breath. So right now, let’s just say it’s positive.”

“Let’s just say it’s positive.” From a coach who does not oversell anything. I’ll take it.

Zach Charbonnet: questionable — and this is genuinely emotional. He was activated off the PUP list, and last week he practiced fully for the first time since ACL surgery on February twentieth. He was ruled out against Kansas City anyway. Macdonald says he’ll practice at “full capacity” this week.

Macdonald’s words: “We’re sticking to our plan.” And: “He had a great week, in great spirits.”

Eight months from an ACL. “In great spirits.” I want that man on the field Monday night.

Jadarian Price: questionable. The rookie first-round pick has missed the last four games with a chest injury, he’s eligible to come off injured reserve this week, and Macdonald says he’s returning to practice.

Also questionable: Drew Lock (elbow), Drake Thomas, Chazz Surratt, Connor O’Toole, and Velus Jones Jr.

So read that list again. Charbonnet at full capacity. Price off IR. Shaheed positive. Seattle’s running back room could go from Emanuel Wilson and a prayer to three healthy bodies against the 26th-ranked run defense in football — in one week, for free.

Which brings me to the trade talk, and I’ll be quick, because I did a whole video on this.

The new wrinkle: Bleacher Report’s Alex Kay published a trade proposal — Emanuel Wilson, a second-round pick and a fourth-round pick to Indianapolis for Jonathan Taylor. And he suggests it could put Seattle back near the top of the Super Bowl favorites.

Let me be absolutely clear, because the channel this came from was clear too, and credit to them: that is a proposal. Not a report. Not a deal. A writer’s idea. Adam Schefter’s actual reporting is only that Seattle is “expected to consider” a Taylor trade, alongside Denver, Minnesota and Green Bay.

And my answer is the same as it was: a second AND a fourth AND the running back who just rushed for a hundred yards? No.

Taylor’s updated numbers are better than when I last looked — 587 rushing yards and eight touchdowns — so he is genuinely producing. But Seattle’s run problem was a right guard, not a running back. Anthony Bradford came back and Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards and allowed zero sacks. And Charbonnet and Price are both practicing this week.

Make the call. Don’t pay the premium. If Indianapolis’s interim front office gets desperate for picks and the price drops to a mid-rounder, do it instantly. Otherwise keep your capital — because with Charles Cross hurt, the position Seattle might actually need to buy is offensive tackle, not running back.

And two corrections, because the version going around is stale.

Seattle is 4-3, not 3-3. The 3-3 was before the Chiefs game. And San Francisco is not 5-0 anymore — they lost in Week 7. The only undefeated team left in the entire NFL is Minnesota. That matters, because the panic framing of “we’re 3-3 and the 49ers are perfect” is simply no longer true. The division opened up the same weekend Seattle beat Kansas City.

Also worth knowing honestly: Seattle’s offense is still 24th in EPA per play and averaging 3.9 yards a carry on the season, and 113.7 rushing yards a game — roughly ten fewer than last year’s regular season and twenty fewer than the playoff run that ended with a championship. One great night doesn’t erase seven weeks. It just proves the ceiling exists.

Now — the one thing that could actually beat us, and I’d be lying if I pretended otherwise.

Turnovers.

Chicago is fourth in pressure rate. They’ve forced ten turnovers. They led the league in takeaways last season. Charles Cross is doubtful. And four of Sam Darnold’s eight interceptions have come with a defender in his face.

That is the entire path to a Seattle loss, and it’s a real one. If the replacement left tackle struggles, Darnold gets hit, the ball comes out, and Caleb Williams gets a short field — on a night Chicago is capable of scoring thirty.

So the game plan isn’t complicated: run the ball, keep it on schedule, don’t throw it into pressure. Which, conveniently, is exactly what Seattle did against the Chiefs. Forty-one carries. Zero sacks. Two hits. Eleven of sixteen on third down.

And let me actually explain how you protect a quarterback without his left tackle, because there’s a real answer and Seattle already showed it.

Mike Macdonald said one specific thing after the Chiefs game that I keep coming back to: “Thought our protection plan was great.”

Plan. Not our line was great. Our plan. And a protection plan against an elite pass rush has four pieces, all of which Seattle has:

One: run the ball on first down. A pass rusher who has to respect the run cannot get up the field. Forty-one carries is a pass-protection statistic. Chicago’s edge rushers are fourth in the league at pressuring quarterbacks — and they are also part of a defense that gives up 4.4 yards a carry. You cannot pin your ears back and set an edge at the same time.

Two: chip help. This is what A.J. Barner and the running backs are for. Macdonald told us Barner is “always within the plan” and “a tough guy to bring down.” A tight end who delays an edge rusher for half a second turns a sack into a completion. That’s the cheapest protection in football and it doesn’t require a Pro Bowl tackle.

Three: the quick game. Sam Darnold’s own diagnosis from his Thursday podium was two and a half seconds — that’s the quick-game benchmark. You don’t need five reads if the first one is open in 2.5. Against a pressure defense with a backup at left tackle, you call three-step throws and let the rusher run past an empty pocket.

Four: the quarterback’s legs. Darnold scrambled for first downs on two separate third-and-longs against Kansas City. That’s not a system — that’s an escape valve, and it works.

Seattle allowed zero sacks against Steve Spagnuolo with a protection plan. The plan is the thing. The tackle is one input into it.

Here’s my prediction. And I want to be clear that this is a prediction — my opinion, not a report. Nobody has told me anything.

Seahawks 27, Bears 17.

How it happens: Seattle runs for over 150 yards again against the 26th-ranked run defense in football. Possession over 34 minutes. Jaxon Smith-Njigba goes over 100 yards and passes Lance Alworth on his first catch of the night. Caleb Williams throws for his yards — he’ll get 250, maybe more — but he doesn’t get the explosives, because nobody gets explosives on this defense, and Chicago’s points come on long drives that take four minutes each.

And the streak goes to thirty-seven games without allowing a hundred-yard rusher.

I think Seattle wins this game, and I think it looks like Sunday night looked. Not because I’m a fan — because Chicago’s weakness is precisely Seattle’s newly rediscovered strength, and Seattle’s strength is precisely what Chicago needs to beat them. That’s not hope. That’s the matchup.
