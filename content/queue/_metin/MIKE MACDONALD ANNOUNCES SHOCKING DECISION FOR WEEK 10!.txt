“He’s not going to take every rep. He’s not going to make every throw again.”

Seahawks Nation, there’s your decision for Week 10 — and Mike Macdonald just confirmed it.

Drew Lock is the plan again. And they’re running the exact same managed-throw week they ran against Arizona.

His words: “Drew’s making good progress. We have a plan. And similar to last week, we’ll go from there.”

Similar to last week. Twelves, last week Drew Lock threw roughly TWENTY footballs in an entire week of preparation and then went out and won seventeen to three.

They’re doing it again.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four, the Las Vegas Raiders are Sunday — and this game has a storyline you need to understand before kickoff, because Seattle is about to play against its own offense. Let’s go.

First, the quarterback, because that’s the actual news.

Macdonald’s full answer: “Drew’s making good progress. We have a plan. And similar to last week, we’ll go from there. He’s not going to take every rep, he’s not going to make every throw again — but I think that’s just trying to be as prudent as possible through the process, to make sure that he’s feeling his best on Sunday.”

“To make sure he’s feeling his best on SUNDAY.”

Twelves, that’s the whole philosophy and it’s worth saying plainly: Seattle is no longer trying to make Drew Lock healthy for practice. They’re spending his health on the game.

And remember what we learned about how that worked last week. Lock told us he threw on one day — Thursday. He didn’t throw Wednesday. He didn’t throw in pregame until he had the shoulder pads and helmet on, and they spent all of Saturday building that plan.

His own words: “the arm felt fresh.”

Of course it did. That was the entire point.

And the quiet part of this answer: Macdonald mentioned the elbow plan but did not mention the oblique as a separate problem. Lock came off the field in the third quarter on Sunday being evaluated for one. Macdonald didn’t say “he’s dealing with two things.” He said “we have a plan” and “similar to last week.”

That reads to me like the oblique is folded into the same management, not a new crisis. He’d have said so otherwise — this coach has told us everything all season.

Now the injury report, and twelves, this is genuinely encouraging.

Asked about Charles Cross, Leonard Williams, Uchenna Nwosu and Rashid Shaheed:

“Well, we’re not going full speed today, so a lot of those guys will have the opportunity to take reps today, and then we’ll see tomorrow. Haven’t ruled anybody out yet, nor have we ruled anybody in yet. But good news on a lot of those guys.”

“GOOD NEWS on a lot of those guys.”

That is a meaningful change in language and I want you to notice it.

Last week the line was “I’d say they all have a chance to play.” That’s a hedge. This week it’s “good news on a lot of those guys.” That’s optimism.

And the mechanics matter here: “we’re not going full speed today, so a lot of those guys will have the opportunity to take reps.”

That’s the trick of a Wednesday walkthrough. A man who can’t practice at full speed can still practice at walkthrough speed. So a guy who’s been stuck at “did not participate” for two weeks can get on the grass today, get his assignments, get his footwork — and then the real test is tomorrow at full tempo.

“We’ll see tomorrow.” Thursday is the day that decides Sunday for Charles Cross, Leonard Williams, Rashid Shaheed and Uchenna Nwosu.

And remember what’s at stake there. Leonard Williams just missed his first game since Week 4 of the 2024 season. Charles Cross has been out two straight weeks. Rashid Shaheed is the only true vertical speed threat on this roster.

Getting even two of those four men back changes what Seattle can do on Sunday.

NOW — the Quenton Nelson update, and this is the best news of the day.

“He had his procedure on Monday.” And then Macdonald said:

“No — he’s here. He’s back in the building, and he looks good. We were able to get to some things treatment-wise that are of the caliber... which is MUSIC TO MY EARS. So I know he’s going to be back sooner than later, if it’s under his control. And we’ll take it really week by week at this point.”

“Music to my ears.”

Twelves, let me lay out this timeline for you, because it’s remarkable.

Sunday: Seattle plays a division game without him. Monday: he has knee surgery. Wednesday — two days after surgery — he is physically in the building and his head coach says he “looks good.”

And the key phrase: “we were able to get to some things treatment-wise.”

That’s surgeon language, and it means the procedure found what they expected and fixed it. When a minor procedure goes badly, you don’t hear “we were able to get to some things.” You hear “we’ll know more in a few weeks.”

And “if it’s under his control” is Macdonald telling you Quenton Nelson is going to try to come back faster than he’s supposed to.

Now let me do the calendar math I did last week, because it’s still the key to everything. Seattle plays at Las Vegas on Sunday. Then Week 11 is the BYE. Then Week 12 is at San Francisco on November twenty-ninth.

That’s two and a half weeks of calendar with only ONE football game inside it after Sunday.

And remember Macdonald’s own framing when he moved the offensive line back: “at least this game and X amount of practices, and then going into the bye.”

The bye week is doing the heavy lifting. Quenton Nelson at San Francisco on November twenty-ninth is very much in play — and Seattle never put him on injured reserve, which would have cost four games minimum.

Now the storyline of this entire football game, and if you don’t know this, Sunday won’t make sense.

Seattle’s offensive coordinator from last season — Klint Kubiak — is the head coach of the Las Vegas Raiders.

So on Sunday, Mike Macdonald’s defense plays against the offense Mike Macdonald watched being built in his own building.

And he was asked how similar it looks:

“There’s a lot of parallels. I think, to their credit, they’ve evolved. They’re building it around the people that they have, and they’re doing it at a high level. So I respect how they operate, obviously. Got great people and good players, and they’re moving the ball and putting up a lot of points.”

“A lot of parallels.” And “they’re moving the ball and putting up a lot of points.”

That’s not coach-speak politeness. That’s a warning.

But here’s the part I loved, and it’s the most human moment of the press conference.

A reporter asked whether you have to guard against overthinking it — like, okay, he knows I think this way, so...

And Macdonald cut him off: “Sure. Yeah. Yeah, for sure. Get in your own way. Yeah.”

“Get in your own way.”

Twelves, this is a real trap and it’s called the familiarity spiral.

When a coach plays against someone who knows his system, the temptation is to go three levels deep. He knows I like to pressure on third down. So he’ll expect pressure. So I’ll drop into coverage instead. But he knows I know he knows — so maybe he expects the drop, so I should pressure after all.

And by the time you’ve run that loop, you’ve abandoned the things your players are actually good at in order to win a chess match in your own head.

Macdonald named it and dismissed it in four words. That’s a coach who’s already decided not to do it.

And his entire approach to the week backs that up: “I think really focusing on our details, and making sure we’re aligned and on the same page and executing at a high level — and then go into the game and make the best adjustments we possibly can as the game starts to unfold.”

Details, alignment, execution, then adjust live. That’s it. No chess match.

Now the strangest part of this matchup: Seattle is preparing for a quarterback who has thrown about EIGHT passes in his NFL career.

That’s Fernando Mendoza — the Raiders’ rookie, out of Indiana, and California before that.

And Macdonald’s answer on how you even prepare for that is excellent:

“Well, I think we have to do a great job of understanding how they operate by situation. Probably at their core. And then I think it’s one of those things where — you talk about this at the beginning of the season too — you want to use the information available to you, but you can’t put all your eggs in one basket on what’s going to happen, or how you think the quarterback’s going to play. That wouldn’t be wise.”

And then: “Obviously we’re going to watch his plays, and lean on our information we had coming out of the draft process. But put our best foot forward.”

“Lean on our information we had coming out of the draft process.”

Twelves, that’s a fascinating admission about how NFL teams actually work.

Seattle scouted this guy. Every team did. They have a file on Fernando Mendoza from before the draft — his college tape, his pro day, his interview, his tendencies, his footwork, what he does under pressure. And because there are only eight NFL passes to study, that pre-draft file is suddenly the primary document.

The scouting department just became the game plan.

And when a reporter asked whether they’d go back to the Cal and Indiana tape, Macdonald’s answer was a flat “no.”

That’s the right call, and here’s why. College tape tells you about the player. It tells you nothing about what the Raiders are going to ask him to do. Different system, different coach, different protections, different receivers. The man’s college film is a scouting document, not a game-plan document.

And on the preseason: “They do a little bit. But it’s kind of do the best you can, you know?”

“Do the best you can.” That’s the most honest answer available and he just said it out loud.

And then he made the smartest point of the whole segment:

“You can’t — I don’t want to speak for Klint, but it seems like, I mean, we’ve been through personnel changes in the middle of the season. You can’t make wholesale changes fundamentally and systematically to what you’re doing. Sure, they’re thinking the same thing. They’re going to tweak some things, as you do every week. But you never want to just put all your cards on one thing, so to speak.”

Twelves, this is the key insight for Sunday, so let me spell it out.

A team does not rebuild its offense because the quarterback changed.

The protections stay. The run game stays. The route concepts stay. The terminology stays. You tweak — you lean on the things your new guy does well and you hide the things he doesn’t. But the skeleton is the same skeleton.

Which means Seattle isn’t actually preparing for a mystery quarterback. They’re preparing for Klint Kubiak’s offense, which they know intimately — with a rookie operating it.

And Macdonald’s summary: “it does shift our focus in terms of how we’re trying to game plan a little bit, but our process really remains the same, honestly.”

On Mendoza himself, he was gracious and brief: “He’s a really good player, and he’s going to have a great career. You can tell that by the information we had coming out. He’s got a great skill set. But we’ll focus on us, and us making sure that we’re playing our brand of ball, and try to apply as much pressure as we can on their offense.”

“Apply as much pressure as we can.”

That’s the only sentence in there about strategy, and it’s the whole strategy. Against a quarterback with eight career NFL passes, you don’t need to be exotic. You need to make him play fast, make him hold the ball, and make him beat you with his third read.

Now — the best football answer of the press conference, and it’s about Nick Emmanwori.

Asked what makes Emmanwori so good at matching up with tight ends, after he covered Trey McBride last week:

“Well, yeah — I think some, not all tight ends run the routes equally. They run different route trees, things like that, in terms of timing of plays and how they get open. Nick has the ability to — he can run with anybody, as we know — but the ability to stay CONNECTED at like the top of routes.”

“Stay connected at the top of routes.”

Twelves, this is the single most useful coverage concept I can teach you, so stay with me.

“The top of the route” is the break point — the moment the receiver stops running straight and changes direction. That’s where separation is created or denied. Everything before that is just running.

And “staying connected” means not losing contact with the man at that exact instant. A defender can run step for step with a tight end for twelve yards and still lose the play entirely if the tight end gets a half-yard at the break.

And here’s why it’s harder against tight ends specifically — Macdonald explained it himself:

“The best tight ends are able to use physicality to create separation. And Nick’s able to either kind of fight fire with fire on that front, or be able to absorb — we won’t call them pushoffs, but, you know, I HAVE MY OWN OPINION ABOUT IT — where you can absorb some of those tricks of the trade, so to speak, and still stay connected.”

“We won’t call them pushoffs, but I have my own opinion about it.”

[That’s a head coach accusing NFL tight ends of getting away with offensive pass interference, in public, with a smile, without quite saying it.]

And he’s right, twelves. That’s the actual skill of an elite tight end. A 250-pound man at the top of a route puts a hand on the defender’s hip and shoves off. It happens on almost every snap. It’s almost never called.

So a safety covering a tight end has two options: get physical first, or be strong enough to absorb the shove and not get moved.

And Nick Emmanwori — six foot three, 227 pounds, with a perfect 10.00 Relative Athletic Score — can do both. He can run with anybody, and he can’t be pushed off the route.

That is why Seattle took the blitz away from him last week and put him on McBride. And it worked: the ball didn’t go there on third down after third down, and on fourth down at the goal line, Emmanwori broke it up.

And “another good tight end obviously this week” tells you the plan is the same on Sunday.

Now the trade deadline — and the honest answer about why nothing happened.

Asked whether Seattle was close to anything:

“Uh, you all know how John rolls. He’s always kind of doing his thing in terms of being in as many things as possible. But nothing really became — nothing became serious enough for us to have that type of conversation.”

“Nothing became serious enough.”

And remember what John Schneider himself said before the deadline: “keeping your powder dry a little bit,” and “we’re still talking to several teams.”

So he kept the powder dry. He was in conversations. Nothing got far enough.

And then Macdonald said this, and it’s the line I’d put on the wall:

“But look — I love our team. The guys that we have, going out from here on out, I’m very optimistic. We have work to do. We have room to grow. And I believe that we can, and we will.”

“I love our team. We have work to do. We have room to grow.”

Twelves, that’s exactly the right balance and it’s the same balance Cooper Kupp struck after the Arizona win. Kupp said there’d be “some really cool stuff to see” on film and also “some really tough stuff to watch.” Macdonald says he loves this team and that it has work to do.

Nobody in that building is overselling five and four. That’s why I believe them when they’re optimistic.

And remember, Seattle did make its big move already — a 2027 third and a 2028 fifth for a Pro Bowl guard. That was the deadline move. It just happened two weeks early.

Now, the part of this press conference I found most revealing about how Seattle is actually run.

Macdonald was asked about Klint Kubiak as a person, and whether he saw a future head coach:

“I just really loved working with Klint. He was a great team player. And I think that mentality is probably an underrated part of being a head coach — being able to see everything kind of in a 360-degree viewpoint. Unselfishly, he was able to remove himself from a lot of situations and do things for the betterment of the team, consistently.”

And then: “He has a way about him where he can tell you the truth and go THROUGH, not around — without sugarcoating it, but also keeping the personal emotions out of it. Which I think gathered respect from both the rest of our coaches and our team.”

“He can tell you the truth and go through, not around.”

That is a beautiful description of a professional and it tells you what Macdonald values in people. Not diplomacy. Not avoidance. Direct truth, delivered without emotion attached to it.

And then a reporter asked something great: they’d heard Kubiak let his assistants submit plays for possible inclusion in the game plan — and does Macdonald do the same with his defensive staff?

“I feel like that’s pretty common. I mean, we still do that on offense, and we do it on defense. A lot of stuff we’re running is NOT STARTING IN MY HEAD — it’s starting in our staff’s head. And it’s all about how we make it come to life.”

“A lot of stuff we’re running is not starting in my head.”

Twelves, that’s a head coach publicly saying his best ideas belong to other people.

And then he explained why it matters, which is the smart part:

“And the reason staff dynamics are so important is because now you can make it be CONNECTED — and you’re not just running like one-off plays. It’s all kind of seamless. So that takes those types of conversations and that process to be right.”

“You’re not just running one-off plays.”

That’s the difference between a system and a collection of gadgets. A play that somebody loved in isolation is a one-off. A play that’s been argued over, fit into a progression, and connected to the three calls around it is a system.

And the closing thought: “when you’re working at your best, everyone’s contributing and everyone feels like they have a say in what we’re doing and how we’re doing it. And I think that’s important.”

That’s the same coach who said after Sunday: “I don’t care about the score. I just want us to go be us and feel like us.” He builds the same way he coaches.

And last, special teams — because the Raiders are dangerous here.
