"I'm responsible for what I say. No matter the circumstance. Whether you're miked up, whether you're not, whether you have some certain type of understanding."

That's Mike Macdonald this week, taking a hit on the chin in front of every reporter in the building. And Seahawks Nation, that press conference was one of the most interesting fifteen minutes I've heard from an NFL head coach in a long time — because he didn't just handle the controversy. He explained how he evaluates players, how his offense hunts explosive plays, and the specific edge his staff has been chasing for the last twenty-four months.

And the bad news: Julian Love is ruled out. Macdonald said his calf tightened up at practice, and now he's out, with real uncertainty about how long. With Ty Okada also out for a third straight game with the hamstring, Seattle's starting safeties on Sunday will be Rodney Thomas the Second — the practice squad call-up who now has a permanent roster spot — and AJ Finley, who will be making the first start of his career after missing all of last season with a knee injury.

Read that again. The defending Super Bowl champions are starting two safeties on Sunday with two career starts between them, and both of those came in the last two weeks.

Also: Jadarian Price is cleared to play after a full practice Friday. Brandon Pili is questionable with the concussion and neck. And Zach Charbonnet might get his practice window opened next week.

Okay. Now let's get into the press conference, because this is the good part.

Start with something small that tells you a lot. Macdonald opened by noting this is Seattle's first normal seven-day week of the entire season — Week 1 was a Wednesday, then eleven days to Arizona — and that it comes with a ten a.m. body-clock kickoff on the East Coast. His message to the team that day: "Let's be decisive in our process."

That's not a throwaway line. When your body thinks it's ten in the morning, the first thing that fails isn't effort or talent. It's decision speed. So before the plane even leaves, the head coach installs decisiveness as the theme of the week.

Next: how do you scout a team with only two weeks of tape and new coordinators? His answer was honest — what they expected is unfolding, and "you've got to get out of the first quarter to the third of the season to really declare what you are schematically." He even apologized that it was the same answer as the last two weeks. That's the truth about September football: anybody claiming they've solved a team from two games of tape is guessing.

Now here's the section that I think every football fan should hear, because he was asked a great question: how do you figure out what a player is actually capable of?

And his answer was beautiful. He said sometimes it's a rep the player loses. A pass rusher gets beaten — but Macdonald sees the lower-body flex. He sees the twitch. And he thinks, man, that'd be really cool, we could work with him like this. Same thing with coverage and movement ability.

That's the whole thing right there. Most of us watch a play and ask who won. A defensive coach watches the same play and asks what the body just showed him — the result of the rep is almost irrelevant. And his specific example of a skill he noticed? Jadarian Price catching the ball out of the backfield.

Then he explained something I'd never heard a coach put this plainly. Seattle has what he called an installation philosophy in the spring and in camp: they deliberately install plays they don't necessarily plan to call during the season, just to touch every skill and movement and see what guys can actually do. Then it narrows down as camp unfolds.

They install plays they never intend to run. Not to use them — to audition their own players. That's why Seattle keeps having the right guy for the right moment. They tested it in May when nobody was watching.

Then a reporter asked whether players with unique abilities challenge a coach's imagination. Macdonald said definitely — and immediately went to Nick Emmanwori.

And his answer was really disciplined. With Nick coming back, he said it's even more of an emphasis to have a tight plan for exactly what they're asking him to do, because you do get excited about what he can do. Then the key line: "there's only so many reps in a week." They have to protect themselves to get the best version of Nick when he's out there.

Think about what he's admitting. I have a player so versatile I could give him twenty jobs — and if I did, he'd be worse at all of them. So I'm limiting my own creativity on purpose. The temptation with a six-three, 220-pound safety who played seven positions as a rookie is to use him everywhere. Macdonald is choosing focus over fun.

And the workload update: "significantly higher" this week. He said they'd see how the game unfolds and how Nick feels, but that he'd be out there "a good bit." So after nine snaps in Arizona, expect a real role Sunday — which matters enormously now that Julian Love is out.

Now, Rodney Thomas the Second, and this was my favorite answer of the whole press conference.

Asked what he'd learned about Thomas in two weeks, Macdonald said: "Shout out to Dennis Green. He's the guy we thought he was."

If you don't get the reference, Dennis Green was the coach behind one of the most famous rants in NFL history — "they were who we said they were!" So Macdonald flipped it into a compliment, and then he did something I really respect: he gave the credit away. He shouted out Seattle's personnel staff for identifying the type of player who would thrive here, specifically. And then he praised Thomas for working his tail off to play at a high level.

That phrase — "thrive here" — is the important one. Seattle's scouting department isn't just grading players. They're grading fit. And two weeks later, that guy is starting at safety in a championship defense.

Alright. Now the apology, because he handled it about as well as it can be handled.

Asked about the video that leaked — the story about Sean Payton and the training camp clips — Macdonald said it wasn't something he intended for public use, and that the most appropriate thing was to reach out to Sean and apologize for it ending up public when it wasn't meant to. He pointed out that Denver had to deal with it in real time through no fault of their own.

And then the quote I opened with. He said he has to be better in that moment, that he's responsible for what he says no matter the circumstance. He said he fell short, that "it stings for me, frankly," and that he'll learn from it. Asked whether all of this was on the record, his answer was perfect: if I'm talking, I'm responsible for what I'm saying. No blaming the production company, no "taken out of context," and he wouldn't even get into how the clip leaked — the details aren't important, it got out.

On Payton: "Sean was great. Sean was great." He said he respects the heck out of him and his program, called him a phenomenal football coach, and said both of them are ready to move on.

That's how you close a story instead of feeding it. And honestly — the way he took responsibility there is the same thing he asks from his players.

Now let's get to the best pure football answer of the day, because this is the one I'd play for anybody who wants to understand this team.

He was asked how Seattle creates explosive plays — scheme, or exploiting defensive mistakes?

His answer: you have to "hunt" explosive plays. Every play is in the game plan for a reason. There's an enormous amount of manpower and resources behind what plays get run and when, so when you watch tape, you watch it through that lens and try to connect the dots on why teams call what they call. His words: "we run things for a reason."

But then came the balance, and this is the part that explains Drew Lock's zero turnovers. He said you have to abide by your rules so you execute the right way. You're not forcing the ball. But when things are there, guys have the liberty to "go rip it."

Hunt explosives, but never force them. That's why Seattle has an 82-yard touchdown and no interceptions in the same two-game stretch.

And then he tied both sides of the ball together in a way I hadn't heard before. He said most explosive plays come from missed tackles — and that missed tackles are a function of pressure. On defense: if you populate the ball and take great angles, it's hard for the offense to create explosives. On offense: if you eliminate the defense's good angles with your ball carriers and keep pushing piles and keep moving forward, that applies pressure on the defense, and eventually, in his words, "the ball will pop."

That's one philosophy running both directions. Squeeze the other team until somebody misses. Then take the big play they hand you. That's not an offensive plan and a defensive plan. That's a worldview.

And then he revealed something specific that I found fascinating. He was asked about dissecting run blocking, and he said he's learned a lot in the last year or two from his own coaching staff about how teams block certain fronts and certain looks — more than he had awareness of earlier in his career. And he called it, directly, "an edge that we're chasing right now over the last 24 months."

He says it's helped Seattle's run defense because guys can play more decisively and play blocks rather than guess, and that understanding who's going to block who matters when you're designing pressures.

Stop and think about that for a second. Here's a 39-year-old head coach who just won a Super Bowl as a defensive mastermind, and he's telling reporters that he's been learning from his own assistants for two years — and that it's made his run defense better. That's the same guy who went looking through fan videos on social media for an edge. It's all the same instinct: assume you don't know enough yet.

A couple more quick ones.

On Dante Fowler Jr., who's had limited snaps: Macdonald said he's been great, with a great spirit and a unique personality — and then this line, which tells you everything about the culture: Fowler is an example of how you can be your own guy and be a great Seahawk at the same time. On the field, he called Fowler's movement ability remarkable, the kind of thing that makes you start inventing ways to deploy him.

On the offensive line after Bradford's IR move: Macdonald expects AB back this season, and when asked whether it's the four-week minimum or longer, he said you're looking at "the shorter side." He praised the Christian Haynes and Beau Stephens competition, and on Stephens specifically: power, finishing plays, chasing the pile. Then the highest compliment you can pay a rookie lineman — "Game's not too big for him. There's poise. There's strength."

Last one, and it's about the offense. Asked for the big picture through two weeks — including how it's gone with Brian Fleury — Macdonald called it a big question with a lot to unpack, then said Seattle is off to a strong start and that he loves the spirit of how they're doing business. He talked about "connective tissue" between the staff, Fleury and the players. He said considering the dynamic of the group, he's really impressed with how they've operated, and that they're establishing an identity.

But he wouldn't let it get comfortable. He said it's still early and he likes feeling the sense of urgency about the growth available to them. And then he described what working with Fleury is actually like: he comes in and says hey, this is awesome, this is awesome — but don't you think we could be better here, here and here? And the answer comes back that they're already on it, there's already a plan. He called that reassuring. Everybody solving problems together, seeing it through the same lens.

So let me pull it all together.

In one press conference, Macdonald took full public responsibility for a mistake, credited his scouting staff instead of himself, admitted he's still learning from his own assistants, deliberately limited his most exciting player so he doesn't ruin him, and explained a philosophy that ties his offense and defense into one idea.

That's why this team is 2-0 with a twelve-game winning streak and two backup safeties starting Sunday. It isn't magic. It's a coach who assumes he doesn't know enough and a building that tests everything.

And Sunday we get the payoff: Sam Darnold back under center, JSN on a historic run, Emmanwori with a real workload, and a Washington team starting Marcus Mariota against a defense that's allowed seventeen points in two games.
