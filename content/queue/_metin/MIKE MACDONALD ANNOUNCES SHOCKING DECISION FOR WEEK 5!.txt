A reporter walked up to Mike Macdonald on Friday and told him that the Seattle Seahawks have the most efficient screen game in the National Football League, and that San Francisco is third.

And then he delivered the punchline: Seattle is thirtieth in the league at defending the screen. And San Francisco struggles at it too.

And the head coach of the defending Super Bowl champions laughed and said:

“Maybe I should be the one asking how you stop it. I mean, we’re that bad at it.”

Seahawks Nation, that one exchange is tomorrow’s football game. Two of the three most efficient screen teams in football, neither of whom can defend a screen, in the same stadium, for first place in the NFC West.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Macdonald met the media on Friday and it was the best press conference he’s given all year — the screen war, the stat a reporter got wrong by fifteen years, a tactical reveal about Rashid Shaheed, and one sentence about John Schneider I can’t stop thinking about. Plus the final injury report, which has real news in it. Let’s go.

Leonard Williams is playing.

When Macdonald was asked Friday morning whether he expected him out there, the answer was “he’ll be out there in some capacity.” And then the official game designations came out, and Leonard Williams has no injury designation at all. He’s not questionable. He’s off the report entirely.

Macdonald’s words: “Leonard is gonna play.” And then the line that explains the whole week: “We just wanted to do right by him.”

And I’ll own this: I talked about it all week, got the shape right and the conclusion wrong. Wednesday limited, Thursday a full DNP, and I told you a backwards Thursday meant sitting him.

Thursday wasn’t a setback. Thursday was a coaching staff buying a thirty-two-year-old veteran an extra day off. That’s what “do right by him” means, and it’s the smartest thing in this report. A twelfth-year, three-time Pro Bowler who’s missed one game in three seasons doesn’t need Thursday’s practice. He needs Sunday’s ankle.

So the man with three sacks, five tackles for loss and six quarterback hits in four games — all team highs — Seattle’s own first-quarter Defensive MVP, with more sacks than any defensive tackle in football since the start of 2024, is going to play.

And there’s a second piece of good news nobody’s leading with: Ty Okada has no designation and will make his season debut. Four games on the shelf with the hamstring, and he’s active tomorrow.

Here’s the full final sheet, both sides.

Seattle: out — Julian Neal with a hamstring and Chazz Surratt with a calf. Questionable — George Holani with ribs and Irv Charles with a knee. Everybody else is clean.

San Francisco: out — Nick Bosa with the knee and the calf, Kyle Juszczyk with a knee, Marques Sigle with an ankle, and James Thompson with an ankle. Questionable — Upton Stout with a shoulder and Mykel Williams with a knee, who is being activated off the physically-unable-to-perform list. And Mike Evans is not on the injury report — he plays.

Nick Bosa is officially out. It’s done. He’s reportedly “pretty darn likely” for Week Six, which means Seattle gets him in the rematch, but tomorrow he’s in a baseball cap.

Okay. Now the screens, because I think this is genuinely the most underrated story of this entire week.

Here are the real numbers, and they’re better than the question.

Seattle has thrown thirteen screen passes this season for 138 yards and a touchdown. That’s 10.6 yards per attempt — second in the National Football League. And on EPA per dropback on screen passes, Seattle is first in the entire league. Number one.

So “most efficient screen game in football” is accurate by the measure that actually matters.

Who’s catching them? Emanuel Wilson took a screen twenty-three yards for a touchdown in the first quarter against the Chargers, then caught another one for thirteen later in that same game. And the week before, in Washington, AJ Barner caught two tight-end screens for thirty-two and fifteen.

Now let me teach you why this matters tomorrow, because a screen is not a cute little play. A screen is a run play wearing a pass play’s clothing.

And Macdonald explained it better than I can. He called it “complementary” — it plays off your action game, your keeper game, your drop-back game. It’s the same idea as play-action: you only get it because of everything else you do.

But here’s the part that makes it lethal, and it’s the sentence I want you to remember: “it’s an extra thing our rushers have to handle.”

Think about what that does to a pass rush. A great defensive end’s whole job is to get upfield as fast as humanly possible. That’s the skill. That’s what he’s paid for. And on a screen, the harder he runs upfield, the bigger the hole he leaves behind him. You use his best quality against him. You make a man punish himself for being good at his job.

Which is exactly why this is enormous tomorrow. San Francisco’s defense is built on edge speed — and it’s built on edge speed without Nick Bosa, which means the guys replacing him are going to be trying extra hard to prove something, running extra hard upfield, on a day when Seattle leads the league in screen efficiency.

And then Macdonald gave away the entire coaching recipe for stopping one, which I loved, because it’s a free football lesson:

“You need to get the ball stopped in the alley. A lot of times the ball is supposed to hit in the alley. So get it stopped, get it forced, and then get backside pursuit to get people on the ground.”

The “alley” is that space between the tackle box and the sideline. Get the ball stopped there, force the runner back inside, and let the chase team catch him from behind. Three steps. Simple to say.

And Seattle is thirtieth in the league at doing it.

So tomorrow, both of these teams are going to throw screens, and neither of them can stop one. Whoever’s alley defense shows up wins a quarter of this football game.

And the most human moment of the week came next, when the reporter noted that Macdonald keeps lighting up about this. His answer: “I just can’t remember being on a team with a great screen game. So excited to be part of it.”

A Super Bowl-winning defensive mastermind, genuinely delighted that his own offense is finally good at a thing he’s never had. He said “how about that?” about it twice this week. And then the coach came straight back out: “We’ve had good returns, but it doesn’t mean we stop working on it.”

He credited the linemen for hitting their targets on the perimeter and in the alley, and the runners for being more decisive. Those are coaching details, not luck.

Alright. Now the stat a reporter brought up, and I have to fix it, because it was off by fifteen years.

The question was that San Francisco is the first team in about thirty-six years to start a season 4-0 without allowing a sack.

It’s twenty-one years. The last team to do it was the Indianapolis Colts in 2005. And the 49ers are only the fifth team to do it since 1963, which is when this league started officially tracking sacks.

Four games. Zero sacks. That’s the real number and it’s terrifying enough without inflating it.

The line doing it: Trent Williams at left tackle, Jake Brendel, Dominick Puni, Colton McKivitz, plus a newcomer named Connor Colby. And Brock Purdy has faced pressure on just thirty-five drop-backs all season — the second-fewest of any team in football.

But here’s what made Macdonald’s answer so interesting. He was asked what that offensive line is doing, and he barely talked about the offensive line.

“Really good team football. They’re running the ball. They’re staying ahead of schedule. There’s very limited third downs. When they do throw it, they’re playing on time.”

And then: “There hasn’t been many occasions where there’s people unaccounted for for the quarterback. And when there has been, he’s gotten rid of the ball.”

That’s the real answer, twelves, and it’s the most useful thing in this press conference. You don’t get sacked if you’re never in third-and-long. The zero isn’t five offensive linemen being superhuman for a month. It’s an offense that never puts them in a losing down.

Which completely changes what Seattle’s job is tomorrow. The job is not “beat Trent Williams.” The job is win first down. Make it second-and-nine. Make it third-and-eight. Get them off schedule, and the sack streak dies on its own.

And on Purdy himself, Macdonald refused to play the game: “I’d have a hard time underrating him. I think he’s a hell of a player.” Then the breakdown: “He can throw it at all three levels. He throws on time. He throws it into windows with timing that makes it hard to defend sometimes.” And the part he said deserves more credit — the competitiveness, the athleticism and the playmaking as the down extends.

Which is why he said this about the rush plan, and it’s my favorite phrasing of the week: “it’s kind of not a one-man job for us. It’s an eleven, twelve-man job.”

Eleven, twelve. He counts the coaches. You don’t sack Brock Purdy with a guy. You sack him with a scheme.

Now the Juszczyk problem, and this is a genuinely fascinating coaching puzzle.

Kyle Juszczyk is out. And Macdonald pointed out that San Francisco “have run twenty-one personnel more than anybody else and been very good in it.”

Quick translation: 21 personnel means two running backs and one tight end. Juszczyk is the fullback. He is that formation.

And here’s the problem Macdonald described: “it’s harder to anticipate how they’re going to deploy their people... Kyle’s been extremely durable over the years, so there’s not a lot of things you can go to and see how they actually did it with the people on their current roster.”

Read that again, because it’s remarkable. There is almost no film of the San Francisco 49ers playing without Kyle Juszczyk. The man never misses. So Seattle is preparing for a formation change it has never seen this team make, with no tape to study.

And Macdonald’s plan is honest in a way coaches usually aren’t: “I don’t think they’re going to end up in eleven personnel all game. I don’t think that’s how they want to operate. We’ll have a plan for eleven, but we’re anticipating multiple tight end people out there, and we’ll have to adjust throughout the game to test how we think they’re going to play it.”

Test how we think they’re going to play it. That’s a head coach saying out loud: we’re going to guess, we’re going to find out in the first quarter, and then we’re going to change it live. That’s the chess match, and that’s why I love watching this guy work.

He also gave Juszczyk a genuine shout-out for his durability, which, two days before you try to beat his team, is a classy thing to do.

And on Mike Evans: “a big target that can run the whole route tree and also win on outside lane throws.” Plus the scouting note: “they’ve moved him around quite a bit more than his whole career... definitely have to understand where he is.”

Okay. The running back room, and then the quote of the week.

On Joe Mixon, Macdonald closed the door with grace: “Just didn’t work out. Didn’t work out. Probably the most we should say about it with respect to the process... those things happen.”

That’s a man protecting another man’s medical privacy, and good for him. I said earlier this week I wasn’t going to speculate about what they saw. Neither will he.

On Robert Henry Jr.: “someone that we had a solid evaluation with throughout the process of him coming out.” And the scouting report: “he’s got good vision. He’s got good burst, got good acceleration. A lot of a lot of good stuff.”

Then a reporter brought up the Texas A&M game — and the real numbers are better than the question.

On August 30th, 2025, Robert Henry Jr. ran for 177 yards and two touchdowns against Texas A&M at over eleven yards a carry — including a 75-yard touchdown on the very first play of the second half. Texas A&M won 42-24, and their head coach Mike Elko spent the next week calling his run defense “very, very correctable.”

That’s an SEC defense. From a kid at UTSA who went undrafted.

Macdonald’s answer about whether that matters wandered a bit — “it’s a part of it... the evaluations for players just varies based on the player” — and then he landed on the best sentence of the entire press conference:

“I’m not going to bet against John evaluating runners.”

Sit with that. A head coach, on the record, forty-eight hours before a first-place game, publicly staking his own credibility on his general manager’s eye for running backs — in the exact same week that general manager said no to a two-time Pro Bowler with 7,400 career rushing yards and yes to a junior college kid with zero NFL carries.

That’s not a throwaway. That’s a head coach telling the entire league: the board is the board, and I trust it.

And the receipt is standing in his own backfield. Emanuel Wilson. Undrafted free agent. Lead running back for the defending Super Bowl champions.

Now the tactical reveal, which I don’t think got enough attention.

A reporter asked whether using Rashid Shaheed out of the backfield was just a reaction to being thin at running back. And Macdonald said no — and the answer was better than the question:

“We’ve been practicing with him at halfback really since Brian got here.”

Since Brian Fleury arrived. All of training camp. Macdonald confirmed it Monday too: “We’ve had that in all camp.”

And then he explained the philosophy, which is the sharpest football thought in the whole session: “part of our running game is you want people, non-halfbacks, carrying the ball. So there’s more things to have to defend.”

That’s not a gimmick born of desperation. That’s a design principle. If your receiver can carry it, your tight end can catch a screen, and your safety plays three positions, then a defense cannot declare anything before the snap. Every personnel group means four different things.

He was honest that Price’s injury accelerated it — “because JD is a fast guy that can get on the perimeter” — and when asked if there’s more of an itch now to see what those plays can do, he said simply: “Probably.”

The numbers: Shaheed has two carries for thirty-three yards and a touchdown against the Chargers, three carries for thirty-seven on the season, plus seven catches for seventy-three yards, nine punt returns for seventy-nine, and ten kickoff returns for 273. Shaheed’s own line on it: “It’s a lot harder for guys to key on what we have planned.” And Sam Darnold compared his flexibility to Nick Emmanwori’s — a guy you can put anywhere.

One honest caveat straight from the staff: Brian Fleury is managing his workload, because a carry out of the backfield carries more collision risk than a play out wide. So they’re choosing the specific plays that limit it. That’s a coordinator protecting a man he just extended.

And finally, the two answers that tell you who this coach is.

On the run blocking: “I think our guys are playing hard. I think we’ve been more connected in terms of our targets and how we want to play better than we were at this point last year.” Then he immediately disciplined himself: “I just think we have to be really careful in comparing our team now to anything else. Is it expectations? Is it last year? This is a journey of this team.”

And on third down, where Seattle has been poor, the most Macdonald sentence ever spoken: “Okay, we’ve fallen short right now on our third down run-pass. Okay, well, let’s attack the process.”

Okay, well, let’s attack the process. No excuse. No deflection. He has a first-round running back on injured reserve and another on PUP, and he could blame all of it. He didn’t mention it.

Watch list, four things.

One: the first screen pass of the game, either direction. Seattle is first in the league in screen EPA and thirtieth at defending them. San Francisco is third and also bad at it. Whoever’s alley defense shows up wins a quarter of this football game.

Two: first down. Not third down. If San Francisco is in second-and-five all day, that sack streak lives and Seattle loses. Get them to second-and-nine and everything changes.

Three: San Francisco’s personnel groupings in the first quarter, without Juszczyk. Macdonald said he’d test it and adjust. Watch how fast the adjustment comes.

Four: the penalty count. Fourteen for a franchise-record hundred and fifty yards against the Chargers, seven personal fouls. Hand a 4-0 team a hundred and fifty free yards and they will bury you with them.

Let me close.

Everything you need to know about tomorrow was in that press conference, and almost none of it was in the headlines.

Leonard Williams is playing, and he’s playing because his coach gave him Thursday off on purpose. Ty Okada debuts after four weeks out. Nick Bosa is officially out and Kyle Juszczyk is out, and Seattle is preparing for a formation it has no film of.

San Francisco hasn’t allowed a sack in four games — the first team to open that way in twenty-one years, and the fifth since 1963 — and the way you break that is not by beating Trent Williams. It’s by winning first down.

And in a game between the defending champions and the only undefeated team in the conference, with first place in the NFC West on the line, the head coach’s most enthusiastic answer of the week was about screen passes. Because he’s never been on a team that could throw one.

That’s the guy. Finds the edge nobody’s looking at, tells you about it honestly, and then says let’s attack the process.
