Problem two, and this is the one I want you to carry out of this video: Mike Macdonald does not make trades.

Mike Macdonald is the head coach. John Schneider is the general manager. Trades, draft picks, cap allocation, contract negotiations, who’s on the fifty-three — that is Schneider’s desk. Macdonald’s job is to win games with the players he’s handed.

Now, do they talk? Constantly. Does a head coach have input? Of course. But a trade is not announced by a head coach and it is not decided by one. Putting “MACDONALD” on a trade headline is like putting the pilot’s name on the airline’s balance sheet. He’s the most visible person in the building, so his name goes on everything — and that’s exactly why you should be suspicious when it does.

Learn that split and you’ll catch half the fake news in this sport before you finish the headline. Coach talks about the team. GM moves the pieces. If a headline has a coach announcing a transaction, somebody picked the name that gets clicks, not the name that signs the paperwork.

Now — here’s why I’m still making this video, and it’s a genuine accident.

The document asks whether Seattle should trade for a high-end tight end. And when it landed in my inbox, that became a real question for the first time all season.

Because on Thursday night in Denver, Elijah Arroyo hurt his shoulder and did not finish the game.

Macdonald afterward: “He has a shoulder. We’ll see what the extent is, but he obviously wasn’t able to finish.” And on Friday: “He’s getting some imaging and testing as we speak, so no update yet.”

As I record this, we still don’t have those results.

And here’s why that matters more than a normal tight end injury. Seattle is thin at the position. And the one structural thing Brian Fleury — the first-year offensive coordinator who came to Seattle from the San Francisco 49ers, where his job was tight ends coach — actually built this offense around was two tight ends on the field together. Arroyo and A.J. Barner.

Why does that personnel group matter? Because two tight ends force a defense to respect the middle of the field. A linebacker has to honor the run and a safety has to honor the seam, and that conflict is exactly what loosens the coverage on your best receiver. Take one of those tight ends away and the bracket on Jaxon Smith-Njigba gets tighter — which is precisely what happened Thursday, when Denver held him to two catches for eleven yards in the first half.

So the document asked the right question in the right week. It just has no idea that it did. It’s arguing from July logic in an October that changed underneath it.
