Let’s do the football.

Seattle trailed 14-3 for most of this game. Denver scored both of its touchdowns in the second quarter — a four-yard run by J.K. Dobbins, and a one-yard run by R.J. Harvey. That was the entire Denver offense for the night. Fourteen points in one quarter, three points in the other four.

And here’s the part that’s going to get buried under the quarterback argument, so let me put it up front: Seattle’s defense held the Denver Broncos to one hundred and sixty-eight total yards.

A hundred and sixty-eight. In their building. Bo Nix, Courtland Sutton, Jaylen Waddle, J.K. Dobbins — all of it, held to a hundred and sixty-eight yards. The Dark Side was magnificent and it does not matter one bit this morning, which is exactly what Macdonald means when he talks about complementary football.

Then the comeback, which was genuinely thrilling and lasted about eleven minutes.

Down eleven in the fourth quarter, George Holani scored a touchdown. Then Rashid Shaheed converted the two-point conversion. And then Jason Myers kicked a field goal in the final seconds to tie the football game and force overtime.

Think about Holani for a second. The man who fumbled at the four-yard line against San Francisco — the man whose coach said “short on backs, I can’t go out there and carry the ball” — scored the touchdown that started the comeback. That’s the stay-ready thing again.

And then overtime happened, and it was over in about ninety seconds.

Now the interceptions, both of them, because the sequence matters.

The first one came late in the fourth quarter, inside the two-minute warning, with Seattle in position to take the lead. Kris Abrams-Drain made that pick. Seattle still managed to tie it after that, which tells you how good the defense was.

The second one was the overtime throw. And Macdonald’s technical read on it was specific: “I think he’s trying to throw a hole shot in Cover 2.”

Let me explain that, because it’s the most useful football in this whole video.

Cover 2 means two safeties split the deep half of the field — each one responsible for half. And between them, right down the middle-to-sideline seam, there’s a soft spot — a hole — behind the cornerback and in front of the safety. The hole shot is the throw that drops the football into that window.

It is one of the best throws in football when it works, and one of the worst decisions in football when it doesn’t, because the ball has to travel a long way and two defenders are converging on the same patch of grass. You need perfect placement and perfect timing. On third-and-seven from your own twenty-eight, in overtime, that is an enormous amount of risk for a throw you didn’t need.

Macdonald, honest as always: “We’ll look at what they were playing and how they made the play.”

But the man who threw it did not wait for the film. And Seahawks Nation, I want to read you what Sam Darnold said last night, because I have never heard a quarterback talk about himself like this.

“No way to sugarcoat it. That one sucked.”

“Can’t turn the ball over.”

And then, on the overtime interception, he used a word that no quarterback ever uses about himself:

“The other one just got greedy.”

Greedy. And he explained exactly what he meant: “I should have just gotten it down to Kupp.” And then — this is the part that got me — he defended the receiver he didn’t throw to: “Kupp ran a really good route, right under the sticks, and I feel like he would have caught it and got the first down.”

So the checkdown was there. Cooper Kupp was open, underneath, at the sticks. Seattle needed seven yards and Kupp was sitting on seven yards. And Darnold went for the hole shot instead.

That’s what “greedy” means in football. It doesn’t mean selfish. It means you had the safe profit and reached for more.

And then he said the sentence I keep replaying:

“I just think it’s BS for me to keep turning the ball over.”

And: “It’s not fair to our defense, not fair to our special teams group, not fair to the entire team.”

Twelves, that is a quarterback holding a hundred and sixty-eight-yard defensive performance in his hands and saying I wasted it. No excuses about the offensive line, which gave up five sacks last night. No excuses about the short week or the altitude or having no running backs.

It’s BS for me to keep turning the ball over.

And here’s what Macdonald added that tells you how that room works: “We have to take care of the ball. Sam knows that. He told the team that.”

He told the team that. The quarterback addressed the locker room himself.

Alright. Now the Drew Lock question, because I promised you a real answer.

Here’s who is actually driving that conversation, by name.

Mike Florio of NBC Sports, last night: “The conversation, however it’s resolved, needs to be had.” And: “At some point, the Seahawks need to ask themselves whether Lock should be the one taking the snaps.”

And Emmanuel Acho: “The Seahawks looked better with Drew Lock coming off the bench.”

Those are media voices. Not the head coach, not the general manager, not a report of any internal discussion. But they’re serious people and the question is fair, so let’s look at it properly.

The case for Drew Lock, and it’s not nothing.

In his two starts this season, Drew Lock posted a 127.3 passer rating, threw for 422 yards and four touchdowns, and Seattle went 2-0. He was, for a stretch, the highest-graded quarterback in football by Pro Football Focus for those starts. And he’s making two and a half million dollars.

And the case against Sam Darnold right now is real too, and I’m not going to soften it.

Last night: 27 of 51, 278 yards, zero touchdowns, two interceptions, a 52.6 passer rating, and five sacks taken. Fifty-one attempts and no touchdowns.

On the season: seven touchdowns and seven interceptions. Even. And since returning from the glute injury, he is 1-3 as a starter.

Those are the numbers. That’s the argument.

Now here’s the honest other side, and I think it matters more.

Look at who each man played. Drew Lock’s two starts came against New England and Arizona. Sam Darnold’s four starts since returning came against Washington, the Chargers, a 5-0 San Francisco team, and Denver on four days’ rest at altitude. Those are not the same schedule. Not close.

And the structural problems don’t change with the quarterback. The offensive line has allowed five sacks in each of the last two games. Seattle’s leading rusher is an undrafted free agent averaging about fifty yards a game. Its first-round running back is on injured reserve. Its other back has been on the PUP list all season. Seattle’s offense ranks fourth-worst in the league in expected points added — and Drew Lock would be standing behind the exact same line, handing off to the exact same backfield.

And then Florio made the point that I think is the single sharpest thing anybody said last night, and it has nothing to do with football.

If Seattle benches Sam Darnold, they are almost certainly moving on from him in 2027 — because his final-year salary is not guaranteed. You cannot bench your quarterback in October and then hand him thirty-five million dollars in February.

So understand what’s actually being proposed. This is not a lineup decision. It’s a franchise decision wearing a lineup decision’s clothes. You don’t bench Sam Darnold for a week. You bench him for good.

Florio acknowledged the other path too: “The Seahawks may ultimately decide to ride or die with Darnold for the rest of the season.”

And here’s where I land, and you can disagree with me in the comments.

Not yet. Not now, and not like this.

You do not make a franchise-altering decision about a quarterback who won you a Super Bowl nine months ago, in the middle of a six-game sample, while he’s playing behind a line giving up five sacks a game with no running game at all and his first-round running back on IR.

But I’m not going to pretend it’s closed either. Here’s the test, and it’s coming.

Zach Charbonnet’s activation deadline is October twenty-second — six days from today. And Seattle doesn’t play again for ten days. So Darnold is about to get the two things he hasn’t had all season: a real running back, and a full week to fix the timing.

If he gets a run game and still turns it over — then the conversation is real, and I’ll have it with you honestly. Until then, the man throwing the ball is not the reason this offense ranks fourth-worst in football.

Now let me give you the number that frames this entire season, and almost nobody is going to say it today.

Seattle’s three losses have come by a combined eight points.

Washington by two. San Francisco by three. Denver by three. Eight points across three football games.

And the other half of it: Seattle lost only three games the entire regular season last year. They’ve matched that in six weeks.

So which is it — a collapsing team, or a championship team losing coin flips?

Here’s my answer: it’s a team with a razor-thin margin that has fallen the wrong way three times, and the thing turning those coin flips is turnovers. Four against San Francisco. Two more last night, both in the last four minutes of regulation and overtime. You cannot win eight-point games while giving the football away in the moments that decide them.

That’s not a talent problem. That’s a four-plays-a-month problem. And four plays a month is exactly the kind of thing a team that fixed fourteen penalties down to five in one week can fix.
