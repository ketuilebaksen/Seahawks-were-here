Okay. Now let’s get into the good stuff, because the back half of this press conference is where Mike Macdonald actually taught us something.

LESSON ONE: how you build a run defense. And twelves, nobody explains this on television.

A reporter asked a genuinely great question: everybody talks about rush and coverage working together in the pass game — but what about against the run? How do the front structures and the coverage structures connect?

Macdonald called it “a good question,” and then he gave the answer in order of operations:

“I think it starts with the type of blocks that you think you’re going to get, and who’s playing those blocks, and how many double-team combinations you’re willing to live with. How many singles you’re trying to create. That’s the starting point.”

Let me break that down, because it’s a complete philosophy in three clauses.

Step one: what blocks are coming? Every run scheme has signature blocks. Wide zone gives you reach blocks. Duo gives you double teams. Power gives you a pulling guard and a down block. So before you draw anything, you ask: what is this specific offense going to try to do to my guys?

Step two: who’s playing those blocks? Not which position — which human being. Leonard Williams taking a double team is a different problem than a backup taking the same double team. And Seattle just played a whole game without Leonard Williams for the first time since 2024, so this is not theoretical for them.

Step three — and this is the one I want you to remember: “how many double-team combinations are you willing to LIVE WITH, and how many singles are you trying to CREATE.”

Twelves, that’s the entire art of run defense in one sentence.

A double team is two offensive linemen on one defender. Your guy is going to lose that. He’s supposed to lose it — his job is to lose it slowly, hold his ground, and occupy two men.

Because every double team the offense uses is one fewer blocker available for somebody else. So if you accept two double teams, you have just created two unblocked or singly-blocked defenders somewhere else on the field.

You’re not trying to win every block. You’re trying to decide WHERE you lose, so you know where you win.

That’s defensive line play as an accounting problem. And then the coverage goes on top: “and then the coverage behind it. I think it starts with your core competencies and what you hang your hat on, and then obviously you’re going to build it around how you feel like you’re being attacked and what the other team does well.”

“Your core competencies and what you hang your hat on” FIRST. Then the opponent. Not the other way around.

AND NOW the part of this answer that is the single best coaching insight of the entire season.

“Obviously your pass responsibility has to take you to your run fit, and vice versa. So if that’s at odds — as a coach, that’s stuff you have to be really vigilant about, to make sure your guys aren’t going ‘I got this gap, I got that guy.’ That’s a problem.”

“I got this gap, I got that guy. That’s a problem.”

Twelves, stop and appreciate what he just described, because it’s the hidden reason defenses break down.

Every defender has two jobs on every snap. A run job — a gap to fill. And a pass job — a man or a zone to cover. And he doesn’t know which one applies until after the ball is snapped.

So imagine the call puts a linebacker’s run gap on the LEFT side of the formation, and his pass coverage responsibility on a receiver to the RIGHT.

Now that man has a split second to diagnose run or pass — and if he guesses wrong, he’s running the wrong direction entirely. Not a step late. Thirty feet out of position.

That’s what “at odds” means. And Macdonald says it’s the COACH’S job to never put a player in that spot.

Your pass responsibility should take you toward your run fit. They should point the same direction. So that even if the player diagnoses it wrong for half a beat, his first step is still useful.

And twelves — THIS is why Seattle’s defense held Kansas City to three points and Arizona to three points. It’s not exotic blitzes. It’s that nobody on that field is being asked to be in two places at once.

And when he said “I need to be... very articulate” — and the room laughed — that’s a head coach joking about how hard it is to explain this, which honestly, same.

LESSON TWO: the dynamic kickoff — and Macdonald just explained why the play you skip to grab a snack is one of the most valuable in football.

Asked how teams have adjusted to the dynamic kickoff:

“Yeah, there’s less explosive returns, as we’re getting more used to the play.”

And then, carefully: “I don’t know if Jay would agree with me on this, but the rule about having another returner — I’m eager to see what the stats are at the end of the season in terms of what that let us do in terms of safety. It feels like it’s being effective. I don’t have the data to back that up, but...”

A head coach giving an opinion and immediately flagging that it’s an opinion, not data. I’ll take that over false certainty every single time.

And then he said this about the play itself: “it’s a good play. I think it’s been evolving at probably a higher rate than you might imagine. It’s been an exciting play. Kudos to all the people involved that got it back in the game.”

“Got it back in the game.” Because the old kickoff had become a dead play — touchback after touchback after touchback. The new rules brought the return back to life.

BUT HERE IS THE PART THAT MATTERS, and this is a real number:

“I mean, there might not be as explosive plays. But there’s a huge difference now — if you can get a kick stopped inside the 25 yard line versus a touchback, or coming out past the 40. Man, we’re talking almost like a POINT, you know? If you can get a stop. Which is great.”

“Almost like a point.”

Twelves, that is a head coach quoting expected points value out loud, and I want to make sure you understand it.

Field position has a dollar value. Every yard line on the field is worth a specific number of expected points for the team that has the ball. A drive starting at the twenty is worth less than a drive starting at the forty. The math on that is well established.

And the gap between “stopped inside the 25” and “out past the 40” is worth roughly a point of expected scoring.

Now multiply that by four, five, six kickoffs a game.

That’s four to six points of hidden margin, decided entirely by whether your kickoff team can tackle.

And Macdonald’s phrase for it: “a kind of high leverage type of play.”

And twelves, this is not abstract for Seattle. Julian Love told us a “cost-of-doing-business penalty on that kickoff” against the Bears gave Chicago the ball near midfield — and the very next play was the forty-yard screen that Love said killed the game.

One kickoff penalty. One play. Game over.

And the reporter’s setup was the warning: THE RAIDERS ARE REALLY GOOD AT GETTING KICKOFF RETURN YARDS.

So watch Seattle’s kickoff coverage on Sunday. That unit might be worth more than a possession.

LESSON THREE: Jadarian Price — and the role is becoming official.

Asked what he liked about JD’s role last week — he caught a big third-down pass on the first scoring drive:

“Yeah, I thought JD looked good. Something that we’ll still continue to look at. He had that skill set coming out of college, and having Zach becoming healthier at halfback definitely helps us be able to do that. And he has vision, he has burst. And it’s exciting. I think he’s going to really help us.”

Two things in there matter.

First: “something we’ll still continue to look at.” That means last week wasn’t an accident — it was a test, and it passed. Expect more of Jadarian Price in the passing game.

Second, and this is the strategic key: “having Zach becoming healthier at halfback definitely helps us be able to do that.”

Twelves, that’s the whole unlock.

You cannot use a running back as a receiving weapon if he’s also your only running back. If Jadarian Price has to carry the ball twenty times, he can’t also be the guy you motion out and run routes with — he’d be exhausted and the defense would know it was him every time.

But if Zach Charbonnet is healthy and running the way he ran on Sunday — falling forward, breaking arm tackles, closing out a four-minute drive from the one yard line — then Price gets freed up. He becomes a specialist instead of a workhorse.

And remember Cooper Kupp’s word for him: “electric.” And “a slash runner. He’s decisive, obviously has great hands.”

And Macdonald’s two words here are “vision” and “burst.” Vision is seeing the hole. Burst is getting through it before it closes. Put those with great hands and you have a matchup problem — a back a linebacker can’t cover and a safety can’t tackle.

Zach Charbonnet getting healthy didn’t just fix the run game. It activated Jadarian Price.

LESSON FOUR: the Klint Kubiak game — and Macdonald named the trap before anyone could fall into it.

He was asked how similar Las Vegas looks to what Kubiak ran in Seattle:

“There’s a lot of parallels. To their credit, they’ve evolved. They’re building it around the people that they have, and they’re doing it at a high level. So I respect how they operate. They’ve got great people and good players, and they’re moving the ball and putting up a lot of points.”

“Moving the ball and putting up a lot of points.” That’s a warning, not a courtesy.

And then the exchange I love. A reporter asked whether you have to guard against overthinking it — “okay, he knows I think this way, so...”

And Macdonald jumped in: “Sure. Yeah. Yeah, for sure. GET IN YOUR OWN WAY. Yeah.”

Four words, and he killed the whole problem.

Because that spiral is real. He knows I pressure on third down, so he expects pressure, so I’ll drop instead — but he knows I know he knows, so maybe I should pressure after all. And by the time you’ve run that loop three times, you’ve thrown out everything your players are actually good at in order to win an argument inside your own head.

His actual plan: “really focusing on our details, making sure we’re aligned and on the same page and executing at a high level — and then go into the game and make the best adjustments we possibly can as the game starts to unfold.”

Details. Alignment. Execution. Adjust live. No chess match.

And on the rookie quarterback with eight NFL passes — Fernando Mendoza — his approach was the same: “you want to use the information available to you, but you can’t put all your eggs in one basket on what’s going to happen, or how you think the quarterback’s going to play. That wouldn’t be wise.”

And asked if they’d study his Cal and Indiana tape: a flat “no.” They’ll lean on “our information coming out of the draft process” instead — the scouting file Seattle built on him before he was ever drafted.

And the smartest point of all: “you can’t make wholesale changes fundamentally and systematically to what you’re doing.” Meaning the Raiders didn’t rebuild their offense for a rookie. They tweaked it. So Seattle isn’t preparing for a mystery quarterback — they’re preparing for Klint Kubiak’s offense, which they know intimately, with a rookie running it.

His verdict on the week: “it does shift our focus in terms of how we’re trying to game plan a little bit. But our process really remains the same, honestly.”

LESSON FIVE: Nick Emmanwori on tight ends — and Macdonald threw a shot at the officiating with a smile.

“Some, not all tight ends run the routes equally. They run different route trees, in terms of timing of plays and how they get open. Nick has the ability — he can run with anybody, as we know — but the ability to stay CONNECTED at like the top of routes.”

“The top of the route” is the break point — where the receiver stops going straight and changes direction. That’s where separation is won or lost. Everything before it is just running.

And then: “the best tight ends are able to use physicality to create separation. And Nick’s able to either fight fire with fire on that front, or be able to absorb — WE WON’T CALL THEM PUSH-OFFS, but, you know, I HAVE MY OWN OPINION ABOUT IT — where you can absorb some of those tricks of the trade, so to speak, and still stay connected.”

“We won’t call them push-offs, but I have my own opinion about it.”

[That’s a head coach accusing NFL tight ends of uncalled offensive pass interference, in public, without technically saying it.]

And he’s right, twelves. A 250-pound tight end puts a hand on a defender’s hip at the top of the route and shoves. It happens constantly. It’s almost never called.

So covering a tight end requires one of two things: get physical first, or be strong enough that the shove doesn’t move you.

Nick Emmanwori — six foot three, 227 pounds, with a perfect 10.00 Relative Athletic Score — can do both. Which is why Seattle took the blitz away from him last week, put him on Trey McBride, and watched the ball not go there on third down after third down until he broke up the fourth-down throw at the goal line.

And “another good tight end obviously this week” tells you the plan is the same Sunday.

NOW — the human part, and this is what I’ll actually remember from this press conference.

When Klint Kubiak left for the Raiders, Seattle had to fight to keep the rest of that offensive staff. And Macdonald described it like this:

“Well, it was very important. We wanted to keep as many of our offensive staff as we possibly could. JB was right there with the rest of the guys. Obviously there’s some dynamics that go down when something like that happens, that is inevitable to some degree.”

And then — the line of the week:

“But my message to our coaches is like: hey, WE HIRED YOU TO BE SEAHAWKS, MAN. Like, we want you to be Seahawks.”

“We hired you to be Seahawks.”

Twelves, that is the coaching version of what this team keeps telling its players.

Because here’s what happens when a coordinator gets a head coaching job: he calls his favorite assistants and offers them promotions and raises. It’s a raid. The best staffs get stripped the moment they succeed.

And Macdonald’s answer wasn’t to match offers or to beg. It was to remind them who they already were.

“I know the staff dynamic was going to change, but everyone did a great job, and you could feel that we were building a cool thing on our staff even though we were going to lose at least Klint.”

And on JB specifically: “JB does an awesome job. He really, really does. We’re on record with that. But it was obviously really important to try to keep him. I think JB WANTED to be here, which is a positive. But obviously you want to be able to work it out where it’s best for everybody as well.”

“I think JB wanted to be here.”

And then came the sentence that tells you Mike Macdonald’s entire philosophy about people:

“I was confident he was. I mean, if people don’t want to be here, we’re not going to force them to be here.”

And then he referenced the famous Mike Tomlin line — the one about wanting volunteers, not hostages.

“We want guys that want to be here. And if they do, then we want to make it work where they’re excited to be here too.”

Twelves, that’s the exact same thing this team says about players. “I’m on the seventy.” “The power of our seventy.” “The ready squad.” And now “we hired you to be Seahawks.”

It’s one message, applied to everybody in the building.

And then he admitted the hardest part of the job, and I respect him enormously for saying it out loud:

“You can’t — I think the challenge I found was like, hey, we only have X amount of pie. We only have X amount of responsibilities. How do you feel like you can best reward people for doing a great job while also...”

“We only have X amount of pie.”

That’s the real problem with running a successful organization and nobody ever says it. There is one offensive coordinator job. One quarterbacks coach job. One play-caller. And if ten of your assistants are doing great work, you cannot promote ten people.

So your reward for building a great staff is that other teams take it from you.

And the earlier answer about collaboration is how he fights that: “a lot of stuff we’re running is not starting in my head — it’s starting in our staff’s head.” And: “when you’re working at your best, everyone’s contributing and everyone feels like they have a say in what we’re doing and how we’re doing it.”

If you can’t give a man a bigger title, give him real ownership of the thing. That’s the pie he can actually eat.

And one last note on why that matters on Sunday: Macdonald described Klint Kubiak as a man who “can tell you the truth and go THROUGH, not around — without sugarcoating it, but keeping the personal emotions out of it.”

That’s the man on the other sideline. Direct, honest, unemotional, and now running an offense that’s putting up a lot of points.
