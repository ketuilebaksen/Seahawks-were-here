“We’re gonna run wide zone against everybody.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s the surprise move. Seven words, from Mike Macdonald, on Wednesday.

And the reason it’s a surprise is what came right before it. A reporter pointed out that the Kansas City Chiefs have “kind of killed outside zone defensively” — and that outside zone, wide zone, is Seattle’s major. It’s the thing this offense is built on.

So the man was handed a perfect excuse to say we’ll adjust, we’ll show them something different, we’ve got wrinkles. Every coach says that. It costs nothing.

Instead he said: we’re going to run our stuff. Against everybody. Including them.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here, and this is the best press conference Mike Macdonald has given all year. He said something about Patrick Mahomes’s knee that nobody else noticed, and something about Kenneth Walker I don’t think he planned to say.

Two quick corrections first, because I’d rather fix them than have your comments do it.

One: this is Week 7, not Week 5. Seattle plays Kansas City on Sunday night, October twenty-fifth. Week 5 was San Francisco, two games ago. A wrong week number in a title tells people the video is old before they press play.

Two: Macdonald didn’t announce a transaction at that podium. But there IS real news from Wednesday, and it’s bigger than a signing:

Zach Charbonnet logged a full practice. Full participation. And today — Thursday, October twenty-second — is his deadline.

And the other side of it: Drake Thomas did not practice. Macdonald confirmed it on camera, as flatly as he says anything: “Drake’s not going to practice today. No.”

Hold onto that one. It matters more than anything else in this video.

Now — the wide zone declaration, because this is the actual story.

Let me make sure you understand what’s being discussed, because “outside zone” gets said constantly and almost nobody explains it.

In a zone run, the offensive line all moves in the same direction and blocks an area, not a man. Everybody flows laterally, the defenders get carried sideways with them, and somewhere in that moving wall a seam opens. The back’s job is to run parallel to the line, read it, and make one hard cut upfield the instant he sees daylight. It’s a timing play and a vision play — not a “blow you off the ball” play.

The opposite is a gap run, or man run — pull a guard, kick out an end, create one specific hole by force, and hit it. That’s power, decided at the point of attack.

Seattle majors in wide zone. It’s their identity — what Fleury came from in San Francisco, what the Shanahan tree is built on.

And the reporter’s point was sharp and correct: Kansas City defends outside zone extremely well, and Seattle has actually had more success this season with its man runs — which aren’t their major.

So here’s Macdonald’s answer, and I want you to hear the whole thing:

“Our philosophy is we always want to keep it within our wheelhouse, and then we’ll kind of target within the wheelhouse towards our opponent.”

And then: “From really reviewing our run game this weekend, I think we can still run wide zone at a high level. It’s a little bit more — there’s a little bit more variance than you probably want, but it does kind of come with the territory to a certain extent.”

And then the line: “We’re gonna run wide zone against everybody.”

That is a coaching philosophy stated in one paragraph.

“Keep it within our wheelhouse, then target within the wheelhouse.” Meaning: we are not installing a new offense on a Wednesday to beat one opponent. We run what we’re best at, and we choose which of those things based on who we’re playing.

And the honesty in the middle is the part I respect: “a little bit more variance than you probably want.” That’s him admitting wide zone is boom-or-bust right now — some runs hit for twelve, some get stuffed for one — and that it’s the cost of doing business while your blocking gets healthy.

You know what fixes variance in a zone run game? A right guard who’s been out for five games. Which brings us to the rest of the week.

Now, Kenneth Walker. And I have to give you the number, because it’s absurd.

Kenneth Walker the Third has 655 rushing yards, six rushing touchdowns and two receiving touchdowns through five games with Kansas City. Against the Chargers on Sunday he ran for 128 yards and two scores in a 22-6 win. He has scored a rushing touchdown in every game but one.

And then two historical markers that I had to read twice.

He is the third player this millennium with at least 750 scrimmage yards and eight touchdowns through five games.

And this one: he is the first player since Jim Brown in 1963 with at least 600 rushing yards, six rushing touchdowns, and multiple receiving touchdowns through five games.

Jim Brown. Nineteen sixty-three.

Andy Reid’s review of him: “He’s a stud.”

And Macdonald’s, when asked about the start: “Off to a tremendous start. Stats back it up. Tape definitely backs it up. He’s playing great football.”

And then somebody asked the real question — whether he’s had second thoughts or what-ifs about Walker leaving. And this is the most human thing Macdonald has said all season.

“I’ll just say, like — I love Ken. I thoroughly enjoyed him being on our football team. We’ve had some great one-on-ones. I feel like we have a great relationship. I respect the heck out of him. I’m happy for him.”

And then: “Obviously we have to do a great job of stopping him this week. But I think there’s mixed emotions, in terms of like — you’re happy for him, but also, hey, we’re trying to figure out ways to stop this guy, because he’s playing at a really high level.”

A reporter also brought up that the two of them went to Disneyland together after the Super Bowl. The head coach and the Super Bowl MVP, at Disneyland in February — and on Sunday night the coach has to scheme up a way to tackle him. That’s not a storyline somebody wrote. That’s just how this sport works.

Now here’s the tactical part, and Macdonald explained it himself without being pushed: Kansas City is using Walker differently than Seattle did. They’re under center.

A reporter noted the Chiefs are under center more this year than last, and more effectively. Macdonald confirmed it: “Probably more frequently than they have in the past.” And then: “They’re actually blocking it well under center. Ken’s seen it great, and he’s run at a high level.”

“Ken’s seen it great.” That’s coach-speak for his vision has been excellent — which is the single most important trait in a zone runner.

So why does under center help a running back? This is the hidden story of Walker’s season.

Out of the shotgun, the back stands beside the quarterback, roughly level with him. The handoff happens almost at the line of scrimmage, and the back has to start laterally — moving sideways before he’s moving forward.

Under center, the back starts seven yards deep, already running toward the line when the ball arrives. He gets a downhill runway. He hits the hole with momentum instead of building it, and he has more time to read the blocking develop, because he’s farther from it when the play starts.

For a one-cut, decisive, violent runner — which is exactly what Kenneth Walker is — that depth is everything. Kansas City didn’t just sign a good running back. They put him in the formation that fits him.

And Macdonald’s nuance on it was excellent: “I think it’s a methods principle thing. There’s been really effective teams that have run out of the gun... certain runs are probably better out of certain sets.” And then the key: “You move people and that provides limitations on things that you can and can’t do.”

Every formation gives you something and takes something away. That’s the whole art of it.

Okay. So how good are the Chiefs? Here are the real numbers, and they’re serious.

Kansas City is 5-0. Entering Week 6, they ranked fourth in the NFL in offensive EPA per play — their best mark since 2022 — and second in EPA per rush, their best this decade. Their defense is allowing 16.3 points per game.

For context on the arc: from 2018 to 2022 the Chiefs ranked first in offensive EPA per play. From 2023 to 2025 they ranked tenth. They’re climbing back.

Macdonald called them “the most explosive offense in the league.” I’ll be straight with you — the metrics have them fourth in offensive EPA per play, not first, and coaches always talk opponents up. But second in the league in EPA per rush is the number that should actually worry you, because that’s the exact thing Seattle has to stop.

His full read: “They’re playing really complimentary football. They’re playing team football. They’re staying ahead of the sticks. They’re being very efficient.” And: “Very few penalties, very few takeaways.”

“Staying ahead of the sticks” means avoiding third-and-long — manufacturing second-and-five instead of second-and-nine, which is where offenses live or die. And “very few takeaways” is pointed, because Seattle has nine turnovers, tied for fourth-most in the league. He’s describing the opposite of his own team.

Now the thing nobody else noticed, and it’s my favorite moment in the whole press conference.

Asked whether Mahomes looks different since the injury, Macdonald said: “If you didn’t know, it’d be hard to tell off the tape that he’s dealt with what he’s dealt with, which is impressive.”

And then: “He’s kind of like Zach in terms of the time[line].”

Stop there. He just compared Patrick Mahomes to Zach Charbonnet.

And he’s right, because Patrick Mahomes tore an ACL last season. Kansas City was 6-7 when it happened and they missed the playoffs. This year he’s posting his highest yards per attempt and passer rating since 2022 and he’s on pace for 32 touchdown passes, his most since 2022.

So on Sunday night you are going to watch two men come back from the same torn ligament — one of them Seattle’s running back, the other the opposing quarterback. Macdonald made that connection out loud in half a sentence and moved on.

And then he said something that is genuinely elite film study, and I want to make sure you catch it:

“It’s probably physically tougher on the QB to work from under center, so that speaks to his health.”

Let me unpack that. Under center, a quarterback has to turn his back to the defense, carry out a fake, drop backward under his own power, and plant. There’s torque through the knee on every turn — far more than catching a shotgun snap and standing there.

So when Kansas City uses Mahomes under center more than they ever have, one year off an ACL, that tells you the knee is fine. Macdonald is reading a man’s medical status through his formation usage. That’s what watching tape for a living looks like.

His verdict: “He’s very decisive on early downs, getting the ball out of his hands, executing their RPO game at a high level.” And: “Looks like the Pat Mahomes to me.”

And there’s one more Chiefs development that’s real news: Eric Bieniemy is back as their offensive coordinator.

He held that exact job in Kansas City from 2018 to 2022. Since then: Commanders OC in 2023, UCLA’s OC in 2024, and the Bears’ running backs coach in 2025. Andy Reid called him an “ultimate team guy” who “brings great energy.”

Macdonald on that pairing: “Have a lot of respect for Coach Bieniemy. He’s a great football coach. Andy Reid’s obviously probably the best there is.” And: “It’s quite a tag team.”

And a detail worth sitting with: a former running backs coach came back to run that offense, and the running game is now second in the league in EPA per rush. Draw your own line.

Now Steve Spagnuolo, and the best scouting observation of the week.

A reporter pointed out that Kansas City’s pressure rate and their sack numbers don’t match. And Macdonald’s answer is the most useful thing in the transcript:

“When you turn the tape on there’s a lot of pressures and a lot of things... you can mistake it by saying that they’re just kind of throwing paint against the wall. But I think really the opposite is true, on how targeted he is.”

“Throwing paint against the wall” versus “targeted.” That’s the entire difference between a bad blitz defense and a great one.

A paint-against-the-wall defense sends five or six men a lot and hopes volume works. A targeted defense sends pressure at a specific protection weakness, on a specific down, against a specific formation — and it looks chaotic on television precisely because it isn’t.

And his description of the unit: “They’re very aggressive. He does a great job game planning. His team plays on one accord.” And: “They definitely have Chiefs fingerprints on their tape, which is a compliment to them.”

On screens, which Seattle has genuinely struggled with, he gave the funniest answer of the day.

Andy Reid is the king of the screen game. Seattle has been one of the worst teams in football at defending them. And Macdonald said:

“We looked at the screens over the weekend. There’s a couple big plays that distort the — it’s like every old-school defensive coordinator is like, ‘take out all the bad plays, we playing great defense, you know.’”

He’s making fun of himself for wanting to throw out the bad snaps. Then he gets serious: “Offenses are allowed to run any play they want, and our job is to stop it... It’s a team play, not any individual player — and we got to do a great job identifying it and getting everybody to the party.”

“Getting everybody to the party.” That’s the actual answer to a screen. A screen beats you when one man overpursues and nobody else arrives. It’s a swarm problem, not a one-on-one problem.

And on run defense, somebody asked a deliberately basic question and got the best answer of the day:

“Fundamentals, man. Blocking and tackling, man.”

Then the detail: “The through line is just fundamentals — block destruction, how you’re going to play blocks, how you’re going to leverage the football. It’s not just the front. You see the ball gets out — it’s either a missed tackle, or multiple missed tackles, or a bad leverage or a bad angle by the third-level player. So it’s got to be team defense.”

“The third level.” Linebackers and safeties. Which is exactly why Drake Thomas not practicing is the most important fact in this video. Walker’s damage comes from the second and third level — he gets past the line clean and makes a safety wrong. Seattle’s thirty-five game streak without allowing a hundred-yard rusher is defended at the third level, and the third level is short-handed.

On the offensive line, he refused to single anybody out and then said something sharper than a complaint:

“You could fill in the blank for our football team right now. The effort is there. The attitude is there. Intent’s there. But there’s room for growth.” And what he wants specifically: “decisiveness and targeting, and then going to let it rip, and trusting our guys’ ability — because we got some good players.”

And then the leadership moment. Asked where his mind goes when a player who’s been good suddenly isn’t:

“As a coach, our natural inclination is that we can do something about it. We take a lot of pride in being able to get the best out of our players. So if we’re not achieving that, the first thing we’re going to do is look at what we’re doing, what we can do better to help the player out.”

And the line I’d put on a wall: “Instead of just ‘you got to play better,’ it’s like — well, how do we help you play better? What are the things that we can do that are under our control?”

That is a head coach taking the first swing at himself. Every single time.

And finally — the actual surprise move, which happened Monday and just got confirmed Wednesday.

A reporter noted that Seattle has been in two-back personnel fewer than ten plays over the last couple of games — and that Robbie Ouzts hasn’t been available this year.

Macdonald: “I wouldn’t discount it, that’s something that we’re not going to go back to. So that’s definitely still on the table — being in 21 and having Brady be out there. Brady’s played good football for us.”

“21 personnel” means two backs, one tight end. And Brady Russell is the fullback who’s been filling in.

So put it together. On Monday Seattle signed a fullback — Jackson Acker, an undrafted rookie out of Wisconsin — and a blocking tight end, Josiah Deguara. On Wednesday the head coach said two-back sets are back on the table. That’s your surprise move: Seattle is going heavier, on purpose, against the best running team in football.

And the rest of the week’s board: Charbonnet logged a full practice Wednesday and his deadline is today — fifty-three-man roster or PUP for the season. Anthony Bradford is back practicing, with Macdonald saying he’d play “a significant amount, if not the whole game” after a good week. Elijah Arroyo is headed to injured reserve, out at least four games. Jadarian Price isn’t eligible until Chicago on November second.

Let me close on the quote that ties it all together.

Asked how he messages an offense to start fast without pressing, Macdonald said this:

“You can’t control starting fast. You can control your mentality, how well you’re on your assignments, how well we’re aligned, what we’re going to call and when.”

You can’t control the outcome. You can control the preparation. That’s the whole philosophy of this coaching staff in two sentences, and it’s why the answer to a 3-3 start was never going to be a trade.
