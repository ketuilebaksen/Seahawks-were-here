The situation: fourth-and-ten from the San Francisco 50. Fifty-four seconds left. Seattle down 32-29.

Sam Darnold throws to Cooper Kupp. Kupp breaks one tackle and dives for the marker while Malik Mustapha pushes him toward the sideline. Officials rule him short. The play goes to replay review. The call stands. Turnover on downs, San Francisco runs out the clock, game over.

Now here is the rule, and I want you to actually learn this one, because I’d bet money most of this fan base doesn’t know it — I didn’t know it precisely until I went and read the league’s explanation.

Mark Butterworth is the NFL’s Vice President of Instant Replay. He gave a pool report on this exact play, and here’s what he said:

For the line to gain, the spot is determined by where the FOOTBALL crosses the plane from inbounds to out of bounds — not where the player’s body lands.

His words: “It’s not where his body part lands out of bounds, it’s where the ball breaks the plane from inbounds to out of bounds.”

So being in the air doesn’t help you. The moment that football crosses the vertical plane running up the sideline, that is your spot, no matter where your body ends up afterward. Once the ball crosses that plane, it is out of bounds and the play is over right there.

And now the part that trips absolutely everybody up, including me:

The goal line is a completely different rule.

At the goal line, the end zone extends. You can be flying out of bounds and reach the ball across the plane and it’s a touchdown. You’ve seen it a hundred times.

The line to gain does not extend. It’s a flat spot on the field, and the ball has to be past it while still inbounds.

Two different rules. They look absolutely identical on your television. And that’s the real reason this feels wrong to so many of you — your brain is applying the goal-line rule to a first-down play, because that’s the highlight you’ve seen your whole life.

And they did review it. Properly. Butterworth: “There was nothing clear and obvious to overturn the call on the field.” They stopped the game and went through every available angle including the boundary-line cameras, and he said they could not find an angle showing Kupp clearly had the football past the line.

And one more thing people don’t realize about replay: it only has two outcomes. His words: “We only have stands or overturned, so we stood with the ruling on the field.” There’s no “we’re not sure, give it to them.” If the video doesn’t overturn it, the field call survives.

So the version going around saying this play “deserved a closer examination” — twelves, it got one. The game stopped. Every camera. The league office in New York. The examination happened and it produced nothing.

But here’s the criticism of that play that IS completely fair, and I haven’t heard a single person make it.

It was fourth-and-ten. And the pass was thrown short of the line to gain.

Read that again. On the final play of the football game, needing ten yards, Seattle completed a pass that didn’t reach the sticks and asked Cooper Kupp to make up the difference after the catch against a closing safety.

That’s the thing to be upset about. Not the spot. The spot was the symptom. The disease was a play design that required a thirty-three-year-old receiver to win a footrace to a line he caught the ball behind.
