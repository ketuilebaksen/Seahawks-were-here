Mike Macdonald had six excuses available to him on Monday night. He used none of them.

Seahawks Nation, look at what this man could have said after losing 34-17 at home. He had a left tackle who wasn’t his starter. He had a Hall of Fame guard who’d been in the building six days. He had a right guard playing a brand-new position. He had a kicker whose field goal got blocked. He had an interception that was tipped at the line of scrimmage. And he had a six-day week staring at him.

Every single one of those is true. He mentioned not one of them.

Here’s what he actually said:

“I think you just — it’s a tip of the cap to the Bears. They won the game. They won it fair and square. They won it in all three phases. And we just got beat. And no other way to sugarcoat it.”

“No other way to sugarcoat it.”

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Chicago 34, Seattle 17. The Bears are 6-2. Seattle is 4-4. And I’m going to walk you through this press conference, because there’s one word in it that explains the entire loss — and there’s an injury update at the end that’s genuinely good news.

But first, the title, because I know why half of you clicked.

There is no quarterback decision. Mike Macdonald did not bench Sam Darnold. He did not hint at it. Nobody asked him to.

Here is literally everything he said about his quarterback, in full:

“I think we want to keep encouraging Sam to use his legs and use his athleticism, his playmaking ability. But — can we design it better and coach up better and be more prepared so we can throw it on time? Sure.”

That’s it. That’s the whole thing.

And twelves, read it carefully, because it’s a vote of confidence with a coaching note folded inside it. Keep encouraging Sam to use his legs. That’s not a man looking for a replacement. And then: can WE design it better, can WE coach it up better, so HE can throw it on time?

He put the fix on the staff, not the player. Again.

For the record: Sam Darnold went seventeen of thirty-four for 188 yards with two touchdowns and an interception that was tipped at the line and caught by James Lynch. Seattle allowed three sacks and Chicago generated a thirty-one percent pressure rate, per Next Gen Stats. That’s not a quarterback crisis. That’s a pocket crisis.

Now the press conference proper — and the order he put things in tells you who he is.

“It’s a team effort. We lost as a team. I think in all three phases, we can have better plans, we can coach it better, we can prepare our players better, and we can play better.”

Count the order. Better plans. Coach it better. Prepare the players better. And only then — play better.

Three coaching failures listed before one player failure. That’s the fourth or fifth time this season I’ve watched this man put the staff in front of the roster in public. It’s not a performance. It’s just how he talks.

And he refused to hide behind the stat sheet either: “You look at all the stats, the things that happened in terms of playing complementary football — we can rattle them off if you want, but they made it happen and we didn’t.”

“We can rattle them off if you want.” Translation: I know the numbers are ugly, I’m not going to hide inside them.

And then the most unusual thing a head coach said all night.

Asked a question about what happened to the offense after a good week, he said:

“Well, I’m not sure yet. I mean, we came off of last week feeling good, and then obviously we have some change up on our offensive line. So I expect us to get back and get better, but I don’t — I don’t have a great answer for you right now.”

“I don’t have a great answer for you right now.”

Twelves, that is forty minutes after a seventeen-point home loss in prime time. Most coaches fill that silence with something. He said he didn’t know yet.

And notice what he did and didn’t do with the line change. He named it — “obviously we have some change up on our offensive line” — and then refused to use it. He mentioned the fact and declined the excuse in the same breath. That’s a very specific kind of discipline.

He followed it with the message he gave the team: “This is what we do. We find solutions and we move forward. We stay together and go make it happen from there.”

Now the word. The one that explains the whole night.

A reporter pointed out what everybody watching noticed: the offense had a hard time getting anybody involved besides Jaxon Smith-Njigba. Macdonald’s answer:

“We didn’t do the things on offense that we need to be able to do to play Seahawk football. So to me it’s the same — we got similar questions throughout the beginning of the season on where we’re falling short. But to me, it’s inputs. If we’re playing the way that we’re supposed to, and we’re running off the rock, and we’re targeted right, and we’re dialed in on what we should be doing — we’re way more efficient. And then the ball is distributed more evenly. So that’s where we need to put our focus.”

“To me, it’s inputs.”

And then: “the ball is distributed more evenly.”

Twelves, I need you to understand how smart that answer is, because it’s the opposite of what fans demand after a game like that.

The complaint is: get somebody else the ball. More Cooper Kupp. More tight ends. More Charbonnet in the passing game. Jaxon Smith-Njigba had six of Seattle’s catches for 118 of its 188 passing yards. One man was most of the offense.

And Macdonald is telling you that is a symptom, not the disease.

You don’t fix concentrated distribution by targeting other people. You fix it by being on schedule — “running off the rock,” being “targeted right,” being “dialed in” — because when you’re in first-and-ten and second-and-five, every option is live. When you’re in third-and-nine eleven times, there’s exactly one guy who can save you, and everybody in the stadium knows his name.

The ball went to JSN because the situations only allowed JSN.

And here’s the part that made my jaw drop when I lined up the week’s transcripts.

Aden Durde spent his Friday press conference praising Chicago for precisely this. His words about the Bears: “They do a great job of moving the ball around the field, getting the ball in different people’s hands, and making you work all the time.”

Monday night: Caleb Williams threw to seven different receivers. Burden seven catches, Loveland seven catches, Odunze four. Nobody over ninety yards. Nobody to double.

Seattle’s defensive coordinator admired Chicago’s distribution on Friday. Seattle’s head coach diagnosed his own team’s lack of it on Monday. Same quality. Opposite sides of the scoreboard.

Now the diagnosis that three different Seahawks gave in the same week — and it’s the actual story of this loss.

Macdonald: “If we keep forcing ourselves into — if we’re not efficient on early downs and we’re not moving the ball forward all the time, it’s tough sledding. And when you play a team like that that is really proficient on third down, then that’s the type of stuff that happens.”

Now put that next to two things said four days earlier.

Sam Darnold, Friday: “You’re converting first downs and you’re going first, second, first down, staying out of those third downs... we need to be more efficient on those first and second downs so we can just stay out of third downs completely.”

Aden Durde, Friday, about Chicago: “They’re efficient on early downs and on third down.”

The quarterback named the standard. The defensive coordinator said the opponent met it. The head coach said Seattle didn’t.

Final number: Seattle went one for eleven on third down.

Three men. One diagnosis. Eleven third downs. One conversion. This is not a mystery, and nobody in that building is pretending it is.

And Macdonald added the urgency: “The sense of urgency for us to improve right now is high.” And earlier: “The margins in our league are too small. When you don’t do the things that it takes to win, that’s how a game like that shapes up.”

Now the running back answer, which is quietly a little sad.

Asked whether the running back rotation went as he envisioned:

“Well, without getting into the play counts and all that — you can’t ask guys to come back and play a certain amount of snaps. Obviously the game shook out a certain way, and that affected how we played our guys.”

Translate that.

Zach Charbonnet came back from a torn ACL — surgery February twentieth — with a specific, managed plan for how many snaps and what types of plays. Macdonald told us about it on Friday: “we have a plan for how much and what types of plays he’s going to have.”

And then Seattle fell behind 20-9 and had to start throwing.

You can’t run a carefully rationed carry count when you’re chasing points. The game script took it away from him. A man waited eight months for Monday night and the scoreboard spent his snaps for him.

Now special teams — and this is genuinely new information that nobody else is reporting properly.

A reporter noted Seattle is normally very good on special teams but had a handful of gaps. Macdonald:

“Again, it was a team effort. We lost in all three phases. We knew the timing of the kickoffs would be different on our kick returns, and it just didn’t seem like we could get the return timed up with the blocks. And that’s how they make their hay, and they’re good at it.”

Read that again: “We knew the timing of the kickoffs would be different.”

They saw it coming. They prepared for it. And they still couldn’t solve it.

Chicago deliberately varies kickoff timing — hang time, placement, tempo — to break the timing between a returner and his blockers. Seattle’s return unit never got synchronized. And on top of that: Jason Myers missed an extra point and had a 33-yard field goal blocked by Chicago’s Austin Booker.

That’s the third phase, and it’s four points and a bunch of bad starting field position in a game Seattle got within three.

“That’s how they make their hay, and they’re good at it.” Credit, again, to the opponent. Fourth time in one press conference.

And the single most specific failure he named:

Asked about a sequence involving a delay-of-game penalty that should have helped Seattle, he said:

“There’s some unfortunate series of events that didn’t go our way. But you’ve got to play the game. You’ve got to be able to tackle guys on the perimeter, and understand the situation, and execute. They did that and we didn’t. So it’s pretty cut and dry.”

“You’ve got to be able to tackle guys on the perimeter.”

That’s the one concrete, fixable thing in the entire press conference. Perimeter tackling — corners, safeties and linebackers making open-field tackles on the edge. Remember Cooper Kupp two weeks ago: “every yard matters.” And remember Emmanwori describing the Kansas City defense as “a swarm.”

On Monday night the swarm didn’t arrive on the edges, and Chicago turned short completions into first downs. That’s how a quarterback goes twenty-four of thirty-four for 247 yards without a single explosive play — and still wins by seventeen.

Now the forward-looking news, and this is the part that actually matters for Sunday.

Seattle is in its first six-day week of the season.

Macdonald: “We’ve got a short week. This is the first of, I think, maybe four or five throughout the season. So it’s really important that we get our bodies back and get our minds ready to go on Wednesday.”

And his plan: “We have a plan. We haven’t had a six-day week yet this year. We had one last year, and we’ll go try to execute it to the best of our ability. But similar to going into a Thursday night game — you don’t have a lot of time. This isn’t time for studying. It’s just: hey, let’s attack the tape, let’s make sure we’re all on the same page, on one accord, when we show up on Wednesday. And let’s go.”

And then the line of the night: “We don’t have time to be licking our wounds or anything like that. So it’s time to move forward.”

“We don’t have time to be licking our wounds.”

Twelves, a six-day week after a blowout is either a curse or a mercy. Macdonald is treating it as a mercy. No time to marinate, no time for a week of talk radio to get into the locker room. Attack the tape, same page, Wednesday, go.

And here is the injury news, which is better than I expected.

From the podium: “Leo had a stinger, and we’ll see how that shakes out.” That’s Leonard Williams. And: “Connor — a foot.” That’s Connor O’Toole. Plus: “To my knowledge, the running backs came out of the game okay.”

And then the follow-up update changed the week.

Macdonald said Leonard Williams has “a chance to come back this week.” And he gave Charles Cross, Uchenna Nwosu and Rashid Shaheed the same outlook — all four have a chance to play against Arizona. With the caveat: “Nothing definitive this early in the week.”

Twelves, Charles Cross possibly coming back is the most important sentence anybody said after this game.

Seattle rushed for eighty-one yards and allowed three sacks with Josh Jones at left tackle. If Cross is back on Sunday, then Quenton Nelson, Grey Zabel and Charles Cross get to play together for the first time — and the thing I’ve been arguing for two weeks finally gets tested properly: is this line a problem, or was it just incomplete?

Add Shaheed back at receiver and Leonard Williams back inside, and Sunday’s team could look meaningfully different from Monday’s.

And one more voice, because somebody in that locker room said exactly the right thing.

Julian Love — the safety who told us Quenton Nelson was “the best player I ever played with” — spoke up after the loss:

“People need to step up, guys need to get together here, and just play better football together.”

And he added something I thought was wise: a lesser team would turn on itself after a loss like that, and Seattle’s players are still connected and still chasing efficiency.

“Guys need to get together here.” Not somebody needs to be fired. Not it’s the coaches. Get together.

Remember who’s been carrying that message all week. Macdonald: “we stay together and go make it happen.” Durde: “it’s about being connected, playing fast, and understanding how each other play.” Cooper Kupp, two weeks ago: “they really love one another.”

The same word keeps coming out of that building: together. At 4-4, after a blowout, that’s the only asset that can’t be traded for.

Now the title, and here’s my verdict.

“Shocking QB decision” is not in this press conference. There is no decision. There is one answer about Sam Darnold and it’s supportive. Nobody got benched, nobody got called out, and the head coach aimed the criticism at his own coaching staff.

“After blowout loss” — that part is dead accurate, and I’m not going to soften it. Seattle lost by seventeen at home in prime time, allowed its most points of the season, went one for eleven on third down, and ended a thirty-game streak of never losing by more than four. That’s a blowout.

But what Macdonald actually gave us is more useful than a fake quarterback story: a one-word diagnosis — inputs — that matches what his quarterback and his defensive coordinator both said four days earlier, and an injury report that could put three starters back on the field by Sunday.

Let me close.

Seattle hosts the Arizona Cardinals in Week 9, on a six-day week, with the trade deadline on November tenth sitting right behind it.

Here’s where I am. This team is 4-4 and it has lost three of four. The margin for error is gone. But the diagnosis is consistent, specific and fixable — early downs, third down, perimeter tackling, red zone, and a left tackle who might be back. Nobody in that building is confused about what’s broken. They just have six days to fix it.

And they are not getting a trade to save them. Indianapolis wants a McCaffrey-sized haul for Jonathan Taylor and Seattle isn’t on the list of inquiring teams anymore. The answer is the room they have.

Twelves, two questions. First: do you buy “it’s inputs”? I do. One for eleven on third down is not a receiving-distribution problem — it’s a first-and-second-down problem, and all three of Macdonald, Darnold and Durde said so in the same week. Tell me if you think that’s a coach deflecting or a coach diagnosing.

And second, the one that decides the season: if Charles Cross plays Sunday and the run game still doesn’t work, what do you conclude? Because that’s the test. No more excuses on that line after this week.
