I told you Seahawks 24, Bears 20. It was Bears 34, Seahawks 17.

I was wrong, twelves. Not a little wrong — wrong by twenty-four points. And before I say one word about anybody else’s performance, I’m going to tell you exactly what I missed.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Chicago 34, Seattle 17, at Lumen Field, on Monday Night Football, in front of you. The Bears are 6-2. Seattle is 4-4. Let’s go through all of it, honestly.

First, the fact that explains how strange this loss was: the streak lived.

D’Andre Swift carried the ball twenty-seven times for sixty-nine yards.

Twenty-seven carries. Sixty-nine yards. Two point six yards a carry.

Seattle has now gone thirty-seven consecutive games without allowing a hundred-yard rusher. The run defense did its job. It did its job brilliantly. Chicago’s lead back needed twenty-seven attempts to get sixty-nine yards.

And Seattle lost by seventeen.

Because D’Andre Swift scored three touchdowns. One yard, five yards, and five yards.

Twelves, read those distances. Seattle’s run defense won every single yard on the field except the ones that counted. Three short runs. Three times Chicago got inside the ten and three times they walked in. That’s not a run-defense failure. That’s a red-zone defense failure, and it’s the exact thing Mike Macdonald listed, out loud, after the Kansas City win: “procedural stuff, red zone stuff, negative plays, how we rush, our red zone defense.”

He told us what wasn’t fixed. It wasn’t fixed.

Now the number that actually decided this football game, and it’s brutal.

Seattle went one for eleven on third down.

One. Of eleven.

Eight days ago, against the Kansas City Chiefs, Seattle went eleven of sixteen — the most third-down conversions in a single game in franchise history since 1991, when play-by-play tracking began.

Eleven of sixteen. Then one of eleven.

And here’s what makes it worse, and I have to say it: Sam Darnold explained this exact failure mode on Friday, in his own words. He said the recipe for a healthy offense is “first, second, first down, staying out of those third downs” — and that Seattle needed to “be more efficient on those first and second downs so we can just stay out of third downs completely.”

Last night they faced eleven third downs and converted one. They didn’t stay out of them and they didn’t convert them. Both halves of his own checklist failed in the same game.

And the totals are ugly: 252 total yards. Eighty-one rushing yards.

Eighty-one.

Against a Chicago defense ranked twenty-sixth in run defense DVOA that allows 4.4 yards a carry. Eight days after Seattle ran forty-one times for 153 yards against a far better front.

And twelves, that’s the part I got most wrong, so let me own it properly.

My entire prediction was built on one matchup: Seattle’s rediscovered run game against the worst run defense they’d see all month. I said it three videos in a row. It was the single most confident thing I told you.

It produced eighty-one yards.

Here’s what I under-weighted. I knew Seattle was starting three new offensive line spots — Josh Jones at left tackle with Charles Cross out, Quenton Nelson at left guard after six days in the building, and Grey Zabel at right guard for the first time in his professional life. I said it was a risk. And then I predicted a win anyway, because I’d fallen in love with the Chicago run-defense ranking.

A rebuilt offensive line does not beat a top-five pressure defense on five days of practice. Macdonald himself warned us: he called it “a process” they had to “attack the heck out of,” and said they’d have to “figure out some solutions as the game goes on going against a really good team.”

He hedged. I didn’t. That’s my mistake, and it’s the right lesson: when a coach tells you something is a process, believe him over the DVOA table.

Now Chicago’s opening drive, because it set the tone for everything.

Seventeen plays. Eighty yards. Eight minutes and thirty-two seconds. It ended with Caleb Williams throwing a one-yard touchdown to Luther Burden, which was Burden’s fourth catch on that single drive.

Twelves, do you see what happened there? Chicago took Seattle’s own blueprint and used it first.

Everything we celebrated eight days ago — long drives, possession football, keeping the other offense on the bench — Ben Johnson did it to us in the first eight and a half minutes. Seattle’s defense stood on that field for a third of a quarter before it ever caught its breath, and the offense started the night cold on the sideline.

And Chicago’s defense answered: they held Seattle to three first downs on its first five possessions.

Three first downs. Five possessions. Macdonald wanted to “stack” off Kansas City. Seattle never got started.

Now Jeffery Simmons, and I want to be fair to a man who warned us.

On Friday, Sam Darnold said this about Simmons: “He’s powerful. He’s fast. He does all the things that you want an interior D-lineman to do... they can put him out wide as well, and try to get a matchup with a tackle, a tight end or a back. So he definitely creates a lot of problems up front.”

Last night: one sack, one pass defensed, six tackles — tied for the team lead. And five of those tackles came in the first half, which is remarkable for a defensive tackle. He sacked Darnold on Chicago’s first defensive possession of the game.

Chicago gave up two first-round picks and a fourth for Jeffery Simmons, and he won them a prime-time road game in his third week with the team.

And Seattle allowed three sacks — after allowing zero against Steve Spagnuolo the week before.

Sam Darnold told us precisely what was coming, and it came.

Now the kicking, because this is where a seventeen-point loss hides a three-point game.

Jason Myers made a 27-yard field goal. Then he missed the extra point after Jaxon Smith-Njigba’s touchdown. Then, early in the fourth quarter, his 33-yard field goal attempt was blocked — deflected by Chicago’s Austin Booker.

Add it up: a missed extra point and a blocked 33-yarder is four points left on the grass. In a game where Seattle got within three points in the fourth quarter.

I’m not going to pin a seventeen-point loss on a kicker. But I’m also not going to pretend those plays didn’t matter, because they absolutely did.

And now the part that made the building shake, because for about four minutes this was a real football game.

Chicago led 20-9 in the fourth quarter. Then:

DeMarcus Lawrence forced a fumble by Colston Loveland. Seattle recovered. Sam Darnold threw a seven-yard touchdown to Cooper Kupp. And Seattle went for two — and Darnold converted it with a pass to Tory Horton.

20-17. Fourth quarter. Lumen Field at full volume. Defending champions one score away.

And then Sam Darnold threw an interception, and D’Andre Swift ran it in from five yards out, and then he did it again.

34-17 with 4:41 left.

That’s the game. Seattle clawed back from eleven down to three, and the single play that ended it was a turnover. Which — and I’m not enjoying this — is the exact thing I told you would beat us: “The thing that beats us is pressure and turnovers.”

I identified the right risk and then picked against it anyway. Doing the analysis and then ignoring it is worse than not doing it.

Two bright spots, and they’re real.

One: Tory Horton caught the two-point conversion.

I told you to watch him. With Rashid Shaheed out, I said somebody would get fifteen snaps in prime time and either do something with them or not. Tory Horton’s name is on the scoring summary of a Monday night game. A 2025 fifth-round pick out of Colorado State converted a two-point play with the season on the line. Small thing. Real thing. Remember the name.

Two: Jaxon Smith-Njigba passed Lance Alworth, and he did it in style.

He needed four yards to move into second place for most receiving yards through seven games in the Super Bowl era, past Alworth’s 871 from 1968.

He got forty-three — and a touchdown. A 43-yard score from Darnold with thirty seconds left in the first half.

That was the one prediction I got exactly right, and it was the easiest one on the board.

Now Caleb Williams, because he was excellent and I’m not going to shortchange him.

Twenty-four of thirty-four. 247 passing yards. One touchdown. A 101.0 passer rating. Zero turnovers. One sack taken.

Twelves, I told you Seattle allows the fewest explosive passes in the NFL and that this was the antidote to a quarterback coming off back-to-back 300-yard games.

I was right about the explosives and it didn’t matter. He didn’t throw for 300. He threw for 247 and never made a mistake. Rome Odunze: four catches, 87 yards. Colston Loveland: seven catches, 65 yards. Luther Burden: seven catches, 56 yards and the opening touchdown.

Chicago didn’t beat Seattle over the top. They beat Seattle underneath, seventeen plays at a time. That is a quarterback and a coaching staff executing a plan, and it’s the most impressive thing either team did all night.

And here’s the streak that actually ended last night, and it stings.

Seattle had gone thirty straight games without losing by more than four points — the fourth-longest such streak in the Super Bowl era.

Thirty games of never getting blown out. That ended on Monday night at home.

For two weeks I’ve been telling you Seattle’s three losses were by a combined eight points, and that it meant this team was closer than the record looked. I can’t say that anymore. This one wasn’t close, and the honest version is: the defending Super Bowl champions got outplayed at home in prime time.

One more injury: Leonard Williams left in the second half with a stinger and did not return. We’ll need an update on that this week, and he’s one of the few men on that defense you genuinely cannot replace.

And on the debuts — I’m going to be careful here, because I don’t have their individual snap data yet.

Zach Charbonnet played in his first game since tearing his ACL in the divisional round. Quenton Nelson made his Seahawks debut at left guard. Josh Jones started at left tackle.

What I can tell you is the team result: eighty-one rushing yards, three sacks allowed, and one-of-eleven on third down. What I can’t tell you yet is how much of that belongs to which man. When the snap counts and the grades come out, I’ll give you the real breakdown — not a guess dressed up as analysis. Check back.

So where does this leave us? 4-4. And eight days from the trade deadline.

Seattle hosts Arizona on Sunday. The deadline is November tenth.

And twelves, I know what the comments are going to say: you said don’t trade for Jonathan Taylor, and we just rushed for eighty-one yards.

Fair. So let me answer it straight, with the numbers that came out this week.

Indianapolis was offered a 2027 second-round pick plus another pick for Taylor, and per Tom Pelissero they rejected it immediately. Pelissero reported they “won’t deal him for anything other than a premium haul” — a package comparable to what Carolina got for Christian McCaffrey in 2022, which was a second, a third, a fourth and a fifth. And trading him costs the Colts about $17.6 million in dead money, which is why the price is that high. Seattle isn’t even on Schefter’s latest list of inquiring teams.

And my answer hasn’t changed, because last night didn’t change the diagnosis.

Seattle rushed for eighty-one yards with a left tackle who isn’t a starter, a left guard who’d been in the building six days, and a right guard playing a new position. You do not fix that by adding a fourth running back. You fix it by getting Charles Cross back and letting three men who have never played next to each other play next to each other for a month.

If the problem is the line, the running back is the wrong purchase. That was true at 4-3 and it’s true at 4-4.

But I’ll concede this much: if Seattle loses to Arizona, that argument gets a lot harder to make, and the conversation stops being about running backs and starts being about whether this is a championship team at all.

Let me close.

Eight days ago this channel told you Seattle was a Super Bowl defense with a franchise-record third-down day and a receiver in the company of Jerry Rice. Last night they went one for eleven on third down, gained 252 yards and lost by seventeen at home.

Both of those are the same football team. That’s the league.

And here’s what I owe you: I was confident and I was wrong. I’m not going to delete the pick, I’m not going to pretend I hedged, and I’m not going to come back next week pretending I called it. I built a prediction on one matchup and ignored the warning that the head coach and the quarterback both gave me in public.

The defense held a starting running back to 2.6 yards a carry and kept the streak alive at thirty-seven games. The offense couldn’t stay on the field. Seattle is 4-4, and the next three weeks decide the season.

Twelves, two questions. First, the real one: what’s actually broken — the offensive line, the red zone, or third down? I say third down, and I say it’s downstream of pass protection. And second: does 4-4 change what you want at the deadline? I still say keep the picks and get Cross healthy. Tell me why I’m wrong — and this week, you’ve earned the right to.
