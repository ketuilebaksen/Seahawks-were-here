Seven turnovers.

Seven. The most in any football game in the National Football League this season.

And here is the entire afternoon in one sentence, Seahawks Nation: San Francisco scored twenty-one points off Seattle’s four turnovers. Seattle scored fourteen off San Francisco’s three.

That’s a seven-point swing in a game Seattle lost by three.

Forty-niners thirty-two, Seahawks twenty-nine. Eddy Pineiro, forty-four yards, one minute and fifty-six seconds left. Seattle is 3-2. San Francisco is 5-0 and alone in first place in the NFC West.

And Seattle led this football game 26-21 in the third quarter.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here, and this is the hard one. Mike Macdonald’s postgame was honest in a way that’s going to stick with me, and we’re going to go through all of it. The sentence he used to describe his own team. The ejection nobody saw coming. The one thing that genuinely got fixed. And the four days — four — until the next one. Let’s go.

Start with the sentence, because he said it in the first forty seconds and it’s the whole video:

“I think our guys played hard. We stuck together. I think we got a tough team. I think we’re a connected team. But in terms of playing complementary football the way that we want to play here as Seahawks, and become the team that we want to become — we’re not there yet.”

We’re not there yet.

That’s a Super Bowl-winning head coach, five games into a title defense, saying out loud that his football team has not become the thing it’s trying to be. Not “we had a tough day.” Not “the ball didn’t bounce our way.”

We’re not there yet.

And then he named it, with no cushion: “We need to grow quickly in terms of complementary football. Definitely taking care of the football. Way too many giveaways at this point in the season.”

Way too many giveaways at this point in the season. That’s the diagnosis, and the numbers back it completely.

So let me walk you through how a football team loses while outplaying nobody and getting outgained by eighteen yards.

Four Seattle turnovers.

Number one: Renardo Green intercepted Sam Darnold, and that set up a San Francisco touchdown drive. Eighteen yards. They didn’t earn it, they were handed it.

Number two: a Seattle fumble that Malik Mustapha recovered at Seattle’s own 11-yard line. Christian McCaffrey ran it in from eleven yards out. An eleven-yard touchdown drive.

Number three is the one that’ll be in your head all week. Eighteen seconds left in the first half. Seattle has the football at the San Francisco four-yard line. They run a stretch play. George Holani gets stripped by Gracen Halton, and Halton recovers it in the end zone for a San Francisco touchdown.

Think about the math on that single snap. Seattle is at the four. Worst case, you kick a field goal and it’s 21-16. Best case you score and it’s 21-20. Instead it’s 21-13 going into halftime, and San Francisco gets points on a play where Seattle’s offense was the one inside the red zone.

That’s a fourteen-point swing on one handoff.

And number four: Darnold threw his second interception to Renardo Green on Seattle’s opening drive of the second half, setting up a Pineiro field goal attempt — which he missed, and that mercy is the only reason this game stayed close. Emanuel Wilson also lost a fumble on the day.

Now, Macdonald got asked directly about that end-of-half stretch play — why run it there, with San Francisco holding three timeouts — and his answer was the best coaching answer of the night:

“We should be able to execute run plays.”

Then he explained the thinking, and it’s sound: “We want to stay aggressive. I mean, he kicks a touchback, we’re two plays away from field goal range and we’re in good shape. So we want to stay aggressive and get points in the end of the half, and the opportunity to get the ball coming out of the second half.”

And then the landing: “But we got to be able to execute our run game and hold on to the football. Straight up.”

Straight up. No scheme excuse. No “we’ll look at the call.” He defended the decision and condemned the execution, which is exactly the right split — because he’s right. Handing the ball off from the four-yard line with eighteen seconds left is not a reckless call. It’s a normal call. You just have to hold the football.

And listen to what he did not do, because this is the part I want you to notice.

A reporter asked whether George Holani should have come out of the game after the fumble, the way Jadarian Price did after the Washington game. And Macdonald’s answer:

“No. I mean, short on backs, you know. I can’t go out there and carry the ball, so nor do we want to.”

That’s a joke at his own expense, four minutes after losing a first-place game. And underneath the joke is the truth about this roster: Jadarian Price is on injured reserve, Zach Charbonnet is still on the PUP list, and the man who fumbled is one of three healthy running backs. You can’t bench a third of your position group.

He also refused to hide behind the turnovers when it came to his quarterback, and this was my favorite exchange of the whole session.

Asked what he says to Darnold on the sideline, he gave the most human answer available: “It’s not like he doesn’t know. We don’t want to throw interceptions, you know. So stay positive, stick to it, trust your reads, take care of the ball.”

It’s not like he doesn’t know. There’s a whole philosophy of coaching in that one line. The quarterback who just threw two picks does not need a lecture about not throwing picks. He needs somebody to keep him upright.

And then Macdonald volunteered the defense nobody asked for: “Sam also did a lot of great things today, extending plays, so you don’t want to take that away from him either.”

Darnold’s line: 18 of 29, 195 yards, one touchdown, two interceptions — and he got sacked repeatedly, on a night when the two teams combined for eight sacks.

And when he was later asked flat out whether this is a quarterback problem, he closed the door on it:

“We’re not panicking or anything like that... if we become more efficient and more effective as an offense, that’s going to help our quarterback too. I think it’s everybody. It’s not just Sam. Sam knows — it’s not like he wants turnovers. We need to just play collectively more efficient. I think when that happens, then the ball doesn’t become in jeopardy as much.”

“It’s everybody. It’s not just Sam.” That’s a head coach standing in front of a microphone after four turnovers and taking the blame off the one man everybody wants to blame. And he’s right, and here’s the proof:

Seattle went three-for-twelve on third down. Twenty-five percent. San Francisco went four-for-twelve. Both teams finished 7-of-24 on the day, which is the ugliest combined third-down number you’ll see all year.

Macdonald’s own breakdown of why: “We got to play better. The protection and the routes and the timing of the play — that needs to be better. We can’t be in second-and-long. We can’t be in third-and-long. We got to keep the ball moving forward. Got to make smart decisions with the football. Got to take care of the ball.”

And then the admission that ties this whole month together: “Too frequently early in games, we’re not doing that.”

I told you before kickoff that everything on this football team comes back to first down. That’s the head coach saying the same thing in his own words, four hours later, after a loss.

Okay. Now the thing that actually went right, because it’s real and it’s being completely buried.

Seattle committed five penalties for 46 yards.

Five. Forty-six yards.

Seven days ago it was fourteen penalties for a franchise-record 150 yards, including seven personal fouls. Four veterans — Jarran Reed, Leonard Williams, Julian Love and Ernest Jones the Fourth — called a players-only meeting about it on Monday with no coaches in the room.

Fourteen to five. A hundred and fifty yards to forty-six. San Francisco had more penalties than Seattle did — eight for seventy.

The meeting worked. In one week. And it got tested in the hardest possible way, which brings me to the strangest thing that happened all afternoon.

Dre Greenlaw got ejected from this football game for head-butting Ernest Jones the Fourth.

Midway through the second quarter. There was a hit on Sam Darnold. Ernest Jones — EJ — was the first man there to get in Greenlaw’s face on behalf of his quarterback. And Greenlaw threw his head forward into Jones’ head.

Here’s the part that makes this historically interesting: officials did not throw a flag for the headbutt. They flagged the hit on Darnold, and that was it. Then, a minute or two later, they announced Greenlaw was disqualified.

Why? Because of a brand-new rule the competition committee approved this year, which allows league personnel to consult with the on-field officials on disqualifications for non-football acts even when nothing was called on the field.

So a man got thrown out of an NFL game by a review process that did not exist last season, in Seattle, in the second quarter of a first-place game. And for the record, Fox’s broadcast caught Greenlaw mimicking the headbutt as he walked off — he’d given one to his own teammate Fred Warner moments earlier. This is also a man who was ejected in 2023 over the Philadelphia security incident, and who was suspended a game last season for chasing a referee after a game.

But the reason I’m spending time on this isn’t Greenlaw. It’s Ernest Jones.

He got headbutted in the face, in front of eighteen thousand phones, and he did not swing back.

And Macdonald’s answer about it was the proudest he sounded all night:

“Bro, that was crazy. I don’t know. I’m actually proud of our guys. You talk about chasing edges — I thought we played the line today, and then they took care of it, given all the things that were going on.”

We played the line, and then they took care of it. That’s a defensive coach saying: we played right up to the edge of legal, we didn’t cross it, and the officials handled the guy who did.

And then on Jones specifically: “EJ led the charge. I know it was an emotional thing, but we got to stay in the pocket too, for us to play our best ball. I thought our guys did a great job, honestly — coming off of last week and understanding how we want to operate.”

And the sentence that is the actual lesson of this whole week: “It just shows you — if we put our attention to things, our guys get better. We get it. We attack it.”

Twelves, think about what happened there. Last Sunday this defense gave away a franchise record in penalty yards, including a head butt of its own from Nick Emmanwori. Four captains called a meeting. Seven days later, their own captain got head-butted by an opponent and kept his hands down — and the opponent got ejected instead.

That is a culture fix, executed in a week, under maximum provocation. They fixed last week’s problem and then lost to a completely different one.

Which is exactly what makes this loss so frustrating and, honestly, so fixable.

Now, the other things worth knowing from that podium.

Byron Murphy the Second — Macdonald called him “Murf” and lit up: “You felt Murf today. Happy for him... Murf played a really good game.” And it mattered: Seattle sacked Brock Purdy twice, with Ernest Jones and Byron Murphy getting one each.

Which means Seattle ended it. San Francisco walked in without having allowed a single sack in four games — the first team to open a season that way in twenty-one years. Seattle put him on the ground twice. The streak died at Lumen Field, and the two men who killed it were a linebacker and a defensive tackle.

Nehemiah Pritchett intercepted Brock Purdy — and Macdonald’s praise of him was the warmest of the night: “Nehemiah played a great game, man. Probably the best play was a special teams play. Guy’s playing double duty — he’s a gunner and a corner for us. Guy should be really proud of himself, the position he’s put himself in. He’s playing really good football.”

And on how he got there: “The third corner spot was going to be something that needed to be competed for throughout the offseason, and he didn’t shy away from it and came every day and did a great job and just stuck to the process and just kept making plays. He’s earned a role for us, which is great for him and it’s good for us.”

He’s earned a role. Same theme as all week. Stay ready.

Ty Okada played in his season debut: “Good to have Ty back. Felt like we were on point.” Honest about the film: “I got to watch it to see how Ty played, but I felt like we did some good things.”

Mike Evans was the difference on the other side — seven catches, 113 yards, a touchdown and the two-point conversion that put San Francisco up 29-26. Macdonald, asked if those were just great individual plays: “Yeah. Great individual plays by him.” And when a reporter suggested Josh Jobe was left on an island out there, Macdonald gave a one-word answer: “No.”

On the pass rush fading in the fourth quarter, he wouldn’t guess: “I’m not sure. You got to give them credit. I think they got to some solutions. I thought their quarterback made some plays. Probably a better answer for you tomorrow. But you’re probably right.”

Probably a better answer for you tomorrow. A head coach refusing to invent an explanation twenty minutes after a game. I’ll take that over confident nonsense every single time.

And on the final play — fourth-and-ten, Cooper Kupp diving for the sticks, ruled one yard short by Malik Mustapha, replay upholding it — he gave the shortest answer of the night: “I don’t know. They said he was short, so got to live with it.”

Got to live with it. That’s it. No conspiracy, no complaint.

Let me give you the rest of the ledger, because there’s real good buried in here.

Jason Myers made five field goals: 49, 50, 43, 48 and 40 yards. Five. He was perfect on a cold night and he is single-handedly the reason Seattle was in position to win. Jaxon Smith-Njigba had five catches for 102 yards and a touchdown — though he also dropped a third-down pass at the San Francisco 25 with 54 seconds left. Emanuel Wilson carried it twenty times for 61 yards and a two-yard touchdown. And Seattle’s defense held the most efficient offense in football to 189 passing yards and 269 total.

So nobody tell you this was a blowout or a collapse. Seattle lost by three to a 5-0 team while giving away the football four times, and still had the ball at midfield with 54 seconds left.

And the locker room message, which is where I’ll leave you:

“We got a tough, connected, great group. We got the capacity to be a really good football team. But right now we’re not doing the things to become a great team. So let’s go do those things. Let’s go through it. Let’s attack it. Let’s figure out ways to get better, and let’s do it in a hurry — because we play pretty soon.”

Let’s do it in a hurry.

Because here’s the schedule, and it’s brutal: Seattle plays at Denver on Thursday night. Four days. A short week, on the road, at altitude, after a divisional loss.

Macdonald found the only silver lining available: “Good news is we have a game in four days that we can move forward and learn from and try to grow from.”

And one injury note: he opened by saying there were no updates except for Drake, who had an ankle but finished the game. And on Charbonnet for Thursday: “The plan right now isn’t for him to play, so that could change, but as of right now, he’s not going to play.”

Which puts the October twenty-second activation deadline in very sharp focus. Thursday’s out. That leaves one more week.

So where are we? 3-2. Second in the division. Five games into a title defense, with a head coach who stood up and said we’re not there yet.

And here’s my honest read, and I’ve sat with it.

This is not a bad football team. This is a good football team that has not played one complete game yet. The penalties got fixed in a week — fourteen to five, by the players, under provocation. The pass rush ended a historic streak. Myers made five field goals. JSN went over a hundred. Pritchett picked off Brock Purdy.

And they gave the ball away four times and lost by three.

You fix turnovers the same way they just fixed penalties. You name it, you attack it, and you don’t wait. Which is precisely what the man said: let’s do it in a hurry.

Go through, not around.
