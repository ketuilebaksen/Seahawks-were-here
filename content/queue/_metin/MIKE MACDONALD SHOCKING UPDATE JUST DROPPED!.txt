First — the correction, because I owe you this.

Last video I told you Seattle waived rookie defensive tackle Devin Eastern and left themselves an open roster spot.

That’s not the full story. He was released on November sixteenth AND RE-SIGNED ONE DAY LATER.

He’s still here. I reported the first half of a two-step move and I should have waited for the second half. My mistake, and I’m telling you about it instead of quietly moving on.

And twelves, what actually happened makes a lot more sense now — this was a paperwork maneuver, not a judgment on the player.

Here’s his actual timeline. Seventh-round pick, number TWO HUNDRED FORTY-SECOND overall in the 2026 draft. On the practice squad. Called up before the Week 9 Arizona game to add depth AFTER THE LEONARD WILLIAMS INJURY. Released November sixteenth. Re-signed November seventeenth.

One day. Off and back on.

And the real move underneath it is this: Seattle CUT QUARTERBACK ADRIAN MARTINEZ on November seventeenth — twelve days after signing him on November fourth.

So the sequence is: open a spot, make the quarterback move, bring the defensive tackle back. That’s a roster shuffle, not a demotion.

NOW — why the Adrian Martinez cut is the most meaningful news of the week.

Let me remind you why he was here in the first place, because I covered this signing when it happened.

Seattle’s quarterback room on November fourth was: Sam Darnold out with a cracked rib and a quad. Drew Lock playing through elbow tendinitis AND an oblique. And behind them, Jalen Milroe — a developmental quarterback who had never thrown a pass in an NFL game.

The math was brutal: if both Sam and Drew went down, Milroe would have been the ONLY quarterback on the roster.

Martinez was insurance. Plain and simple. I said exactly that at the time: “a UFL MVP on the practice squad as insurance.”

And now he’s gone.

And twelves, this read is exactly right: “cutting a quarterback tells me the team FEELS GOOD about Sam and Drew. If we were worried, THAT INSURANCE POLICY DOESN’T LEAVE.”

That’s it. That’s the whole signal.

You don’t release your emergency quarterback in the middle of a playoff race while your top two are still hurt. You release him when your top two aren’t hurt anymore.

That move is a medical update disguised as a transaction.

And a quick word about Adrian Martinez, because the man deserves it. At Nebraska in 2021 he threw for TWO THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED SIXTY-THREE YARDS and fourteen touchdowns, and added FIVE HUNDRED TWENTY-FIVE RUSHING YARDS and thirteen more scores on the ground.

That’s over thirty-three hundred total yards in one season. Plus a Big 12 championship at Kansas State, and a UFL MVP award with the Birmingham Stallions where he LED THAT ENTIRE LEAGUE in rushing.

The NFL hasn’t been kind to him — one game played, one rushing attempt. But that’s a real football player and I hope somebody gives him another shot.

Macdonald: “SAM’S OUR STARTING QUARTERBACK.”

Twelves, four things in there and every one matters.

ONE: “Sam’s our starting quarterback.” Present tense. Not “will be.” Is.

And that’s now the THIRD time this has been established. Macdonald told a national broadcaster, the day BEFORE the Raiders game — asked if there was anything Drew Lock could do to keep the job — “NO.” And then: “IT’S SAM’S TEAM.” And after the game he said “it’s great that OUR STARTER goes down and nobody blinks an eye.”

Before the game. After the game. And now again. Same answer three times, across a game in which his backup went fourteen of seventeen for two hundred forty yards.

TWO — and this is the one nobody is talking about: “MAYBE FOR THE FIRST TIME THIS YEAR.”

Twelves, read that again.

Mike Macdonald just told you that Sam Darnold has not been fully healthy at ANY POINT this season.

Not one week. Not one game.

And when you line that up with what we already know, it tells a story. A glute injury in Week 2. Then Macdonald’s own words about the Bears game: “he cracked his rib on the SECOND PLAY of the game. Playing the whole game, WITHOUT EVEN MAKING A NAME OF IT.” Plus the quad on his final drive. And Macdonald’s verdict: “I wasn’t aware of the rib throughout the game until after.”

So the eight interceptions, the eighty passer rating, the one-to-one touchdown ratio — all of it happened to a man who, by his own head coach’s account, was never once at full health.

I’m not excusing the numbers. I’ve criticized them every week and I’ll keep doing it if they continue. But “maybe for the first time this year” is important CONTEXT, and nobody should be handing out verdicts without it.

THREE: “a FULL WEEK OF WORK.”

That’s the standard, and it’s been the standard since Macdonald first announced it: “you’re going to want him to PRACTICE A SIGNIFICANT AMOUNT OF TIME for him to play.”

And twelves, here’s why that’s the right test instead of “does it hurt.”

Watch what Drew Lock has been doing. He has won games throwing on exactly ONE DAY of the practice week — twice. A reporter asked him “did you only throw on Thursday again?” and he said “Yeah.”

And Lock can do that because, in his own words: “for the last couple years, that’s how I’ve been able to get ready for games. IT’S THE ONLY WAY I’VE BEEN ABLE TO GET READY FOR GAMES.”

Eight years as a backup built him a system for preparing without reps. Sam Darnold does not have that system. He’s a starter. Starters need the week.

FOUR: “HURDLES SAM STILL NEEDS TO CLEAR.”

That’s the honest part, and it means it’s not official. And remember Macdonald also said Darnold has to be ONE HUNDRED PERCENT. Not cleared. Not good enough. A hundred.

So there’s still a version of this where Drew Lock starts in San Francisco. It just doesn’t look likely.

AND NOW — the thing I want to say about this debate, because I think people have it backwards.

The honest framing from this breakdown was great: “when Drew was called on, HE SHINED. But the coach has made his call — and honestly, getting our starter back healthy WHILE HAVING A BACKUP THIS GOOD is A NICE PROBLEM TO HAVE.”

That’s exactly right, twelves.

Most teams in this league are praying they have ONE quarterback who can win games. Seattle has two, and a third that its head coach keeps calling “really good.”

And Macdonald told us about Jalen Milroe before the Raiders game, unprompted: he’s been “TREMENDOUS” with the first team, “really, really impressed,” “VERY DECISIVE,” and “delivering the ball ACCURATELY and ON TIME.”

That’s not a courtesy. Those are the exact three things the pre-draft scouting report said he needed to fix.

And the voice that should end this argument is Drew Lock’s own, from after he won the game: “that’s the life of a backup. WE DON’T WANT TO BE OUT THERE. That’s what’s best for the team. OUR STAR NEEDS TO STAY HEALTHY. AND I LOVE SAM.”

The man people want in the job called Sam Darnold “our star.” Unprompted. While celebrating his own win.

The framing: “we’re six and four and still fighting for a playoff spot — but the front office already has REAL DECISIONS COMING. And let’s be honest, WE FELT WHAT HAPPENED LAST OFFSEASON. We lost a lot of talent and it affected how this season has gone.”

And the painful example: “would we be better off if we’d kept Boye Mafe and Kenneth Walker? ABSOLUTELY. BUT THE PRICE WAS TOO STEEP. That’s the reality.”

Twelves, I want to sit with that for a second because it’s the honest tension of running a football team.

“The price was too steep” is not a cop-out. It’s the actual job. Every dollar you spend on one player is a dollar you can’t spend on another. Overpay for a good player and you can’t afford a great one.

But you also live with the consequences. And a whole lot of Seattle’s problems this season — the thin edge rusher rotation, the running back room that had nobody healthy in September — trace back to men who left.

And the line that ties it to this team’s identity: “as Seahawks fans know, it takes SEVENTY PLAYERS to win a championship, not just a handful of stars.”

Seventy. That’s “the power of our seventy” — Macdonald’s phrase, which Drew Lock repeated unprompted as “the READY SQUAD, it’s seventy guys ready to roll at any time,” and which Macdonald used again about Bobby Hart after the Raiders game: “that’s another POWER OF THE 70 THING.”

So here are the four men Seattle has to keep. And twelves, I have opinions on all four.

NUMBER ONE: QUENTON NELSON.

“This one’s simple. The trade ONLY WORKS if we re-sign him to a MULTI-YEAR EXTENSION. He’s THIRTY YEARS OLD and he’s missed a couple of games with a knee injury HE ALREADY HAD BEFORE WE TRADED FOR HIM. If he walks, WE GAVE UP A 2027 THIRD-ROUND PICK AND A 2028 FIFTH-ROUND PICK FOR NOTHING.”

Twelves, that’s the most important sentence in this whole video.

Quenton Nelson is on an EXPIRING contract. He has played ONE GAME for Seattle. One. And then he needed a knee procedure to clean out cartilage.

So right now, as it stands, Seattle traded a third and a fifth for ONE GAME of football.

And the read on how John Schneider operates is correct: “he USUALLY GRABS A GUY ON AN EXPIRING DEAL WITH THE PLAN OF EXTENDING HIM once he’s in Seattle.”

That’s the whole model. You don’t pay market price in free agency — you acquire the player cheaply, get him in your building, and then extend him before anybody else can bid.

And the evaluation: “when healthy — AND HE ALMOST ALWAYS IS — Nelson is an ELITE left guard. Keep him, and the hope is that Grey Zabel becomes a good right guard next to him.”

And Macdonald’s own words on him are the argument: “he’s one of those guys that’s going to be a FORCE MULTIPLIER for our group — not just our offensive line, but really our whole offensive unit and our football team. SO IT’S WORTH THAT INITIAL INVESTMENT.”

“Worth that initial investment.” A head coach saying that out loud about a man on an expiring deal is a message to the building, not to us.

NUMBER TWO: JOSH JONES, offensive tackle. And twelves, this is the name nobody saw coming.

“This one might be tough. He was only re-signed on a ONE-YEAR, FOUR MILLION DOLLAR DEAL and he’ll be THIRTY. But look at what he’s doing filling in for Charles Cross and Abe Lucas.”

And here are the numbers, and they’re outstanding:

“HE HASN’T ALLOWED A SACK IN THE LAST THREE GAMES in place of Cross. ONLY ONE QUARTERBACK HIT. And he’s been effective blocking for the run.”

Twelves, stop and think about that.

Three games. Zero sacks allowed. One quarterback hit. Filling in for the starting left tackle. Protecting a quarterback who was playing on a sore elbow and a strained oblique.

And remember what Drew Lock said about his protection after the Raiders game: “those guys up front — I mean, TWO TACKLES THAT AREN’T OUR NORMAL STARTING TACKLES.”

And the twenty carries for ninety-four yards from Zach Charbonnet came behind that same group.

And here’s the flexibility that makes him so valuable: “Cross could return in WEEK 12 after the bye, and Lucas hurt his ankle in Week 10. IF LUCAS MISSES GAMES ONCE CROSS IS BACK, JONES JUST FLIPS TO THE RIGHT SIDE.”

That’s the definition of a swing tackle, and twelves, I want to tell you how rare that actually is.

Playing both tackle positions is genuinely hard — I covered this when Grey Zabel moved sides, and Mike Macdonald confirmed it himself: “it’s probably TOUGHER THAN ONE MIGHT JUST ASSUME going from one side to another.”

Everything mirrors. Your stance flips. Your first step reverses. Your punch hand changes. The teammate you’re combo-blocking with moves to your other shoulder.

A man who can do that at a high level on EITHER side is one of the most quietly valuable players on a roster.

And here’s the worry, and it’s completely legitimate: “keeping him as a BACKUP would be INVALUABLE. BUT PLAYING THIS WELL, ANOTHER TEAM COULD SIGN HIM AS A STARTER. Honestly, that WORRIES ME.”

Twelves, that is the cruelest math in football.

Josh Jones is playing SO well as a fill-in that he’s pricing himself out of being a fill-in. Somebody is going to offer him starter money, and Seattle can’t pay starter money to a backup.

That’s the same trap Mike Macdonald described about his own coaching staff: “we only have X AMOUNT OF PIE. We only have X amount of responsibilities. How do you best reward people for doing a great job?”

Same problem. Different department.

NUMBER THREE: ZACH CHARBONNET.

“He’s been EXCELLENT the last two weeks, and he’s proving HE DIDN’T COME BACK TOO SOON from the knee injury he suffered in the playoffs last season. He and Jadarian Price will split reps the rest of the way, but CHARBONNET SHOULD GET THE MAJORITY like he did in the win over the Raiders.”

And the question: “it’s the SAME ONE KENNETH WALKER HAD TO ANSWER last offseason. DOES CHARBONNET WANT TO GO SOMEWHERE? He’s the CLEAR RB1. Him and Price would be a FANTASTIC DUO, but THAT MIGHT ONLY LAST HALF A SEASON. I’D HATE TO LOSE HIM.”

Twelves, I’m going to be direct: PAY THAT MAN.

And here’s my case, built entirely on things we’ve watched.

He tore his ACL in January. Mike Macdonald told us before the Bears game that he “hasn’t had a live rep SINCE THE INJURY” — not one, in eight months. He came back, got held under thirty yards, and a national analyst’s word for him was “RUST.”

Three weeks later: TWENTY CARRIES, NINETY-FOUR YARDS. Nearly two hundred yards in three games. Behind an offensive line missing two starters in one of those games and three in another.

And Drew Lock’s description is the one that convinced me: “when CHARBS does his classic ‘YOU CAN’T TACKLE ME’ thing and SHOVES PEOPLE TO THE GROUND, it’s pretty fun to watch. That’s kind of like a — oh, THERE it is.” And: “the LOOK IN HIS EYE when you come back to the huddle.”

And Macdonald: “definitely felt Zach in his POWER, and the ability to BREAK TACKLES and OWN THE SIDELINE.”

Twelves, an ACL tear is the single worst injury a running back can have, because the entire job is cutting, accelerating and stopping on a dime. To be doing this eleven months later is remarkable.

And the thing Seattle has to understand: a running back’s value is not just yards. It’s what he UNLOCKS. Macdonald told us himself that “having Zach becoming HEALTHIER at halfback definitely HELPS US BE ABLE TO DO THAT” with Jadarian Price in the passing game.

Charbonnet taking twenty carries is what turns Jadarian Price into a weapon. You lose one and the other one gets worse.

NUMBER FOUR: TY OKADA, safety. And this one has a mechanism people don’t know.

“He’s a RESTRICTED FREE AGENT, so we can EASILY KEEP HIM, and Seattle has the RIGHT OF FIRST REFUSAL. He’s due for a raise, though. ANOTHER TEAM COULD MAKE AN OFFER SO HIGH that John Schneider isn’t comfortable matching it.”

Twelves, let me explain restricted free agency because it’s genuinely useful to know.

A restricted free agent can negotiate with other teams — but his original team gets to MATCH any offer he receives. That’s the “right of first refusal.” You can’t just sign him away.

So Seattle holds the cards. The only way they lose him is if somebody makes an offer so expensive that Seattle decides it isn’t worth matching.

And the good news attached: “BUD CLARK RETURNS IN 2027 after missing this season with that PRESEASON INJURY. If we keep Okada, THAT SAFETY GROUP SHOULD BE SET FOR YEARS.”

That’s a real point. Nick Emmanwori — who covered Trey McBride and broke up the fourth-down pass at the goal line against Arizona — plus Julian Love, plus Ty Okada, plus Bud Clark coming back. That’s four safeties.

And remember what Macdonald said about Emmanwori’s coverage ability: “he can run with anybody, as we know — but the ability to STAY CONNECTED at like the top of routes.” Plus: “we won’t call them push-offs, but I HAVE MY OWN OPINION ABOUT IT.”

That group is good, and it’s cheap to keep good.

NOW — let me connect all of this, because the two stories really are one story.

The framing was right: “this is A DECISIVE MOMENT, but I LEAN HOPEFUL. The quarterback room looks STABLE and we’ve got three good quarterbacks. THE CHALLENGE IS PROTECTING THE DEPTH THAT GOT US HERE.”

Because look at what has actually carried this football team. Not stars. DEPTH.

Not one of those men is a star. Every single one of them won Seattle football games.

And the message to the front office is exactly the right one: “DON’T LET A GOOD THING SLIP AWAY. Let’s finish this season strong and then DO IT RIGHT IN THE OFFSEASON.”
