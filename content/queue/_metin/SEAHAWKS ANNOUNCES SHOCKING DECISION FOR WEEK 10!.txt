“Get in your own way.”

Seahawks Nation, four words. That’s Mike Macdonald cutting a reporter off mid-question, and it’s the most important thing he said all week.

Because on Sunday, Seattle plays Klint Kubiak — the man who ran this offense last season, now the head coach of the Las Vegas Raiders.

And twelves, that is the exact kind of game teams lose by outsmarting themselves.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four. Las Vegas on Sunday afternoon, then the bye. Drew Lock is starting on a managed elbow, Sam Darnold is out, Quenton Nelson is out — and I’m going to give you my prediction at the end, plus the three things that actually decide this football game.

Let’s go.

THE SETUP: this is a reunion game, and Seattle is the one who lost somebody.

Klint Kubiak ran Seattle’s offense last season. Now he’s the Raiders head coach. And Macdonald was asked how similar Las Vegas looks to what Kubiak built here.

“There’s a lot of parallels. To their credit, they’ve evolved. They’re building it around the people that they have, and they’re doing it at a high level. So I respect how they operate. They’ve got great people and good players, and they’re MOVING THE BALL AND PUTTING UP A LOT OF POINTS.”

“Moving the ball and putting up a lot of points.”

Twelves, that is not a courtesy. Coaches don’t hand out that sentence about bad offenses. That’s a warning, delivered politely.

And here’s what makes this matchup genuinely strange: the Raiders are running a system Seattle’s head coach watched get built, in his own building, for a full season. Every install meeting. Every third-down package. Every red zone wrinkle.

Which brings us to the trap — and Macdonald named it before anybody could fall into it.

A reporter started to ask: do you have to guard against overthinking it, like “okay, he knows I think this way, so...”

And Macdonald jumped in. “Sure. Yeah. Yeah, for sure. Get in your own way. Yeah.”

Let me explain the spiral, because it’s real and it has cost real teams real games.

He knows I like to pressure on third down. So he’ll be expecting pressure. So I’ll drop into coverage instead and surprise him. But wait — he knows that I know that he knows. So maybe he’s expecting the drop. So I should pressure after all.

And three loops into that, you’ve abandoned the two or three things your players are actually elite at in order to win an argument inside your own skull.

That’s how you lose a familiarity game. Not because the other guy outsmarted you — because you outsmarted yourself.

And Macdonald’s answer to it is almost boring, which is exactly why I trust it:

“Really focusing on our details, and making sure that we’re aligned and on the same page and we’re executing at a high level — and then go into the game and make the best adjustments we possibly can as the game starts to unfold.”

Details. Alignment. Execution. Adjust live.

No chess match. Play your brand, fix it in real time.

And twelves, “adjust throughout the game” is the thing this staff has proven it can do. They held Kansas City to three points. They held Arizona to three points. And when they didn’t do it — against Chicago — Julian Love told us exactly why, and it wasn’t scheme. It was a screen on third and nine and a penalty on a kickoff.

THE SECOND PROBLEM: Seattle is preparing for a quarterback who has thrown about eight passes in his NFL career.

That’s Fernando Mendoza, the Raiders’ rookie.

And twelves, I want you to actually think about how weird that is as a coaching assignment. There is almost no film. Eight passes. You can’t build a tendency report off eight passes. You can’t tell what he does on third and seven versus second and four, because he hasn’t had enough of either.

Macdonald’s approach:

“We have to do a great job of understanding how they operate BY SITUATION — probably at their core. And then you want to use the information available to you, but you can’t put all your eggs in one basket on what’s going to happen, or how you think the quarterback’s going to play. That wouldn’t be wise.”

“Understanding how they operate by situation, at their core.”

That’s the key insight, and here’s the translation: don’t scout the quarterback. Scout the team.

Because what do you actually need to know? What do they call on first and ten? What’s their third-down staple? What do they do inside the ten? What’s the two-minute package? Those are organizational answers, not quarterback answers — and the Raiders have nine games of them.

And then Macdonald said the smartest thing in the whole press conference, and this is the part people are missing:

“I don’t want to speak for Klint, but we’ve been through personnel changes in the middle of the season. You can’t make wholesale changes fundamentally and systematically to what you’re doing. I’m sure they’re thinking the same thing. They’re going to obviously tweak some things, as you do every week.”

Twelves, THIS is the answer to the mystery-quarterback problem.

A team does not rebuild its offense because the quarterback changed.

The protection calls stay. The run scheme stays. The route concepts stay. The terminology stays. You TWEAK — you lean harder on the things the new guy does well and you bury the things he doesn’t.

But the skeleton is the same skeleton.

So Seattle isn’t actually preparing for a quarterback they don’t know. They’re preparing for Klint Kubiak’s offense — which they know down to the syllable — with a rookie operating it.

And asked whether they’d study his college tape from Indiana and Cal, Macdonald gave a flat “no.”

That’s correct, and here’s why. College tape tells you about the human being. It tells you nothing about what the Raiders will ask him to do. Different system, different protections, different receivers, different coach. His college film is a scouting document, not a game plan document.

What they ARE using: “lean on our information we had coming out of the draft process.”

The pre-draft file. Seattle scouted this guy before anybody drafted him — his footwork, his pocket habits, what he does when the first read is covered, how he handles pressure. And because there are only eight NFL passes to study, that scouting report just became the primary document.

The scouting department is the game plan this week. That’s a genuinely unusual thing to say about an NFL game.

And on the preseason tape: “They do a little bit. But it’s kind of do the best you can, you know?”

“Do the best you can.” That’s the honest answer and he just said it out loud instead of pretending.

On the kid himself, he was gracious and short: “He’s a really good player, and he’s going to have a great career. You can tell that by the information we had coming out. He’s got a great skill set.”

And then the only strategy sentence in the whole answer — and it’s the whole strategy:

“We’ll focus on us, and us making sure that we’re playing our brand of ball, and TRY TO APPLY AS MUCH PRESSURE AS WE CAN on their offense.”

Apply as much pressure as we can.

Twelves, that’s it. That’s the plan against a rookie with eight career passes.

You don’t need to be exotic. You need to make him play fast. Make him hold the ball. Make him get to his third read. Because here’s the thing about inexperienced quarterbacks: they’re usually fine on the first read. The first read is what they practiced all week. It’s when the first read is covered and the pocket starts moving that eight passes of experience stops being enough.

Make him uncomfortable and let the rest take care of itself.

THE THIRD PROBLEM — and this is the one almost nobody is talking about. The kickoff battle.

A reporter pointed out that the Raiders are really good at getting kickoff return yards, and asked how teams have adjusted to the dynamic kickoff.

And Macdonald’s answer turned into the best hidden-value lesson of the week.

First the honest part: “There’s less explosive returns, as we’re getting more used to the play.” And on the safety rules: “I don’t know if Jay would agree with me on this, but the rule about having another returner — I’m eager to see what the stats are at the end of the season. It feels like it’s being effective. I DON’T HAVE THE DATA TO BACK THAT UP, but...”

A head coach giving an opinion and immediately labeling it an opinion. I’ll take that over false certainty every day of the week.

And then he praised the play itself: “it’s a good play. It’s been evolving at probably a higher rate than you might imagine. Kudos to all the people involved that got it back in the game.”

“Got it back in the game.” Because the old kickoff had died — touchback, touchback, touchback. The new rules resurrected the return.

But here is the number, twelves, and this is why I’m spending time on it:

“There might not be as explosive plays. But there’s a huge difference now — if you can get a kick stopped inside the 25 yard line versus a touchback, or coming out past the 40. I mean, we’re talking almost like A POINT, you know? If you can get a stop.”

“Almost like a point.”

That’s a head coach quoting expected points value on live television.

And the math is real. Every yard line on a football field has a scoring value attached to it. A drive starting at the twenty is worth meaningfully less than a drive starting at the forty-plus. The difference between those two starting spots is worth roughly a point of expected scoring.

Now multiply by five or six kickoffs in a game.

That’s four to six points of margin, decided entirely by whether your kickoff team can tackle.

And Macdonald’s label for it: “a kind of high leverage type of play.”

Twelves, this is NOT theoretical for Seattle. Julian Love told us exactly how the Bears game turned: a “cost-of-doing-business penalty on that kickoff” gave Chicago the ball near midfield — and the very next snap was the forty-yard screen that Love said “just killed all momentum.”

One kickoff. One play. Season-altering loss.

And now Seattle travels to a team that is among the best in football at returning kickoffs.

So here’s my first key to the game: Seattle’s kickoff coverage unit might be worth more on Sunday than any single offensive player.

NOW — the injury picture, because it decides what Seattle can even attempt.

Drew Lock is the plan again, same managed week. Macdonald: “Drew’s making good progress. We have a plan. And similar to last week, we’ll go from there. He’s not going to take every rep. He’s not going to make every throw again — but that’s just trying to be as prudent as possible to make sure that he’s feeling his best on SUNDAY.”

Last week that meant roughly twenty-something throws in an entire week, all on one day, and nothing in pregame until the pads were on. The result: nineteen of twenty-eight, 175 yards, a touchdown, zero turnovers, seventeen to three.

And Sam Darnold is out, so there’s no ambiguity about who’s playing.

On Charles Cross, Leonard Williams, Uchenna Nwosu and Rashid Shaheed: “we’re not going full speed today, so a lot of those guys will have the opportunity to take reps today, and then we’ll see tomorrow. Haven’t ruled anybody out yet, nor have we ruled anybody in yet. But GOOD NEWS on a lot of those guys.”

“Good news on a lot of those guys” is a genuine upgrade from last week’s hedge. And the Wednesday walkthrough is the mechanism — a man who can’t go full speed can still get his assignments and his footwork at walkthrough tempo, and then Thursday is the real test.

And Quenton Nelson, two days after his knee procedure: “He’s here. He’s back in the building, and he looks good. We were able to get to some things treatment-wise that are of the caliber — which is MUSIC TO MY EARS. So I know he’s going to be back sooner than later, if it’s under his control.”

Surgery Monday. In the building Wednesday. “Music to my ears.” Out Sunday, week to week — and with the bye in Week 11, San Francisco on November twenty-ninth is genuinely in play. Seattle never put him on IR, which would have cost four games minimum.

NOW — the matchup I’m most interested in, and it’s the one Macdonald was asked about directly.

Nick Emmanwori on another good tight end.

He covered Trey McBride last week and erased him. And Macdonald explained exactly why he’s built for the job:

“Some, not all tight ends run the routes equally. They run different route trees, in terms of timing of plays and how they get open. Nick has the ability — he can run with anybody, as we know — but the ability to stay CONNECTED at like the top of routes.”

“The top of the route” is the break point. Where the receiver stops going straight and changes direction. That’s where separation is created or denied — everything before it is just running.

And then the part that made me laugh: “the best tight ends are able to use physicality to create separation. And Nick’s able to either fight fire with fire on that front, or be able to absorb — WE WON’T CALL THEM PUSH-OFFS, but, you know, I HAVE MY OWN OPINION ABOUT IT — where you can absorb some of those tricks of the trade, so to speak, and still stay connected.”

[That is a head coach accusing NFL tight ends of uncalled offensive pass interference, in public, without technically saying the words.]

And he’s right. A 250-pound tight end puts a hand on a defender’s hip at the break and shoves. It happens constantly. It’s almost never flagged.

So covering elite tight ends requires one of two things: be physical first, or be strong enough that the shove doesn’t move you. Nick Emmanwori — six foot three, 227 pounds, with a perfect 10.00 Relative Athletic Score — can do both.

And “another good tight end obviously this week” is Macdonald telling you the assignment is the same on Sunday. Watch for it on third down.

And here’s the coaching detail behind it: “we didn’t rush Nick as much this week.” They took his blitz role away to make him a cover man. Expect that again.

NOW — how Seattle actually stops the run, because Macdonald gave a full philosophy and it’s the best football of the week.

Asked how front structures and coverage structures connect against the run:

“It starts with the type of blocks that you think you’re going to get, and who’s playing those blocks, and how many DOUBLE TEAM combinations you’re willing to LIVE WITH. How many SINGLES you’re trying to CREATE. That’s the starting point.”

Twelves, that’s the entire art of run defense in one sentence and I want you to have it.

A double team is two blockers on one defender. Your guy is going to lose that — he’s SUPPOSED to lose it. His job is to lose it slowly, hold ground, and occupy two men.

Because every double team the offense uses is one fewer blocker available somewhere else. So if you accept two double teams, you have just created two singly-blocked or unblocked defenders elsewhere on the field.

You’re not trying to win every block. You’re choosing WHERE you lose so you know where you win.

And then the coverage goes on top: “it starts with your core competencies and what you hang your hat on. And then you build it around how you feel like you’re being attacked.”

Your strengths first. The opponent second. Not the other way around.

AND THEN the single most illuminating thing he said all week:

“Obviously your pass responsibility has to take you to your run fit, and vice versa. So if that’s at odds — as a coach, that’s stuff you have to be really vigilant about, to make sure your guys aren’t going ‘I got this gap, I got that guy.’ That’s a problem.”

Every defender has two jobs on every snap. A run gap to fill, and a man or zone to cover. And he doesn’t know which one applies until after the ball is snapped.

So if a linebacker’s run gap is to the LEFT and his coverage assignment is to the RIGHT, that man has a split second to diagnose — and if he’s wrong, he’s not a step late. He’s thirty feet out of position.

And Macdonald says it’s the COACH’S job to never put a player in that spot. Your pass job should point you toward your run fit. Same direction. So even a half-beat of wrong diagnosis still leaves you useful.

Twelves, THAT is why this defense held Kansas City to three and Arizona to three. It isn’t exotic blitzing. It’s that nobody out there is being asked to be in two places at once.

Quick notes on the rest, because they matter.

On Jadarian Price’s expanded role: “JD looked good. Something we’ll still continue to look at. He has vision, he has burst.” And the key: “having Zach becoming healthier at halfback definitely helps us be able to do that.” Translation — Zach Charbonnet carrying the load is what frees Price up to be a receiving weapon instead of a workhorse.

On the deadline: “you all know how John rolls... nothing became serious enough for us to have that type of conversation. But look — I love our team. We have work to do. We have room to grow. And I believe that we can, and we will.”

And on his own staff, the line I’d put on a wall. Macdonald described Klint Kubiak as a man who “can tell you the truth and go THROUGH, not around — without sugarcoating it, but keeping the personal emotions out of it.”

That’s the man on the other sideline Sunday. Direct, honest, unemotional.

And on how Seattle builds its own calls: “a lot of stuff we’re running is not starting in my head — it’s starting in our staff’s head.” Plus: “you’re not just running one-off plays, it’s all kind of seamless.”

A head coach saying his best ideas belong to his assistants. That’s not humility for the cameras. That’s how you keep a staff after somebody takes your coordinator.

Let me bring it home with the three keys and a prediction.

KEY ONE: the kickoff battle. Las Vegas is excellent at returns and Macdonald himself put the value at “almost a point” per stop. Five or six kickoffs, four to six points of hidden margin, and a Bears loss that turned on exactly this. If Seattle’s coverage unit is clean, that’s the game.

KEY TWO: make the rookie get to his third read. Eight NFL passes is enough to be comfortable on read one. It is not enough to be comfortable with a moving pocket and a covered primary. Macdonald said it himself: “apply as much pressure as we can.”

KEY THREE: protect Drew Lock. He’s on a twenty-throw week with a sore elbow, and if Charles Cross gets back, everything about Sunday gets easier. Zero turnovers in three straight games, zero interceptions on the year, and a punter who can bury anybody — that’s a formula that beat Arizona seventeen to three.

My prediction: Seahawks twenty-four, Raiders twenty.

And I’ll show my work, because I owe you that after missing the Bears game by twenty-four points.

I’m picking Seattle because a rookie quarterback with eight career passes against this defense, on the road against a team that just held two offenses to three points, is a hard way to live.

But I’m keeping it close for three reasons. One: Seattle still may not have Charles Cross, Leonard Williams or Rashid Shaheed. Two: Klint Kubiak knows this personnel intimately, and “moving the ball and putting up a lot of points” came out of Macdonald’s own mouth. And three: the Raiders’ return game is a real path to free points.

If Seattle wins the kickoff battle, it’s a comfortable afternoon. If Las Vegas hits one big return and gets a short field, this is a one-score game into the fourth quarter.

Two questions for you, twelves. First: does the Klint Kubiak familiarity help Seattle or help Las Vegas? My read is slightly Seattle — because Macdonald already named the trap out loud. “Get in your own way” is the mistake, and he identified it before anybody could make it.

And second: who do you most need back on Sunday? Mine is Charles Cross, and it isn’t close. You’ve got a quarterback managing an elbow and an oblique. The best gift you can give him is his left tackle.
