“That’s honestly my fault.”

Seahawks Nation, that is the first thing I want you to hear today. Not a stat. Not a record.

A man who had just caught nine passes for a hundred and fifty yards against the best team in football, who had a hundred and twenty-nine of those before halftime, who had scored the touchdown that broke the Kansas City Chiefs — got asked about the one bad play of the night, and said:

“That’s honestly my fault. Really just trying to do too much. And I’ll take that one personally.”

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seahawks 20, Chiefs 3. Seattle is 4-3. And Jaxon Smith-Njigba just gave the most revealing press conference of the season. Let’s go through it, because the man told you exactly how he’s doing this — and then took the blame for the only thing that went wrong.

First, the table-setting, because what he did put him in a club with two members.

Nine catches. A hundred and fifty yards. A twenty-eight-yard touchdown. His sixth hundred-yard game in seven games. He’s at 867 receiving yards and eight touchdowns.

And the company: through seven games, only two other players in NFL history have had 800-plus receiving yards and eight touchdowns. Jerry Rice in 1986. Cooper Kupp in 2021.

Jerry Rice. Cooper Kupp. Jaxon Smith-Njigba. And Cooper Kupp was standing in the same huddle with him on Sunday night, which is one of the strangest and best facts of this entire season.

He opened with gratitude, and then he said “backs against the wall” — and he meant it.

His first words: “Hello. Hello. What a win. Felt great. Got to thank my Lord and Savior Jesus Christ for another win, another week.” And then: “Our backs were against the wall, and just how we came out here as a brotherhood and fought and swung. The resiliency of our team is something special and we’re going to keep going.”

A reporter pushed — did you actually feel the urgency after Denver, after two straight losses? “Ten thousand percent. We don’t — losing two in a row, I don’t know the last time we’ve been a part of that.”

“I don’t know the last time we’ve been a part of that.”

Twelves, that’s an honest sentence from inside a locker room. This team was not used to losing two in a row. It happened, the whole internet decided the season was over, and his answer wasn’t “we tuned out the noise.” It was closer to: we noticed, and it bothered us, and we went to work.

He said this too: “We know how great of a team Kansas City is. So our weekly — coming in Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday — our process, we just stuck to that. And I’m super proud of our guys this week.”

Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday. He didn’t say they changed anything. He said they stuck to the process on the three days nobody films.

And then: “We knew how big this game was, and we wanted this one badly. We saw it as a playoff game. And we’re going to attack each week like that.”

“We saw it as a playoff game.” In Week 7. Against a 5-0 team. And they won it 20-3.

Now the football. And this is where it gets really good, because he explained his own game better than any analyst could.

Kansas City came out and played man coverage on him. A lot of it. They pressed him. They challenged him physically. Steve Spagnuolo looked at Seattle’s best player and said: we’re going to cover him with one guy.

Here is what Jaxon Smith-Njigba said about that:

“Definitely something — my eyes get big when I see it. And I know if we get the chance to take a shot in man coverage, we’re going to take it. So I always love my chances when I see that.”

“My eyes get big.”

That is a competitor telling you that single coverage is a gift. And remember what Mike Macdonald said about the touchdown — that he knew where the ball was going the moment he saw the coverage: “when you see him singled like that, and based off that play call, I knew that’s where Sam was going to go with the ball.”

Coach saw it. The receiver saw it. The quarterback saw it. Kansas City left him alone and all three men on Seattle’s sideline knew it was over.

And here’s the part that matters for the rest of the season: Seattle has struggled against man coverage this year. A reporter said it to his face — you’ve had challenges against man, and you were great against it tonight, what changed?

His answer was not a scheme answer: “Just keep fighting, keep going. We know what type of players we are. And the more we just keep going out there and swinging and taking chances, we’re going to come down with those balls. I know that we can get it done. And we have all the confidence in the world to get it done. So it was good to see it come through tonight.”

“It was good to see it come through.” That’s a man who believed it before it worked.

Now press coverage — and this answer is my favorite of the whole night.

Kansas City presses as aggressively as anybody in the league. A reporter asked how he gets around it, and pointed out that not a lot of guys do.

“I love to see it. I invite it.”

“I invite it.”

And then he gave away the technique, which is rare: “For me it’s just to play fast and get to my spot and beat it. Picking a shoulder and really just reacting off the DB so he can’t be right.”

There’s a real coaching point buried in there. “Picking a shoulder” means committing hard to one side of the defender’s body. “Reacting off the DB so he can’t be right” means not running a predetermined route — reading which way the corner opens his hips, and going the other way.

He’s not beating the press with a plan. He’s beating it with a response. You cannot guess right against a man who waits to see what you do.

Then the follow-up: it’s hard to press a guy who’s in motion, right?

“Ten thousand percent. Being all over the field, it’s hard for a defense to pick up on it. If they want to key in on me, we’re going to make their job hard.”

“We’re going to make their job hard.” Klint Kubiak moving him around the formation isn’t decoration. It’s the counter to being the only guy on the scouting report.

Now the middle of the field, because this is the thesis of his entire game.

A reporter noticed something: besides the two sideline catches, Seattle completed a ton of slants. Was the middle of the field something you knew you could attack?

“Ten thousand percent. And that’s kind of my game — definitely attack the middle of the field. Something I’m comfortable with, something I’ve been doing for a very, very long time.”

And then the sentence that should scare every defensive coordinator left on this schedule:

“To be able to run all routes in the middle of the field, line up outside, go balls, everything just opens up — and I can use everything to my advantage.”

Think about the problem he just described. Take away the middle and he lines up outside and runs past you. Take away the sideline and he lives on slants. Press him and he reacts off your shoulder. Play him in man and his eyes get big. There’s no answer that’s an answer.

And the touchdown — he told us exactly how it happened. It was not a called play that survived. It was a check at the line.

“Sam gave me — Sam, what a game Sam played. He saw the coverage and we checked it at the line, and he threw an incredible pass.”

A quarterback and a receiver, at the line against a Kansas City defense, changing the play based on what they saw — and then executing it for a twenty-eight-yard touchdown. That’s two men reading the same page. Nine days after people were telling me to make videos about Sam Darnold wanting out of Seattle.

And then on the catch itself — did you know you were in, or did you need the replay?

“I could tell I was in. I saw my foot, I believe, on the goal line. But just let the process be the process. And it was a touchdown.”

“Let the process be the process.” Even on a replay review.

Now Sam Darnold — hear this one in full, because of what this city spent two weeks saying about that man.

“Like I said, what a great, great game Sam played. He came out here, he led us to this win. Being able to use his feet when there’s nothing there, and create plays, and lead us — it was big time to see him step up like that. And that’s just the player and person he is. You know, that’s why we love him.”

“That’s why we love him.”

Sam Darnold on Sunday night: twenty of twenty-eight, 257 yards, one touchdown. Zero sacks. Hit twice — after five sacks against San Francisco and five against Denver. And the best receiver on the team, unprompted, said that’s why we love him. Twice in one press conference he went out of his way to credit the quarterback.

Now third down — the single biggest thing that changed, and the receiver’s explanation for it.

Seattle came into this game twenty-ninth in the NFL in third-down conversion rate. On Sunday night they converted eleven of their first fifteen and finished eleven of sixteen — against a Kansas City defense that went three of eleven on its own third downs. And a lot of Seattle’s were third-and-seven or longer.

So what changed? Notice who he credits first:

“Man, I just saw execution. It starts with the guys up front. They played a hell of a game — keeping Sam clean. And then Sam just making the right reads. Like I said, tucking it when he had to. And us receivers, we understand that we need to stay on the field. That’s how you beat great teams, is staying on the field. And we did that tonight. We executed, and it felt good.”

“It starts with the guys up front.” The number one receiver, after a hundred-and-fifty-yard night, started his third-down answer with the offensive line.

And remember what we’ve been telling you for two weeks: Anthony Bradford came back at right guard. Zero sacks, a hundred and fifty-three rushing yards, thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds of possession. The fix was a guard, not a trade.

And “us receivers, we understand that we need to stay on the field” — that’s a wideout describing his job as possession rather than production.

He also credited a coach a lot of people wanted fired three weeks ago. Asked about the cadence — because Seattle was mixing up snap timing all night, quick snaps and long holds — he said:

“We want to mix up the cadence, snap the ball, and also have a long cadence to really see what they’re in. And I thought Brian Fleury put us in a great position to be able to read the defense and then make counter moves to it.”

“Make counter moves to it.” That’s the whole game in four words. Hold the cadence, watch Kansas City declare its coverage, then counter. And Macdonald said the same thing about third down from his own podium: “Brian led the charge, and I thought we came up with some really great ideas and solutions.”

Two different men, two podiums, same name. Brian Fleury had a hell of a night.

Now Nick Emmanwori — and this is the quote that’s going to travel.

A reporter brought up seeing Emmanwori in practice, running stride for stride with Xavier Worthy and also rushing off the edge.

“I mean, most versatile player in the league, you can say. Being able to put his hand in the dirt, and then guard receivers — I’ve never seen a player like him. He’s a special player, and I’m definitely happy he’s on our team.”

“I’ve never seen a player like him.”

And Emmanwori did it on Sunday on a hip injury that showed up on Friday’s report — four tackles, a pass breakup, and he helped force the throw that Ty Okada intercepted. Macdonald’s entire response to a list of what he’d done was: “Yeah, he’s pretty good.”

And on the defense that did the actual destroying, he went all the way:

“Super incredible. Time and time again, I continue to say it: they’re the best in the world, and they prove it. They back it up. And the swagger, and how fast they’re playing, is incredible.”

And the short version: the Chiefs never reached the red zone — the first time ever in a Mahomes start. 213 yards, fifty plays. And the streak lived: thirty-six straight games without a hundred-yard rusher. Kenneth Walker got thirty-six yards on fourteen carries.

Which brings me to the saddest little moment of the whole press conference. Someone asked if he got a chance to talk to Ken before or after the game.

“I didn’t get to. I didn’t get to catch him.”

That’s the whole answer. Two men who played together in this building, on opposite sidelines for the biggest game of the season, and in the chaos of a 20-3 Sunday night they never found each other.

Now the ceiling — and he did not hedge.

Asked how far this offense plus this defense can go:

“Oh man, we can win. We can win a lot of games. We can be the best in the league, of course. And that’s something we preach — playing complementary football in all three phases. That’s who we are. So it was great to get back on track, and I feel like this is going to leap us forward into the next weeks.”

“We can be the best in the league, of course.”

“Of course.” Not we think. Not if everything goes right. Of course. From the man who’s on a Jerry Rice pace.

And “complementary football in all three phases” is not a cliché here. Seattle held the ball thirty-six minutes, which held Kansas City to fifty plays, which held Kenneth Walker to fourteen carries. The offense was the run defense — the arithmetic we’ve been doing all month, said out loud by a receiver.

And now the ending. The last question of the press conference was about the interception, and his answer is the reason I made this video.

Sam Darnold threw an interception in the end zone at the end of the first half — Seattle’s one real mistake. The play broke down, Darnold scrambled, and the throw went to the wrong place.

The question was essentially: did the play just get scrambled? The easiest thing in the world would be to say yes. It did get scrambled. Nobody would have argued.

“Yeah, that’s — it’s honestly my fault, honestly. On that one, really just trying to do too much. And I’ll take that one personally.”

“Trying to do too much.”

Twelves, understand the situation. His quarterback had been destroyed for two weeks. His quarterback led the league in interceptions. There was a loud, organized campaign to bench the man. And his best receiver — on the night he went for a hundred and fifty yards and put himself next to Jerry Rice — stood up and said the interception was mine.

That is what a locker room looks like when it’s actually fine. Not a slogan. A star taking a turnover off his quarterback’s ledger in public, four days after the whole league said that quarterback was done.

Macdonald said something in his own press conference that fits here: “He’s had some adversity this week. I’ll leave it like that. But just really proud of him as a person. He’s just a person, competitor, teammate. How do you not have the most respect for the guy and love him for how he operates?”

“I’ll leave it like that.” His head coach drew a line around something private in that young man’s life this week, and I’m going to respect that line too. It’s not our business. What I’ll say is: whatever it was, he carried it into Sunday night and put up a hundred and twenty-nine yards before halftime.

So let me be careful about one thing, because I promised you I always would.

The title of this video says he terrified the AFC. That’s my framing, not his. He never mentioned the AFC and never called anybody out — he thanked his teammates, credited the line, praised his quarterback, and took the blame for an interception.

But the AFC part isn’t nothing. Kansas City is the standard in that conference. They came in 5-0, with one of the great defensive minds in the game, and they decided to cover Jaxon Smith-Njigba with one man. He went for a hundred and fifty and a touchdown and they lost by seventeen. That’s a real message, even if he was too classy to send it himself.

And one honest asterisk. Kansas City was banged up — Rashee Rice out, Travis Kelce limited all week, Brashard Smith out, and down at least one starting tackle. Does that take something off it? A little. Not much. Seattle played without Charbonnet, Drake Thomas, Chazz Surratt and its backup quarterback, and lost Charles Cross and Rashid Shaheed during the game. Both teams were hurt. One of them scored three points.

So where are we? Seattle is 4-3 — three losses by a combined eight points, and now a seventeen-point win over the last undefeated team in football. Chicago comes next, November second. The trade deadline is November tenth — and after a hundred and fifty-three rushing yards with zero sacks, I’d bet John Schneider is a lot less interested in paying a premium for a running back than he was a week ago.

Let me close it.

Nine days ago this team was 3-3, its quarterback led the league in interceptions, and I was being asked to make videos about him leaving town.

On Sunday night, Seattle’s best player stood at a podium and said: my eyes get big when I see man. I invite the press. The middle of the field is my game. It starts with the guys up front. That’s why we love Sam. We can be the best in the league, of course. And the interception was my fault.

Monster form isn’t just the hundred and fifty yards. It’s a receiver on a Jerry Rice pace who credits his offensive line first and takes the turnover himself.

Twelves, two questions. First: what’s the most un-coverable thing he does — the slant, the sideline, the motion, or the way he reacts off the press? Mine’s the press answer. “Reacting off the DB so he can’t be right” is the most quietly terrifying sentence any receiver has said this year. And second, the real one: do you believe him about the ceiling? He said we can be the best in the league, of course. Seattle is 4-3. Is he right, or is he a competitor being a competitor?
