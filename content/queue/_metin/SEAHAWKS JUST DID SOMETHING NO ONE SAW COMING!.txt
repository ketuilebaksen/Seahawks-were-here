“There’s a difference in catching it eight yards deep in the end zone and making a play like that.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s Mike Macdonald describing the play that closed out this football game — and it is the single wildest thing Seattle has done all season.

On the last kickoff, Tory Horton caught the ball EIGHT YARDS DEEP IN HIS OWN END ZONE. With one hand. On purpose. By design. Because Seattle told him to.

And twelves, here’s the part that makes it insane: the two men who normally do that job weren’t available.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is six and four. Drew Lock is four and oh. And this press conference has the kickoff story, two new injuries, Jadarian Price’s first career touchdown, a cornerback with two tackles for loss, and the clearest Sam Darnold update we’ve gotten yet.

Takeaways and red zone. Both sides of the ball. Still on the list.

NOW — THE KICKOFF PLAY. And twelves, I need you to understand how improvised this was.

“It’s a tremendous play. I mean, Tory’s actually TRYING to catch that ball — albeit with one hand — but an amazing, just an incredible play.”

And then the context that makes it remarkable:

“And I think, you know, with GEORGE HOLANI GOING DOWN, then we had to take JD off of kickoff return. So those are guys that HAVEN’T NECESSARILY PRACTICED A BUNCH IN A WHILE — especially with this game plan. And so to be able to step in and execute some of those critical situations is some great stuff by them.”

Let me lay out what happened.

George Holani — one of Seattle’s kickoff returners — got hurt during the game. And that forced Seattle to pull Jadarian Price OFF kickoff return, presumably because they weren’t going to risk their healthy running back back there with Holani down.

So the men who ended up fielding a critical late-game kickoff were guys who HADN’T PRACTICED IT MUCH, in a game plan they hadn’t drilled.

And a reporter asked whether special teams coordinator Jay Harbaugh called them together on the sideline and explained it, or whether they just knew.

And Macdonald said: “No — we TOLD them that was a situation going in.”

Twelves, they planned for this. Before the game. In the meeting room.

And then the sentence that explains everything: “There’s a difference in catching it eight yards deep in the end zone and making a play like that.”

Here’s the football, and this is where it gets beautiful.

Under the dynamic kickoff rules, where you catch the ball changes everything about where your offense starts. Take a knee in the end zone and you get the ball at a set spot. But catch it deep and break it out, and you can do much better than that.

And remember what Mike Macdonald told us about kickoffs just last week? His exact words: “there’s a huge difference now — if you can get a kick stopped inside the 25 yard line versus a touchback, or coming out past the 40. We’re talking ALMOST LIKE A POINT, you know?”

“Almost like a point.”

He explained the value of the play on Wednesday. And then on Sunday his team cashed it in — with backups who hadn’t practiced it — to close out a football game.

That is coaching. That is a staff that teaches the WHY and not just the WHAT.

And Macdonald’s credit was shared: “great job by our coaches, and it really helped us close the game out.”

NOW — the play that turned the football game, and the coaching behind it is excellent.

A reporter noted Devon Witherspoon got a sack after Las Vegas had scored on its first couple of drives, and asked if that turned the tide.

Macdonald’s first answer: “Yeah, in retrospect probably. I think we’re just trying to HUNT FOR A POSITIVE PLAY for us.”

And then he stopped, thought about it, and corrected himself: “But now that I’m thinking about it — honestly, yeah, probably SPARKED US.”

I love that. He was asked a leading question, gave a careful answer, and then reconsidered in real time and agreed. That’s a man actually thinking instead of performing.

And then came the real story. Was it something you saw ahead of time, or an in-game adjustment?

“Uh, it was an ADJUSTMENT in the game. He was HOT, you know? So it was just like — all right, what’s on the sheet that we can bring 21? So that’s our coaches’ credit. They saw it, they ENCOURAGED ME TO DIAL IT UP, and Spoon made it come to life.”

And he clarified what “hot” meant, because a reporter asked if he meant angry: “No, no. Like — I think we called one earlier, he didn’t get there but the ball was out. But I think HE WON HIS RUSH. And so we were just like, all right, let’s keep sending him.”

Twelves, let me unpack this because it’s the whole game in one sequence.

Seattle called a Witherspoon blitz earlier. He didn’t get a sack — the quarterback got the ball out in time. BUT HE WON THE RUSH. He beat his man.

And Mike Macdonald looked at that and thought: the result was nothing, but the process was good. Keep going.

That is such an important distinction. Most people judge a blitz by whether it got there. Macdonald judged it by whether his player beat his block. And because the answer was yes, he kept calling it — and the second time, it landed.

“What’s on the sheet that we can bring 21?” That’s him asking which calls in the game plan send number twenty-one. Devon Witherspoon.

And here’s the part I respect most: “that’s our COACHES’ credit. They saw it, they ENCOURAGED ME to dial it up.”

A head coach saying publicly that his assistants told him to make the call. And remember what he told us on Wednesday: “a lot of stuff we’re running is not starting in my head — it’s starting in our staff’s head.”

He said it. Then he proved it. Same week.

And then the honest team note: “it’s great timing, it’s great ability by him — but also, if the quarterback has a place to go on his first read, then Spoon’s not getting there in time. So it’s a TEAM PLAY. But Spoon’s a dynamite blitzer and he made it come to life.”

“If the quarterback has a place to go on his first read, Spoon’s not getting there in time.”

Twelves, that’s the sack explained properly. The coverage took away read one. The rookie had to hold the ball. And THEN the blitz arrived.

And that is EXACTLY the trap the national analysts described before this game. Their words: “that combination can punish hesitation without requiring a spectacular mistake. Hold the ball, lose yardage, face an obvious passing situation.”

It worked precisely as advertised.

Short, intermediate, deep, after the catch, scramble drill, AND the run game.

NOW — the play I want to highlight most, because it’s the kind nobody notices.

A reporter said: I don’t often see cornerbacks with multiple tackles for loss WITHOUT blitzing — but Josh Jobe had two, making plays on the perimeter.

And Macdonald’s answer is a clinic:

“Well, they’re great AWARENESS plays. To be able to IDENTIFY THE CRACK BLOCK, because HE’S NOT THE PRIMARY FORCE PLAYER on those plays.”

Twelves, let me explain the crack block, because it’s one of the sneakiest things in football.

On a perimeter run or a bubble screen, the offense wants to get the ball outside. And there’s one defender whose job is to turn that play back inside — he’s called the FORCE player. Usually a safety or an outside linebacker.

So the offense sends a receiver BACK INSIDE to block that force player in the side. That’s the crack block. It’s called “cracking” because it comes from an unexpected angle.

And when the receiver leaves to crack, that frees the cornerback — who was covering him — to come downhill and replace the force player.

And that’s the thing. The corner has to RECOGNIZE it. Instantly. He has to see his man leave, understand what it means, and sprint to a job that isn’t normally his.

Josh Jobe did that TWICE. For tackles for loss.

And Macdonald credited the whole structure: “then really the rest of the defense to get the ball to the bubble. On the TEMPO perimeter play, I think Drake saw it quick. Julian SHOT HIS GUN — he was the force player in that situation, and that really made the ball come to life for Josh.”

“Julian shot his gun.”

Julian Love attacked so aggressively as the force player that the offense had to throw the ball outside — right to where Josh Jobe was arriving.

That’s a defense working as one organism. One man forces the ball somewhere, and another man is already there.

And Macdonald’s close: “Josh has shown that he’s a PHYSICAL CORNER that has a really high ability to tackle. So it’s team play, but great play by Josh as well.”

NOW — Dante Fowler, and this is a vindication.

Asked about Fowler getting on the board with a couple of sacks:

“Yeah, I’m happy for Dante. He’s worked hard. He’s done a great job. Really HIS SKILL SET HASN’T BEEN ABLE TO SHINE as much as we envisioned BECAUSE OF THE TYPE OF GAMES WE’VE BEEN IN. But we’ve been able to CREATE SOME MORE PASSING SITUATIONS in the last few weeks. And I think you felt his ability to get after the quarterback. So this is MORE OF THE ROLE THAT WE ENVISIONED FOR HIM when we brought him along.”

Twelves, that’s the honest explanation of why a pass rusher looked quiet for two months.

Dante Fowler is a pass rush specialist. And a pass rush specialist needs the other team to be PASSING. If your games are close and grindy and the opponent is running the ball on second and five, there’s nothing for him to do.

But when your defense forces obvious passing situations — third and long, playing from behind — now he’s on the field and he’s hunting.

“We’ve been able to create more passing situations in the last few weeks.”

That’s not Dante Fowler improving. That’s the defense getting better, which created the conditions for Dante Fowler to be useful. And Macdonald said it with no spin at all.
