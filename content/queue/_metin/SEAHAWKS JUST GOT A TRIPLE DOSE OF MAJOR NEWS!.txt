THIRTEEN DAYS.

Seahawks Nation, that’s how long Quenton Nelson has to get himself ready to play football — and it’s the number that frames this entire bye week.

And it comes with two more pieces of news: George Holani’s knee injury is a SPRAINED MCL that’s week to week. And Mike Macdonald does NOT expect a single one of the players hurt in Las Vegas to go on injured reserve.

Not one.

That’s your triple dose, and every single part of it is good.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is six and four, San Francisco is thirteen days out, and I’m going to walk you through every injury, the quarterback decision nobody wants to hear, and a playoff picture that just got a lot more interesting because of something that happened in Detroit. Let’s go.

First, the single most important sentence of the week.

Mike Macdonald said he does NOT expect any of the Seahawks who got hurt over the weekend to go on injured reserve.

And twelves, I want to make sure you understand why that matters so much, because it’s the most reliable signal in football.

Injured reserve is not a suggestion. If you put a man on short-term IR, he must miss a MINIMUM number of games. It’s a rule. There’s no early return, no exceptions, no negotiating with it.

So when a head coach says nobody’s going on IR, he is telling you — in the only language the league gives him — that his medical staff believes these are SHORT absences.

You don’t decline IR for a man you think is out a month. You decline it for a man you think is out a game or two.

And here’s the honest caveat, which was made well: “in the long run, that is good news — though it DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY MEAN they come back right away.”

True. No IR means “not long.” It doesn’t mean “Sunday.” Fair.

NOW — George Holani, and this one has a real diagnosis attached.

Ian Rapoport reported that Holani had been dealing with pain against the Raiders, and that the injury is a SPRAINED MCL — described as a week-to-week injury.

And twelves, let me explain what that actually is, because MCL is one of those terms people hear and panic about.

The MCL is the ligament on the INSIDE of your knee. And here’s the critical difference from the injury everybody fears: an MCL sprain is NOT an ACL tear.

The ACL is inside the joint and it does not heal on its own — that’s surgery and a year of your life. Zach Charbonnet can tell you all about it.

The MCL is on the outside of the joint, it has a blood supply, and it HEALS. Depending on severity, a sprained MCL can be anywhere from a couple of weeks to much longer. But many of them are genuinely short.

And the framing we got is exactly right: “‘week to week’ tells you the team is EVALUATING HIM CONTINUALLY and does not have a FIXED RETURN DATE. In Holani’s case, the fact that it is described as week to week — and that the head coach does not expect anyone from the weekend to land on injured reserve — POINTS TOWARD THE SHORT END of that range.”

And then the sentence that makes this whole bye week valuable: “a player who might miss ONE GAME loses NOTHING, because the schedule gives him extra time to recover. That is THE QUIET ADVANTAGE OF THE TIMING HERE.”

Twelves, that’s the thing I want you to hold onto for the next two weeks.

A bye week turns a two-week injury into a zero-game absence.

Think about the math. If George Holani needs two weeks, and one of those weeks has no game in it — he misses nothing. He’s back for San Francisco.

And remember how hard Seattle felt his absence. Mike Macdonald said after the game: “with GEORGE HOLANI GOING DOWN, then we had to take JD off of kickoff return. So those are guys that HAVEN’T NECESSARILY PRACTICED A BUNCH IN A WHILE.”

Holani going down is what forced Jadarian Price off kickoff return — and what put Tory Horton in position to leap ten feet in the air and keep that kickoff alive. Everything’s connected.

NOW — Abe Lucas and Nick Kallerup, and here we have to be honest about what we don’t know.

“For Abe Lucas and Nick Kallerup, there is NOT MUCH IN-DEPTH INFORMATION on where each one is. What we do know is that IT DOES NOT LOOK LIKE EITHER WILL GO ON INJURED RESERVE.”

And then the honest labeling, which I appreciated: “this is PURE SPECULATION on my part, but to me, no long-term or short-term IR sounds like a WEEK MISSED. Maybe two.”

Good. That’s how you handle something you don’t know — you say it’s a guess and you move on.

And here’s where the bye saves Seattle again. Macdonald’s own words after the game were: “ABE IS AN ANKLE. What degree, we’re not sure.” And initial reports have suggested it could be a high ankle sprain — which, if true, is typically weeks rather than days.

But with no IR and a bye week, Abe Lucas missing “a week, maybe two” means he could miss ZERO additional games.

And twelves, here’s the beautiful part about the bye for banged-up players, and this was explained perfectly:

“A bye is MORE THAN A REST FOR THE STARTERS. It is a WINDOW FOR THE TRAINING STAFF to take players who are slightly banged up and give them REAL RECOVERY DAYS WITHOUT ADDING GAME WEAR. When a team has several players on the edge of returning, the bye can turn a TWO-WEEK INJURY into a ONE-GAME ABSENCE.”

“Without adding game wear.”

That’s the whole thing. Because in a normal week, a player who’s eighty percent healthy still has to practice, still has to travel, still has to absorb a football game — and often ends the week more hurt than he started it.

A bye removes the damage from the equation entirely. It’s the only two weeks all season where healing is the only thing happening.

And the conclusion is right: “a roster heading into the final stretch is BETTER OFF USING A BYE TO GET HEALTHY than to hold on with a PATCHWORK LINEUP.”

Twelves, Seattle has been running a patchwork lineup for a month. Both starting tackles out. A Pro Bowl guard out after surgery. Leonard Williams out. Rashid Shaheed out for two games. Uchenna Nwosu out for two games. And the starting quarterback out for three.

“The schedule could not have fallen better.” Agreed completely.

NOW — Quenton Nelson, and twelves, this is the situation I’m watching hardest.

“He had the procedure done LAST WEEK and was BACK IN THE FACILITY, but he HAS NOT YET BEEN OUT ON THE PRACTICE FIELD. Still, it feels like positive steps are being made. The hope is that he will be back by the San Francisco game, and AGAIN, THERE IS NO GUARANTEE. He has THIRTEEN DAYS to get ready.”

And the assessment: “for a veteran guard, that is a REASONABLE amount of time. And with the bye in the middle, he has time to work WITHOUT MISSING A GAME.”

And then came the smartest piece of analysis in this entire breakdown, and I want to give it room:

“Guards play in the MIDDLE of the line, where pressure arrives FAST and the physical contact is CONSTANT. A player coming back from a procedure needs to do MORE THAN FEEL FINE. HE NEEDS TO TRUST HIS BODY IN THOSE COLLISIONS.”

Twelves, that is exactly right and it’s the thing people miss about offensive line injuries.

A wide receiver coming back from a knee needs to be able to run and cut. He gets to choose when he takes contact, mostly.

A guard does not get that choice. On EVERY SINGLE SNAP, a three-hundred-pound man runs into him. There is no play where he avoids collision. There’s no easing into it.

So “does the knee feel okay” is not the standard. The standard is: can he plant that knee, take a bull rush from a defensive tackle, and not flinch?

And then the roadmap, which is the useful part: “that is why THE STAGES MATTER. First he is IN THE BUILDING. Then he PRACTICES. Then he PLAYS.”

“Missing the practice field is NOT A BAD SIGN right now, since he is ONLY A WEEK PAST THE PROCEDURE. THE QUESTION TO WATCH IS WHEN HE STARTS PRACTICING. That will tell us whether the San Francisco target is realistic.”

Twelves, write that down. THAT is the thing to watch over the next thirteen days.

Not whether he’s in the building — he already is, and Macdonald told us he “LOOKS GOOD” and that the procedure cleaned out cartilage, which he called “MUSIC TO MY EARS.”

Not whether people sound optimistic — they already do. Macdonald said “I know he’s going to be back SOONER THAN LATER if it’s under his control.”

The signal is the first practice. When Quenton Nelson’s name shows up on a practice report — even as limited — that’s when San Francisco becomes real.

And remember what he is to this offense. Macdonald called him “a FORCE MULTIPLIER for our group — not just our offensive line, but really our whole offensive unit and our football team.”

NOW — Sam Darnold, and twelves, this is the part that’s going to upset some of you.

“From what Mike Macdonald said, it sounds like we are finally going to see a FULLY HEALTHY VERSION of him, which in the long run is going to be a big deal. He appears to be ON TRACK TO START against the 49ers.”

And the critical nuance: “Macdonald made it clear that Darnold will be the starting quarterback WHEN HE IS HEALTHY. Macdonald also said Darnold has to be ONE HUNDRED PERCENT HEALTHY. So there is a chance — a world — where Drew Lock ends up starting against San Francisco. It does not feel like that is the case right now.”

Twelves, those are two different conditions and both matter.

Condition one: it’s Sam’s job when he’s healthy. That’s settled. Macdonald told a national broadcaster, the day BEFORE the Raiders game — asked if there was anything Drew Lock could do to keep the job — “NO.” And then: “IT’S SAM’S TEAM.”

He said that before he knew Lock would go fourteen of seventeen for two hundred forty yards. The answer was the same either way.

Condition two: ONE HUNDRED PERCENT healthy. Not “cleared.” Not “good enough.” A hundred percent.

And Macdonald set the practical test himself: “you’re going to want him to PRACTICE A SIGNIFICANT AMOUNT OF TIME for him to play.” Plus he’s since said he’s “REALLY OPTIMISTIC” Darnold will be ready.

So if Darnold practices fully, he plays. If he can’t, Drew Lock starts in San Francisco. That’s the actual rule, in public.

AND NOW — the most honest paragraph I’ve heard a fan say all season, and I respect the daylights out of it.

“I want to be honest about where I started. When I first heard it, my reaction was the same as many fans. THIS STINKS. Lock is the better quarterback right now and he looks like the better fit for this team in the long run. I think that is a FINE OPINION TO HAVE, and I had it.”

And then: “I try not to be a hot take guy. I try not to OVERREACT TO ONE GREAT GAME and demand a starting job because of it. But when I look at this team, it is VERY CLEAR what they are going to do.”

And then the part I want every single one of you to hear:

“It helps me to think about WHY fans lean toward Lock in the first place. When a backup plays well, people SEE THE RESULTS AND REMEMBER THEM. That is a natural reaction. BUT A HEAD COACH HAS OTHER INFORMATION — what he sees in practice, how the quarterback handles the game plan, HOW THE LOCKER ROOM RESPONDS, and how the player performed OVER A LONGER PERIOD.”

“That does not make the fans WRONG. It just explains why the decision is NOT SIMPLE.”

Twelves, that’s the most mature take anybody has offered on this, and it’s the same idea Mike Tomlin gave on national television — that the discussion inside the building is different from the one outside, because Sam Darnold has “money in Coach Macdonald’s EMOTIONAL BANK ACCOUNT” from a championship six or seven months ago.

And where he landed is where I land: “I am a SEAHAWKS fan, not a fan of ONE QUARTERBACK only. If the team goes with Darnold, I am going to ROOT MY HEART OUT FOR HIM.”

And: “if former players in the media say it should be Darnold, if former coaches say it should be Darnold, and if the Seahawks coaching staff says it should be Darnold — THEN I AM GOING TO TRUST THEM.”

And don’t forget the voice that should settle it. Drew Lock himself, after winning: “that’s the life of a backup. WE DON’T WANT TO BE OUT THERE. OUR STAR NEEDS TO STAY HEALTHY. AND I LOVE SAM.”

NOW — the reason to actually be optimistic about Sam Darnold, and twelves, this is the best football argument in the whole breakdown.

“There is a NEW WRINKLE in this offense, a new style THAT COULD MAKE HIS LIFE EASIER. The running game is, in my opinion, FINALLY READY TO GO.”

And the numbers: “in THREE GAMES, Charbonnet is almost at TWO HUNDRED YARDS and he is RUNNING AWAY FROM DEFENDERS. He might not have the same getaway speed that Price has, but THAT IS WHY YOU HAVE A TWO-HEADED BACKFIELD. And the fact that he looks this good COMING OFF ACL SURGERY IS UNBELIEVABLE.”

And the context nobody should skip: “in the games where Charbonnet and Price have had the most success, they were running behind an offensive line MISSING TWO STARTERS IN THE FIRST GAME AND THREE IN THE THIRD GAME. Bobby Hart was getting meaningful snaps.”

Twelves, that’s the detail that makes me believe in this run game.

Twenty carries, ninety-four yards against Las Vegas — behind three replacement starters.

And the honest framing: “I am not trying to attack anyone. I am just saying the Seahawks DID NOT HAVE A RUN GAME LIKE THIS EARLIER IN THE SEASON.”

And then the tribute to Charbonnet that I completely agree with: “an ACL TEAR IS ONE OF THE HARDEST INJURIES FOR A RUNNING BACK, because the entire game depends on CUTTING, ACCELERATING, AND STOPPING ON A DIME. To come back and average close to two hundred yards in three games while making defenders miss is A TESTAMENT TO HIS WORK.”

And Drew Lock’s version stays the best: “when CHARBS does his classic ‘YOU CAN’T TACKLE ME’ thing and shoves people to the ground.” And: “the LOOK IN HIS EYE when you come back to the huddle.”

AND NOW — the chain of logic that explains why this matters for the quarterback, and twelves, this is the most useful football explanation of the week.

“When a team can run the ball, opponents have to ADD DEFENDERS TO THE BOX. When more defenders go into the box, there are MORE ONE-ON-ONE MATCHUPS ON THE OUTSIDE.”

“First, it should make PLAY ACTION work much more effectively. Second — and this is important — it allows more FIFTY-FIFTY BALLS to Jaxon Smith-Njigba, Cooper Kupp, and Rashid Shaheed, who have proven they can WIN those matchups.”

And then the key: “A DEFENSE THAT IS WORRIED ABOUT THE RUN KEEPS ITS SAFETIES CLOSER TO THE LINE. That means FEWER DEFENDERS DEEP, which means SIMPLER READS. A quarterback does not have to hold the ball while he scans the field, because he can LOOK AT THE BOX COUNT, FIND THE MATCHUP HE WANTS, AND THROW.”

Twelves, that is the exact lever Drew Lock described himself: “run the ball, run the ball — forcing them to get into SINGLE HIGH coverages, three or man, and THAT OPENS UP THE PASS GAME. Then you start hitting the pass game, they’ve got to play SHELL, and you come back to the run.”

And Cooper Kupp said the same thing from the receiver’s side: “when you can keep defenses in this place where they are playing two-high, it’s a great opportunity to build around the ball.”

Three separate voices — a quarterback, a receiver, and an analyst — describing the same lever.

And here’s why it matters for Sam Darnold specifically: “when the line is PROTECTING WELL and the run game is WORKING, A QUARTERBACK DOES NOT NEED TO BE PERFECT. He can rely on a few good reads, AVOID THE BIG MISTAKES, and let the supporting cast carry some of the weight. For a quarterback who has struggled with consistency, THAT KIND OF ENVIRONMENT IS IDEAL.”

Twelves, think about what produced those eight interceptions. A hurt glute in Week 2. A cracked rib on Monday night. Three brand-new starters on the offensive line. A thirty-one percent pressure rate against Chicago. And a run game that didn’t exist because both of his running backs were hurt.

Now give that man Charles Cross, Quenton Nelson, a healthy Zach Charbonnet, a healthy Jadarian Price, and the man leading the NFL in receiving yards.

That’s not the same quarterback situation. Not remotely.

NOW — the playoff picture, and twelves, something big just happened in Detroit.

First, the uncomfortable truth: “the Seahawks and Rams are tied JUST OUTSIDE THE PLAYOFF FIELD if the season ended today.”

Outside. Both of them. Six and four.

And the race identified: “it certainly feels like the big wildcard chase will be between SEATTLE, LOS ANGELES, AND GREEN BAY. Green Bay and the Rams still have to PLAY EACH OTHER, so keep an eye on that matchup.”

And here’s a genuinely clever piece of thinking: “IRONICALLY, IT MIGHT BE BETTER FOR SEATTLE IF THE RAMS WIN — because then Seattle can CONTROL ITS OWN DESTINY MORE by playing the Rams and beating them.”

Twelves, that’s sharp, and let me explain why.

Seattle plays the Rams TWICE in the final three weeks — at home on Christmas and then again to close the season. Seattle does NOT play Green Bay.

So if Green Bay is the team ahead of you, you can’t do anything about it. You need help.

But if the RAMS are the team ahead of you, you get two chances to pass them with your own hands.

You’d rather compete with someone you still have to play. That’s always true.

AND NOW — the Detroit news, and it’s enormous.

“The Detroit Lions are the top team in the NFC North, and they are getting MORE INJURY-RIDDLED as the year goes on. AIDAN HUTCHINSON had a celebration that did not feel so fun by the end of the day, because HE GOT HURT. He has a SIGNIFICANT KNEE INJURY AND IS OUT FOR THE YEAR.”

Twelves, Aidan Hutchinson is one of the best defensive players in football. Out for the season.

And the question that follows is the right one: “how does that affect the Lions team that still has A LOT OF NFC NORTH GAMES and difficult games remaining?”

Plus: “the Minnesota Vikings are also dealing with injuries, sending a wide receiver to injured reserve.”

And here’s the structural point that should give you hope: “the teams in the North CANNOT ALL KEEP WINNING, BECAUSE THEY HAVE TO PLAY EACH OTHER.”

That’s just math. Detroit, Green Bay, Minnesota and Chicago are stacked at the top of the wild card picture — and they have to spend December beating each other up. Every North-versus-North game produces a loss for somebody in Seattle’s way.

And the line that ties it together: “it feels like WHILE SEATTLE IS GETTING HEALTHY, OTHER TEAMS ARE NOT.”

That’s the whole case for this bye week in one sentence.

NOW — the schedule plan, and twelves, I like this a lot.

“Seattle still controls its own destiny. JUST WIN. Look at the schedule. There are THREE GAMES AGAINST NFC EAST TEAMS and YOU SHOULD BE THREE AND OH in those. Even if the Seahawks lose to San Francisco, you should still win the next three. Then the question is whether they can beat the PANTHERS and TAKE AT LEAST ONE GAME FROM THE RAMS.”

Let me lay the whole thing out for you. After the bye: at San Francisco on November twenty-ninth. Then the Cowboys at home on Monday Night Football. Then the Giants at home. Then at Philadelphia. Then the Rams at home on Christmas. Then at Carolina. Then the Rams again.

Three NFC East games — Dallas, New York, Philadelphia. Carolina. And the Rams twice.

And the honest math: “winning the three games against the NFC East, beating Carolina, and taking at least one from the Rams would put them in a STRONG POSITION. And that is a plan I like a lot more than HOPING FOR HELP.”

Twelves, that’s five wins out of six games that don’t include San Francisco. That would put Seattle at eleven and six.

That’s a playoff team. And none of it requires anybody else to lose.

And the final note on tiebreakers, which people always forget: “wildcard races are decided by HEAD-TO-HEAD RESULTS AND CONFERENCE RECORDS, which is why the Green Bay and Rams matchup matters to Seattle. Even though the Seahawks are not playing in it, THE RESULT CHANGES WHO IS AHEAD IN THE CHASE and it can affect TIEBREAKERS LATER.”

So yes — watch that game. Just don’t depend on it.
