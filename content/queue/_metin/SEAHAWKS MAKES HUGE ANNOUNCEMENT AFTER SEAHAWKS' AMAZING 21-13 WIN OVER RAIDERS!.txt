NOW — the most important special teams play of Seattle’s season, and a former NFL player says he has never seen it before in his life.

Here’s Ross Tucker describing it:

“You knew that the Raiders did not want those FOUR SECONDS to go off the clock. So we figured they were either going to try to kick a touchback or kick it out of bounds. It’s really only a five-yard difference in that situation. He CRUSHED the kick, and I thought — okay, here’s a touchback.”

“And TORY HORTON GOT ABOUT TEN FEET HIGH OFF THE GROUND to be able to keep that in the field of play. That was GIGANTIC. Truly unique. I’VE NEVER SEEN THAT BEFORE.”

And then: “We always used to joke about having a tall guy try to block field goals by jumping up at the goal post like that. I’VE NEVER SEEN A GUY IN AN NFL GAME OR ANY FOOTBALL GAME DO THAT BEFORE.”

Twelves, let me explain the situation, because it is genuinely clever on both sides.

There were four seconds left before the two-minute mark. Las Vegas did NOT want those four seconds to run off, because Seattle was going to take the ball and sit on the clock.

So the Raiders’ plan was to kick it so deep that it’s a dead ball — a touchback — which stops the clock and preserves those seconds.

And Tory Horton leapt roughly TEN FEET IN THE AIR and swatted the ball back into the field of play.

Because a ball that stays in play means the clock KEEPS RUNNING. Those four seconds disappear. And now Seattle gets the ball inside two minutes instead of before it.

And then — Rashid Shaheed, in his first game back, did what Rashid Shaheed does with the football in his hands.

And twelves, here is what elevates this from a highlight to the identity of this football team.

Mike Macdonald said, in his own press conference: “we TOLD them that was a situation going in. There’s a difference in catching it EIGHT YARDS DEEP IN THE END ZONE and making a play like that.”

They covered it on Saturday. In a meeting.

And then Macdonald added the detail that makes it absurd: “with GEORGE HOLANI GOING DOWN, we had to take JD off of kickoff return. So those are guys that HAVEN’T NECESSARILY PRACTICED A BUNCH IN A WHILE — especially with this game plan.”

So the men executing the most situationally precise play of Seattle’s season weren’t even the regular returners. Holani got hurt. Jadarian Price got pulled off the unit. And the replacements knew the plan anyway.

Remember what Macdonald told us about kickoff value last week? “If you can get a kick stopped inside the 25 versus a touchback or coming out past the 40 — we’re talking ALMOST LIKE A POINT.”

He taught the economics on Wednesday. On Sunday a backup jumped ten feet and cashed it.

AND NOW — the single most damning tactical observation of the game, and it’s about Devon Witherspoon.

“For some reason, the Raiders DID NOT HAVE AN ANSWER for Devon Witherspoon blitzing off the edge ONCE THE WHOLE GAME. That’s a problem, because you KNOW that the Seahawks do that a lot.”

NOT ONCE. The entire game.

And then — because Tucker is a former offensive lineman — he explained exactly what the Raiders failed to do, and twelves, this is a clinic:

“As a former offensive lineman, when a team’s able to bring the SAME BLITZ HOME THREE OR FOUR TIMES IN ONE HALF, boy, that bothers me. Because you’ve got to have an answer for it. And they didn’t.”

“Whether you SLIDE THE PROTECTION to Spoon, or you at least ID HIM and Mendoza knows he needs to THROW HOT — the Raiders did NEITHER. They paid the price for it.”

Let me break down those two options, because this is real football.

Option one: SLIDE THE PROTECTION. You take your five offensive linemen and shift the whole wall toward where the blitz is coming from. Now there’s a body in Witherspoon’s path. Simple. Mechanical.

Option two: ID HIM AND THROW HOT.

And this is the one that matters, because Drew Lock used that exact phrase a week ago. Remember? He described his own job as going “through reads, through progressions, KNOWING YOU’RE HOT.”

“Throwing hot” means this: if you can’t block the blitzer, you accept that he’s coming unblocked — and you have a predetermined quick throw you get to immediately. The ball comes out before he arrives. The blitz costs the defense a defender in coverage, so your hot receiver is open.

It’s a trade. You let him come free, and you punish the space he left.

And the Raiders did NEITHER. They didn’t block him and they didn’t have a hot answer. Three or four times in one half.

And look at how Macdonald described calling it: “he was HOT, you know? So it was just like — all right, what’s on the sheet that we can BRING 21?” And: “I think we called one earlier, he didn’t get there but the ball was out — BUT HE WON HIS RUSH. So we were just like, all right, LET’S KEEP SENDING HIM.”

Twelves, that’s the whole thing in two quotes from two sides.

Macdonald called a Witherspoon blitz, it didn’t get a sack, but his player WON THE MATCHUP — so he kept calling it. And the Raiders never adjusted. Not once.

And Macdonald gave the credit away, as always: “that’s our COACHES’ credit. They saw it, they ENCOURAGED ME to dial it up, and Spoon made it come to life.”

And Tucker’s closing note on the violence of it: “I’m actually STUNNED that Mendoza never fumbled or that he didn’t get hurt, because he took some VICIOUS, VICIOUS SHOTS.”
