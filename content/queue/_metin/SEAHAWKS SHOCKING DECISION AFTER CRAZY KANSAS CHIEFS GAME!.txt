Thirty-six yards.

Seahawks Nation, that’s it. That’s Kenneth Walker the Third’s night. Thirty-six rushing yards on fourteen carries.

The man who came into Lumen Field with 655 rushing yards in five games — the first player since Jim Brown in 1963 to open a season the way he did — went back to Kansas City with thirty-six.

Seahawks 20, Chiefs 3.

The Kansas City Chiefs came in 5-0. They left with their first loss, three points, and 213 total yards.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is 4-3. And I have been waiting all season to make this video. Let’s go.

First, the headline’s “shocking decision” — because there was one, and it was genuinely nervy.

Drew Lock was inactive.

Macdonald after the game: “He’s working through that elbow. Not sure on the severity or how long it’s going to be, but it was best for us right now to kind of try to calm it down the best we can.”

So Seattle played a Sunday night game against the undefeated Kansas City Chiefs without its number two quarterback — the man who went 2-0 earlier this year with four touchdowns and no interceptions. Macdonald said another arm was ready behind Darnold if it came to that, but that was the plan: go into the biggest game of the season one injury away from improvising.

They won by seventeen and Sam Darnold was not sacked a single time.

Sometimes the nervy decision is just the right one.

Now let me go through what happened, because the defense played the best game of Mike Macdonald’s tenure and the record book agrees.

Start with the records, because they’re extraordinary:

This was the first time in a Patrick Mahomes start that the Kansas City Chiefs were kept out of the red zone entirely.

Read that again. Not held to field goals in the red zone. Never got there.

And it keeps going. It was the Chiefs’ lowest total yardage and lowest first-down total in any Mahomes start. 213 yards. Eleven first downs. They went three-of-eleven on third down and ran only fifty plays all night.

And per the AP: the first-half shutout was only the fifth time — playoffs included — that Kansas City has been held scoreless in a half with Mahomes at quarterback.

Fifth time ever. Against a defense that was missing two linebackers.

Macdonald on his group: “Our guys played great, man. They’re just a bunch of warriors. We had some guys that were banged up that played through some things that went into the game.”

They played without Drake Thomas and Chazz Surratt. Nick Emmanwori played through the hip he picked up on Friday. And they held the most explosive offense in football to three points.

Macdonald: “How do you not just tip your cap to those guys?”

Now the streak — and the reason it survived.

Seattle has not allowed a 100-yard rusher in thirty-six consecutive games. It was thirty-five coming in. Kenneth Walker the Third, averaging 131 yards a game, got thirty-six.

And Macdonald explained the plan in one sentence that’s worth keeping: “We have to play in waves... because I don’t know, he’s still going to break tackles. I’m sure he broke a host of tackles. That’s what he does. And so we had to get people to the football.”

“Get people to the football.” Not stop him at the line — accept that he’ll break the first tackle and make sure there are three more men arriving. That’s the light box, two-high philosophy he explained last week: “more people doesn’t equal better run defense.”

He told you exactly what he was going to do, and then he did it, and it worked.

And here’s the other half of it, which I want to flag because we talked about it all week: Walker only got fourteen carries. Not because Seattle stuffed every one — because Seattle had the ball for thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds.

Seattle’s offense was Seattle’s run defense. Thirty-six minutes of possession means Kansas City ran fifty plays. Fifty. You cannot hand a running back the ball twenty-five times when you only have the ball for twenty-four minutes.

That’s complementary football, and it’s arithmetic, exactly like we said.

Now Jaxon Smith-Njigba, because he is doing something historic and last night he made it undeniable.

Nine catches. 150 yards. A 28-yard touchdown. And 129 of those yards came in the first half — he’d already broken the game before halftime.

That’s his sixth hundred-yard game in seven games. He now has 867 receiving yards on the season.

And here’s the company he’s keeping. With 800-plus receiving yards and eight touchdowns through seven games, Jaxon Smith-Njigba joins exactly two other players in NFL history: Jerry Rice in 1986, and Cooper Kupp in 2021.

Jerry Rice. Cooper Kupp. And Jaxon Smith-Njigba. And Cooper Kupp is standing in the same huddle.

Macdonald, asked how he does it week after week: “I don’t know. I mean, he’s just — he’s tremendous. He’s tremendous. I don’t know how else to describe him.”

And then he said something that deserves respect: “He’s had some adversity this week. I’ll leave it like that. But just really proud of him as a person. He’s just a person, competitor, teammate. How do you not have the most respect for the guy and love him for how he operates?”

“I’ll leave it like that.” Something happened in that man’s life this week and his head coach drew a line around it. I’m going to respect that line too — that’s not our business, and he went out and put up 150 yards anyway.

And on the touchdown, Macdonald gave away the whole play: “I have pretty good confidence that if we’re throwing him the ball, he’s going to come down with it. But when you see him singled like that, and based off that play call, I knew that’s where Sam was going to go with the ball.”

Singled. One-on-one. Against a Kansas City defense that knew exactly who he was — and one of the great defensive coordinators in football left him single-covered, and Seattle made them pay instantly.

Now Sam Darnold. And after the month he’s had, this is the part I most wanted to tell you.

Twenty of twenty-eight. 257 yards. One touchdown. One interception.

And the number that matters more than all of them:

Sam Darnold was not sacked. Not once. And he was hit twice.

Twice. After five sacks against San Francisco. Five sacks against Denver. Fourteen on the season. He got hit twice in sixty minutes against a Steve Spagnuolo defense.

Macdonald: “I thought we ran the ball more effectively... This was just cleaner football. Thought our protection plan was great.”

And he threw in a detail I loved: Darnold scrambled for first downs on two separate third-and-longs. That’s a quarterback who, nine days ago, was being told he was finished, keeping two drives alive with his legs.

The one blemish was real: an interception in the end zone at the end of the first half. Macdonald acknowledged it: “We know we missed an opportunity there.” And Seattle’s red zone was genuinely poor — three trips inside the twenty produced six points, two short field goals and that pick.

But remember what Darnold told you on Thursday? He said the problem was “the eighth play” of a drive — stringing six, seven, eight plays together and then failing on third down.

Seattle went eleven for sixteen on third down last night.

Eleven of sixteen. Sixty-nine percent. Kansas City went three of eleven. The quarterback named the disease on Thursday and the team cured it on Sunday.

Macdonald: “Third down’s been a point of emphasis over the last few weeks, especially this week.” And on the staff: “Brian led the charge, and I thought we came up with some really great ideas and solutions.” That’s Brian Fleury, the first-year coordinator everybody wanted to blame.

And Macdonald on his quarterback: “That’s Sam. Don’t expect anything more or less from him. He’s just a ball player, man... proud of the way he handled it. He’s part of the solution on how we play.”

“Part of the solution.” Third week in a row he’s used that exact phrase. He never stopped saying it, not once, through all of it.

Now the running game — and Anthony Bradford, who I have been shouting about for two weeks.

Emanuel Wilson: twenty-four carries, 100 yards, and a 21-yard touchdown run in the fourth quarter. The best game of his career in Seattle.

And Seattle rushed for 153 yards on 41 carries as a team. Forty-one carries. For a team that had been twenty-ninth in the league in rush EPA, that is a transformation in one week.

What changed? The right guard came back.

Macdonald on Bradford: “I think so. I think so. I can’t wait to watch the tape. I know he got through the game well. So it’s great to have him back.”

Zero sacks. A hundred and fifty-three rushing yards. Forty-one carries. Thirty-five minutes of possession. That is what a healthy starting five looks like, and it’s why I kept telling you the answer was a guard and not a trade.

And the way it ended was perfect. Wilson got to a hundred yards on a late carry, and then on fourth-and-one inside a yard, Macdonald went for it — and handed it to A.J. Barner.

His explanation: “We didn’t want to put the ball in jeopardy there on the fourth down. So we were going to go for it regardless, but inside a yard, we felt like give it to AJ.”

“We were going for it regardless.” Up seventeen, four minutes left, and he’s going for it on fourth down to end the game on his own terms instead of punting it back to Patrick Mahomes. That’s a coach who has decided he’s not going to be nervous anymore.

And on Barner generally: “AJ’s always within the plan. Believe it or not, we are trying to get him the ball... he’s a tough guy to bring down once he gets the ball in his hands.”

Now the turnover, and the man who caused it.

Ty Okada intercepted Patrick Mahomes — and Nick Emmanwori helped force the errant throw. Emmanwori finished with four tackles and a pass breakup, playing on a hip that was new on Friday’s injury report.

A reporter listed what he did in the first half alone: covered Kenneth Walker in the flat on third down, and guarded Xavier Worthy down the field. Macdonald’s response: “Yeah, he’s pretty good.”

And then he said something thoughtful about how you use a player like that: “As coaches, we bear the responsibility of trying to have him — and really our best players — affect the game the best we can without overloading him. That’s a burden that we carry every week.”

“Without overloading him.” That’s a coach protecting a young player from his own versatility. And he named the others in the same breath: “Murf” — Byron Murphy the Second — “Leo” — Leonard Williams — the edge guys, and “Spoons,” Devon Witherspoon.

Now the injuries, because there’s a cost.

Charles Cross and Rashid Shaheed both left and didn’t finish. Macdonald didn’t have details: “I just don’t have the information. I think Charles has got an ankle... I think Rashid is his knee.”

Charles Cross is the starting left tackle. Rashid Shaheed is the man who had the 42-yard catch, the two-point conversion and the 31-yard punt return in Denver. Losing either for any length of time matters, and we’ll know more this week.

Macdonald also noted the offense had to adjust mid-game: “With Rashid going down, being able to kind of evolve and pivot and get to some other answers as the game started to clear. Some really good stuff by our offensive staff.”

And Drew Lock’s elbow is still an open question. No timeline.

Now — the part that tells you this team isn’t getting ahead of itself.

Macdonald could have spent ten minutes celebrating. Instead, listen to how much of his own press conference he spent on what’s still broken:

“There’s so much more opportunity for us to improve... Just some procedure things, you know, red zone offense. You guys know the things.”

And: “I think our guys understand that there’s more levels to where we can go. And so I hope they feel the sense of urgency that we can get there, because it is stuff that we can control. I’m talking about procedural stuff, red zone stuff, negative plays, how we rush, our red zone defense.”

He won 20-3 against the best team in football and gave you a list of five things that need fixing.

And he was honest about the noise: “You lose, you know, three or four or something — there’s probably as much noise as they’ve heard in a while. But it didn’t affect them, and it didn’t really affect our operation.”

“It didn’t affect them.” Two weeks of the internet calling for his quarterback’s job, and the locker room that was holding its own player-organized cross-position meetings just went out and won by seventeen.

And then the line that’s going on the wall: “We still have — we’re still a championship caliber team, and that’s what we’re chasing.”

Plus the thing he said twice: “That was 12 as one right there.” He means “twelve as one” — the whole philosophy, and he credited you for it: “It was a great environment. So shout out to the twelves.”

And one more detail I loved, because it’s pure Macdonald. Asked about the big plays, he pointed to blocking and tackling by guys who don’t get credit: George Holani on the sideline, A.J. Barner on the sideline, and Charles Cross chasing on the interception return. His words: “Every yard matters in that situation. We needed it going into that field goal attempt. To get the guy to cut back so George could tackle him — that’s 12-as-one football, man.”

He singled out an offensive tackle making a tackle. That’s who he is.

And let me put the whole night in one comparison, because the two stat lines side by side are almost absurd.

Seattle: 410 total yards. Twenty-four first downs. Forty-one rushing attempts. Eleven of sixteen on third down. Sixty-nine plays. Two punts. Thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds of possession.

Kansas City: 213 total yards. Eleven first downs. Three of eleven on third down. Fifty plays.

Seattle ran nineteen more plays, gained almost two hundred more yards, and had the ball for twelve minutes longer than the best offense in football.

Two punts. All night. Against Steve Spagnuolo.

And remember what Darnold said about that defense on Thursday: “They attack offenses and they don’t allow the offense to attack them.” He named the thing he had to beat — and then Seattle spent thirty-six minutes doing the attacking.

And here’s the one I can’t get over: Kansas City never reached the red zone.

Think about what that requires. It isn’t a goal-line stand. It’s eleven men keeping Patrick Mahomes and Travis Kelce and Kenneth Walker outside the twenty-yard line for four full quarters. Not once. Not on a short field, not off a turnover, not on a two-minute drive.

Never in a Mahomes start had that happened before. And Seattle did it with two linebackers in street clothes.

And one honest asterisk, because I promised you both sides all week. Kansas City was genuinely banged up: Rashee Rice was out, Travis Kelce was limited all week with an ankle, Brashard Smith was out, and they were down at least one starting offensive tackle with Jaylon Moore out and Josh Simmons reportedly on injured reserve.

Does that take something off this win? A little. It doesn’t take much. Because Seattle was missing Charbonnet, Drake Thomas, Chazz Surratt and its backup quarterback, lost its starting left tackle and its best return man during the game — and still won by seventeen while holding a team out of the red zone.

Both teams were hurt. One of them scored three points.

So where are we?

Seattle is 4-3. The three losses are still by a combined eight points, and now there’s a seventeen-point win over a 5-0 team on top of them.

Next up: the Chicago Bears, November second. Macdonald: “It’ll be a fun next couple days and we’ll get to work on Chicago.”

And the trade deadline is November tenth — two weeks out. And after watching Seattle run for 153 yards with a healthy line, I’d guess John Schneider just got a lot less interested in paying a premium for a running back. The fix really was in the building.

Also coming: Jadarian Price’s practice window can open this week.

Let me close.

Nine days ago this team lost in overtime in Denver, its quarterback led the league in interceptions, and I was being asked to make videos about him wanting out of Seattle.

Last night: zero sacks. Two hits. Eleven of sixteen on third down. A hundred and fifty-three rushing yards. Thirty-six minutes of possession. Kansas City kept out of the red zone for the first time ever with Mahomes under center. Thirty-six games and counting without a hundred-yard rusher. And a receiver in the company of Jerry Rice.

The fix wasn’t a trade. It wasn’t a new quarterback. It was a right guard, a third-down plan, and a week of honest meetings.

Macdonald told you on Wednesday: “You can’t control starting fast. You can control your mentality, how well you’re on your assignments, how well we’re aligned, what we’re going to call and when.”

They controlled what they could control. And they beat the best team in football by seventeen.

Go through, not around.
