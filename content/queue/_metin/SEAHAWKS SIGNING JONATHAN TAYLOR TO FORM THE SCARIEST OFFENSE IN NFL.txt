You cannot sign Jonathan Taylor.

Seahawks Nation, I want to start there, because it’s the most important sentence in this video and almost nobody is saying it.

Jonathan Taylor is not a free agent. He signed a brand-new extension in August. He is under contract through 2028, with a potential fourth year after that. Seattle cannot sign him, cannot bid on him, cannot offer him a contract.

If Seattle wants Jonathan Taylor, Seattle has to trade for him — and that means draft picks going out the door and somebody else’s contract coming in.

But here’s why you should stay: the report is real, it’s sourced, and the number it would cost Seattle for the rest of this season is a lot smaller than you think. I’m going to show you the actual math.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is 4-3, we just beat the Kansas City Chiefs 20-3, and the trade deadline is November tenth — two weeks from today. Let’s do this properly.

First: what was actually reported, and by whom.

Adam Schefter of ESPN reported that the Seattle Seahawks are among the teams “expected to consider” a trade for Colts running back Jonathan Taylor. The other teams named: Denver, Minnesota, and Green Bay.

That’s the report. And twelves, I need you to hear the exact wording, because wording is everything in this business.

“Expected to consider.”

That is the single weakest construction in the insider dictionary. It does not mean a deal exists. It does not mean an offer was made. It does not mean the two teams have spoken. It means a very well-connected reporter believes Seattle will think about it.

That is a real report, from a real source, and I’m treating it as exactly what it is — no more. Nobody has said Seattle is signing him, trading for him, or close to anything.

Now — why is Jonathan Taylor available at all? Because Indianapolis just blew up its front office.

Here’s the part of this story that actually makes it plausible, and it’s not about Taylor:

The Colts fired general manager Chris Ballard. On the Monday after a 26-20 home loss to the Tennessee Titans, with the team 2-4, in what would have been Ballard’s tenth season.

Assistant general manager Ed Dodds is now overseeing the personnel department. Head coach Shane Steichen and his staff now report directly to ownership.

Twelves, stop and think about what that means two weeks before a trade deadline.

An interim personnel chief has exactly one incentive, and it is to accumulate draft picks. He is not protecting a 2-4 roster. He is not protecting the previous general manager’s contracts. He is auditioning for the permanent job, and the way you audition is by turning expiring, expensive veterans into 2027 capital.

That is why Jonathan Taylor’s name is in the air. Not because Indianapolis soured on him. Because Indianapolis changed the man who decides.

And it’s not just Taylor. Quenton Nelson — a thirty-year-old, eight-time Pro Bowl guard — has been floated too. I’ll get to him.

Now the money. And this is the part I promised you, so pay attention, because it’s going to surprise you.

Jonathan Taylor signed a two-year, forty-four-million-dollar extension with Indianapolis in August. Thirty-nine million of it is guaranteed. That’s about twenty-two million a year in new money — top-of-the-market running back money.

Here is his base salary schedule as reported: seven million dollars in 2026. Sixteen million in 2027. Eighteen million in 2028.

Now here’s the mechanic that nobody explains, and it’s the only thing that matters in an in-season trade:

Signing-bonus proration does not travel in a trade. Base salary does.

What that means in plain English: Indianapolis paid Jonathan Taylor’s signing bonus, and Indianapolis keeps every dollar of the cap charge for it. When they trade him, all of that remaining proration accelerates onto the Colts’ 2026 cap at once. Seattle doesn’t inherit a cent of it.

What Seattle would inherit is the base salary — and only the part that hasn’t been paid yet.

Base salaries are paid out across the eighteen weeks of the regular season. A trade right after Week 8 means roughly ten of eighteen weeks remain. Ten-eighteenths of seven million dollars is about three point nine million dollars.

Three point nine million dollars.

Twelves, that is what Jonathan Taylor would cost Seattle’s salary cap for the rest of this season. Less than four million dollars for a three-time Pro Bowl running back, for ten games and a playoff run, by a defending Super Bowl champion.

That’s the cheap part. Now here’s the expensive part, and it’s two separate bills.

Bill one is the draft picks, and nobody has reported a price. Not a round, not a conditional, nothing. One analysis said Taylor would likely bring Indianapolis “the biggest return” of anything they have to sell — which is a polite way of saying this will hurt.

Bill two is 2027 and 2028: sixteen million, then eighteen million. Thirty-four million dollars across two seasons for a running back who turns twenty-eight in January.

And here’s the honest limit of what I know, and I’m telling you instead of pretending: I do not know how much of that 2027 and 2028 base salary is guaranteed. Thirty-nine million of the forty-four was guaranteed at signing, but I have not seen the year-by-year structure. And that one fact is the whole decision. If 2027 is guaranteed, Seattle isn’t renting a running back for ten games — Seattle is marrying one. If it isn’t, this is a cheap rental with an option attached.

Anybody who calls this a no-brainer without knowing that number is guessing.

Now the scouting report — and twelves, I have to be honest with you about this one, because it’s not what the hype says.

Jonathan Taylor is not having his best season.

Through the Colts’ first stretch of games: 398 rushing yards and six touchdowns, averaging 79.6 yards a game — tenth in the NFL. That’s a good back.

But: he is averaging 3.9 yards per carry. His career average is 4.8.

And he has not had a hundred-yard rushing game in eleven months.

And this one bothers me most: his third-down conversion rate is 33.3 percent. His career mark is 47.3 percent. On the exact downs you’d be buying him for.

By the numbers the fan reports are using, through six games he’s at 492 rushing yards on 122 carries — that’s four point oh yards a carry. Below his career number, by a lot.

So why is he still worth talking about? Two words: eleven touchdowns.

Seven rushing touchdowns, plus fifteen catches for 163 yards and four more scores. Eleven total touchdowns in six games. On a 2-4 team with no quarterback stability. That is a man who still finishes drives even when he’s not ripping off chunk runs.

And remember what he did a year ago: 1,585 rushing yards and a career-high-matching 18 rushing touchdowns. That’s not ancient history. That’s last season.

Now here’s the most interesting thing I found all week, and it’s the argument for the trade hiding inside the argument against it.

Indianapolis’ running backs coach, DeAndre Smith, explained why Taylor’s numbers are down. His explanation: when defenses don’t have to blitz, they keep seven men back — and Taylor has to run through several layers of defenders. And this year the Colts lack explosive threats, so defenses just focus on him. Pittsburgh played a five-man front against him.

Twelves, do you hear it?

Jonathan Taylor’s problem is that nobody is afraid of the rest of his offense.

That is the exact opposite of Seattle’s situation. Seattle has Jaxon Smith-Njigba — a man with 867 receiving yards and eight touchdowns in seven games, who national analysts just called the best receiver in football, who draws a safety wherever he lines up. You cannot put seven men in the box against Seattle. JSN makes that illegal.

So the real question isn’t is Jonathan Taylor still good. The question is: how much of his 3.9 yards a carry is him, and how much of it is Indianapolis? And honestly? Most of it looks like Indianapolis.

Now let me give you the case against, fairly, because a smart person made it.

One national ranking put the four Taylor landing spots in order — Denver first, Minnesota second, Seattle third, Green Bay fourth — and argued Seattle “would be better off sticking with that committee.”

The reasoning: Zach Charbonnet is nearing a return, and Jadarian Price could come off injured reserve as early as Week 8. In other words — Seattle’s running back room isn’t broken, it’s injured, and injured is temporary.

And that’s a real argument. Zach Charbonnet has not played a single down this season. He practiced fully last week and was still ruled out against Kansas City. Jadarian Price is on injured reserve with a chest injury. Neither of those men is gone. Both of them are coming back.

Why would you pay a premium for a position you’re about to have three healthy bodies at?

And then there’s the film from Sunday night, which is the best counterargument of all — and it’s mine.

Twelves, I’ve been telling you this for two weeks and Sunday night proved it:

Emanuel Wilson: twenty-four carries, 100 yards, a 21-yard touchdown in the fourth quarter. The team: forty-one carries for 153 rushing yards. Zero sacks allowed. Thirty-five minutes and fifty-one seconds of possession. And a drive where Seattle burned four minutes and forty-six seconds off the clock running the football at a team that knew it was coming — and scored.

Against the Kansas City Chiefs. With Emanuel Wilson and George Holani. No Charbonnet, no Price, no Jonathan Taylor.

So what changed? Anthony Bradford came back at right guard.

One guard. That’s the variable. A team that was twenty-ninth in rush EPA ran forty-one times for 153 yards and ran out the clock on the best team in football, because the right guard was healthy.

If the problem was the offensive line, then trading a premium pick for a running back is paying to fix something that already fixed itself.

But I’m going to argue the other side too, because I don’t do one-sided videos.

Here’s the case for being aggressive, and it’s strong:

One. Seattle is 18th in the NFL in yards per carry and 17th in total offense. Sunday night was one game. One game does not erase seven weeks.

Two. The depth is one ankle deep. Charbonnet hasn’t played. Price is on IR. If Emanuel Wilson goes down, Seattle’s running back room is George Holani and a prayer. And Seattle just lost Charles Cross to an ankle and Rashid Shaheed to a knee in the same game. Injuries are not hypothetical for this team right now.

Three. Seattle is the defending Super Bowl champion.

Say it out loud. This franchise won Super Bowl LX. And national analysts just spent Sunday night calling this defense “a Super Bowl defense” after holding Patrick Mahomes to three points and keeping Kansas City out of the red zone for four full quarters.

You do not sit on a window like that. When you have a championship defense and a receiver on a Jerry Rice pace, the picks are worth less than the year is. That’s not recklessness, that’s arithmetic.

Four. Look who you’re bidding against. Denver — the team that beat Seattle in overtime two weeks ago. Minnesota — currently 7-0 and the last undefeated team in the NFL. Green Bay.

Every one of those teams is a direct competitor. And Minnesota, at 7-0, can offer Taylor something Seattle can’t: an undefeated team. If you don’t bid, somebody in your conference does — and you watch Jonathan Taylor run against your defense in January.

Now Quenton Nelson, because the second name is actually the more interesting one.

Quenton Nelson. Thirty years old. Eight-time Pro Bowl guard. One of the best interior offensive linemen of this entire era.

He was asked about the rumors after Ballard’s firing, and his answer was pure class. He told the Indianapolis Star: “I have no questions.” He said he leaves the matter to God while he concentrates on playing his best. And he said he loves the organization and has built friendships there he doesn’t take for granted.

That’s a man who has no interest in being traded. Which doesn’t mean he won’t be — players don’t get a vote. But it tells you he isn’t pushing.

And the case for Seattle wanting him is real: Grey Zabel has struggled in his second year. Anthony Bradford just came back from missing five games and is in the final year of his rookie contract. Seattle’s line regressed from last season. Nelson would be the best lineman on the roster the day he walked in, and he’d help the run game and the pass protection simultaneously — which no running back can do.

But here’s my problem with it, and it’s the same problem as Taylor.

Zero sacks against Steve Spagnuolo. A hundred and fifty-three rushing yards. Forty-one carries. The week Bradford came back.

The line’s issue may have been availability, not ability. And Grey Zabel is a second-year player who was a first-round pick. You don’t give up premium capital to replace a developing first-rounder eight games into year two. You coach him.

And one piece of housekeeping, because people get this wrong constantly: if you’re imagining Seattle paying for all this with compensatory picks — you can’t trade a comp pick before it’s awarded. They don’t exist as tradeable assets until the league hands them out in the spring. Whatever Seattle spends here comes out of real, already-owned draft capital.

So what would I actually do?

Here’s my honest answer, and it’s not the exciting one.

I’d make the call. I wouldn’t make the trade — not at a premium.

At three point nine million dollars for the rest of the season, you absolutely pick up the phone. That price is nothing for a defending champion. If Indianapolis’s interim decision-maker is in a hurry to collect picks and the price comes down to something reasonable — a mid-round pick, a conditional — you do it instantly and you don’t think twice.

But if the price is a first, or a second plus real future money, and Taylor is at 3.9 yards a carry, and Charbonnet and Price are both walking back through the door in two weeks, and your line just produced 153 yards with the backs you already have?

Then the smart move is the boring one. You keep the picks.

Because here’s the thing, twelves: the mistake this front office is accused of was letting Kenneth Walker the Third walk in free agency. And on Sunday night, Seattle’s defense held Kenneth Walker the Third to thirty-six yards on fourteen carries while Seattle’s own back ran for a hundred.

Sometimes the answer to a running back problem isn’t a running back.
