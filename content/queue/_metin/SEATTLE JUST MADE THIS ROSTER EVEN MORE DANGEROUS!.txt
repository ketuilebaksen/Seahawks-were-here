Seattle never made an aggressive push to bring Riq Woolen back in free agency. He hit the market, and this organization let him walk to Philadelphia.

And I want to be fair about why. Woolen was genuinely maddening at times. There were careless decisions on the field. There's been reporting that people inside that building felt he needed a fresh start. Those are real things and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

But talent doesn't disappear because a locker room changes.

And right now Woolen is one of the breakout stories of Eagles camp, flashing exactly the brilliance that made him must-watch television in Seattle. So what did Seattle do instead? They scrambled. They signed Terrion Arnold. Then they traded for Trevon Diggs.

And here's the part I keep coming back to. Those were reactive moves, not planned ones. Diggs arrived on August 19th — nineteen days before the season. That's not roster construction. That's triage.

Now here's why losing Woolen matters more than his stat line suggests, and this is the smartest point in this whole conversation.

Devon Witherspoon is a legitimate star. But think about how Mike Macdonald actually uses him. He's most dangerous rushing the passer and defending the run. He's the guy blitzing off the slot thirteen times in a Super Bowl. He's a chess piece, not a chess square.

Woolen's presence is what allowed that. When you have a corner who can lock a side of the field by himself, your best defensive back gets to roam.

Take Woolen off the roster, and suddenly Witherspoon's flexibility gets squeezed into a box he is far too talented to be stuck in.

That's the hidden cost. It's not just one corner. It's what one corner unlocks for everybody else.

Meanwhile Josh Jobe steps into a full-time starting role for the first time in his career. Jobe and Witherspoon could genuinely be one of the better cornerback duos in this league.

Trevon Diggs was once one of the most feared corners in football. He led the league with 11 interceptions in 2021. But he's battled knee issues across five straight seasons, and he's starting this year on the practice squad still proving his body can hold up.

And it points at something bigger.

Because this isn't just a cornerback problem. There's a structural pattern here that this franchise has lived through before.

Every team deals with injuries. The quality of your depth is often what separates a champion from a pretender.

San Francisco proved that point directly last season. They weathered major injuries because their bench was actually built to survive them. Seattle watched that happen from the other sideline.

And now, entering 2026, there's a real argument that Seattle's roster construction looks closer to the old Pete Carroll approach than San Francisco's disciplined model.

Here's the history for anybody who wasn't watching closely.

From 2010 through 2023, Pete Carroll had final say over the roster. And the early magic of that era was built on exactly one skill — turning undrafted free agents and late-round picks into real contributors. That's how the Legion of Boom happened. Fifth rounders and no-names becoming All-Pros.

But that magic faded over time. And the inability to keep finding hidden gems became one of the reasons that run eventually ended.

Now John Schneider controls the roster, working alongside Mike Macdonald. And to be completely fair, he's taken a visibly different approach — proactively extending core players a full year before their contracts expired.

Jaxon Smith-Njigba. Derick Hall. Devon Witherspoon. All locked up early, before the market could set a higher price.

That's genuinely smart business. That's the opposite of the late-Carroll problem.

But the depth behind those stars tells a different story heading into the opener.

We just went through the corners. Now look at running back, because it might be worse.

Kenneth Walker III — the Super Bowl MVP — left for Kansas City in free agency. Zach Charbonnet is still recovering from that playoff knee injury and is on the PUP list, meaning he misses at least the first four games.

Neither one of them is available for the opening stretch of this season.

That leaves rookie Jadarian Price, George Holani, and journeyman Emanuel Wilson carrying the load at the single most physically demanding position in football.

And Jacardia Wright — the man who ripped off 81 yards on eight carries with that 69-yard touchdown against Tennessee — got waived on cutdown day.

So if Jadarian Price goes down, a run-heavy offense is in serious trouble very fast.

There's a legitimate argument that Schneider should have chased a more proven veteran back instead of banking entirely on unproven names. And if the injuries pile up, that decision is going to look an awful lot like a classic late-Carroll gamble.

Now let me give you the other side, because I don't want to spend thirteen minutes doom-posting about a Super Bowl champion.

None of this means the season is doomed. The starters are genuinely good enough to compete at the absolute highest level — this is still the defense that led the entire NFL in points allowed and in DVOA. Ten of the twelve defenders who earned elite grades last season are back.

And every one of these depth questions could resolve itself without a single incident. Arnold's situation could clear. Diggs' knees could hold. Charbonnet could come back in week five and everything looks fine in hindsight.

But championship windows close fast. And if the backups can't hold the line when a starter goes down in October, we could be watching that old pattern repeat in real time.

Which brings me to a move this week that nobody is talking about — and that I think is the most revealing transaction Seattle has made in a month.

Seattle re-signed safety DeAnthony Bell to the practice squad. And in the corresponding move, cut linebacker Marvin Jones.

On the surface, that's routine. That's the kind of paperwork that happens on every team every single week.

But look closer, because this one says everything.

Bell's path back here was not a straight line. He originally signed with Cleveland as an undrafted free agent out of Iowa Central after the 2022 draft — and he stuck on that roster for three straight seasons, building a reputation as a reliable special teams contributor.

When the Browns declined to tender him as a restricted free agent in 2025, he signed a one-year deal with Seattle. He appeared in 14 games last season and put up 15 total tackles, half a sack and a tackle for loss.

Solid. Unspectacular. Exactly what a rotational safety looks like.

Late in the season Seattle waived him, and Carolina claimed him almost immediately. And now, less than a year later, he's back in this building.

Why?

Because the coaching staff wanted somebody who already knows Mike Macdonald's calls without needing an adjustment period.

And now think about the timing.

Bud Clark, the 64th overall pick, broke his ankle in Nashville and is out for months. Nick Emmanwori is still working his way back from ankle surgery and hadn't practiced as of that report. Seattle is carrying only three safeties on the 53-man roster.

Three.

DeAnthony Bell is not a flashy addition. Nobody is making a highlight reel about this transaction. But he is exactly the kind of experienced, system-savvy body a coaching staff wants sitting one phone call away when injuries start piling up in November.

And look at the rest of that practice squad, because the pattern is unmistakable.

Trevon Diggs is there. AJ Finley is there. Maxen Hook and Rodney Thomas II round out the safety depth. Add Emmanuel Henderson Jr. and Ricky White III at receiver, and you can see exactly which position groups this front office decided to protect.

Seattle is stacking defensive backs. Deliberately. In volume.

That is a front office telling you, without a press conference, that they know precisely where this roster is most vulnerable.

And here's what I've come to appreciate about that. Championship teams are not built on splashy moves alone. They're built on exactly this kind of quiet insurance policy — the kind that never trends, that nobody clips, that shows up on an injury report in November when it actually matters.
