“If they can get into the tournament, this team is still good enough that they could win the whole thing.”

That’s a national analyst, on national television, about the Seattle Seahawks. And the other one went further — he called them a top four team in the National Football League.

Seahawks Nation, those words were said BEFORE Sunday. Before Seattle beat Arizona seventeen to three with six starters in street clothes.

And twelves, here’s why this video is different from every reaction video you’ll watch this week: this entire national panel broke down Seattle’s problems in detail — the offensive line, the running backs, the quarterback, the receivers — and then Seattle went out on Sunday and answered almost every single one of them.

So we’re going to do something fun. I’m going to give you their exact arguments, and then I’m going to check each one against the tape from Sunday.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Seattle is five and four. Las Vegas is next. Let’s go.

First, I owe you honesty about the title. “Super Bowl monster” at five and four is a stretch and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

But “top four team” is a real quote from a real national analyst, and so is “they could win the whole thing.” That part is completely accurate. Let’s hear his reasoning, because it’s better than you’d expect.

ARGUMENT ONE: the Bears loss was a bad spot, not a bad team.

Here’s the quote: “Seattle’s fine. Like Kansas City, it’d be nice if they had another receiver in the building, but last night they’re playing musical chairs with the offensive line. They don’t have their left tackle, left guard to right guard. That’s not going to work with Jeffery Simmons and Montez Sweat, who are game wreckers.”

And then he stacked the circumstances: “It was the first game back for their running backs. It was their third straight prime time game.”

And the kicker: “We told you all week it was one of my favorite plays of the week. Everybody, all the sharps were on Chicago last night.”

Twelves, let me translate that last part, because it’s important.

“All the sharps were on Chicago.” In betting language, “sharps” are the professional bettors — the people who move lines for a living. And he’s saying the smart money saw this coming all week.

And he was right, and I was wrong. I picked Seattle twenty-four, Chicago twenty. It was thirty-four to seventeen. I’ve owned that every single video since and I’ll own it again here.

But look at WHY he said Seattle would lose, because this is the whole thing:

“Musical chairs with the offensive line.”

He’s describing exactly what I warned about. No Charles Cross at left tackle. Grey Zabel pulled off the left side and moved to right guard. A brand-new left guard who’d been in the building nine days. Against Jeffery Simmons — a man Chicago traded two first-round picks and a fourth to acquire — and Montez Sweat.

“That’s not going to work.” He said it plainly, and it didn’t.

And here’s his conclusion: “Seattle’s got too many good players, too good of a coach, too much talent. Last night was a bad spot. I still think they are a top four team.”

A TOP FOUR TEAM. After a seventeen-point home loss.

Now — CHECK IT AGAINST SUNDAY.

On Sunday, Seattle played Arizona with no Charles Cross, no Quenton Nelson, and a THIRD different interior line in two weeks — Anthony Bradford back at right guard, Grey Zabel back at left guard.

And they did not allow a single sack.

Macdonald’s words after: “with the movement that we’ve had across our line in the last two weeks, to kind of come together and especially finish the game the way they did was really cool.”

So the analyst’s theory held up perfectly. The line was the problem. Fix the line, fix the team. One week later, the line held and Seattle won by fourteen.

ARGUMENT TWO: the playoff math — and this is the most useful thing anybody said.

“This is going to pivot for me with Seattle. It’s still just a ‘get into the tournament.’ You’ve got to just do whatever you can. If you can get into the tournament, this team is still good enough that they could win the whole thing. But you’ve got to fight your way to get into the tournament.”

“Just get into the tournament.”

Twelves, this is the single most important strategic idea for the rest of Seattle’s season and I want to make sure you really understand it.

The NFL playoffs are not a reward for the best regular season. They’re a tournament. And in a single-elimination tournament, the gap between the fourth-best team and the first-best team is almost nothing, because one bad quarter eliminates anybody.

So the argument is: Seattle should stop worrying about seeding, division titles, home field, all of it. The only job is get in. Because once you’re in, this roster — with its defense, its coaching, and Jaxon Smith-Njigba — can beat anybody on a given Sunday.

And at five and four, that’s exactly the right framing. Seattle is not winning the number one seed. Seattle doesn’t need to. It needs to survive to January and then be dangerous.

ARGUMENT THREE: the Quenton Nelson evaluation — and this is the best football analysis in the whole segment.

Asked what he thought of the trade then and now, after watching one game:

“I loved it. I loved the trade. And look, he’s going to be more of an impact to the run game than the pass game — either, in both areas he’s a massive upgrade of where they were, but his real strength is going to be what he can do in the run game. They’ve got to get that going.”

And then the specific thing he saw on tape: “You saw him move some people. Well — you saw him create a lot of movement on down blocks, kind of wiping defensive tackles down.”

Twelves, “wiping defensive tackles down” is a beautiful piece of scouting language and I want to explain it.

A down block is when an offensive lineman blocks a defender inside of him — away from the play. The point isn’t to drive him backward. The point is to wash him sideways, down the line, out of the hole.

And “wiping” is exactly the verb. When a guard like Quenton Nelson executes a down block, he doesn’t just occupy the defensive tackle — he removes him from the geography of the play entirely. He erases a man.

That’s why Nelson is a run-game addition first. He’s not primarily a pass protector upgrade. He’s a man who creates a hole by deleting the person standing in it.

And this connects directly to what Macdonald said: “considering the newness, he looked like Quenton Nelson. And a lot of good stuff in the run game.”

Two completely independent evaluations — a national analyst and the head coach — watched the same tape and said the same thing: the guard was fine. The problem was elsewhere.

ARGUMENT FOUR: the running backs — and here’s where Sunday proved them wrong.

“I thought Charbonnet did some really good things, but I thought he was a little rusty after having been gone for a while. I thought Price was a little hesitant on a couple runs. And some of that, hopefully it’s just a matter of him getting back into the flow of things. But there was — I thought ‘rust’ was kind of like the word I just kept coming back to.”

“Rust was the word I kept coming back to.”

And that was fair! It was completely fair. Macdonald told us himself: Zach Charbonnet “hasn’t had a live rep since the injury.” Not one, since January. Of course he looked rusty. He’d played eight months of nothing.

NOW CHECK SUNDAY.

Macdonald after the Cardinals game: “Definitely felt Zach in his power, and the ability to break tackles and own the sideline there.”

And on Jadarian Price — remember, the analyst said “hesitant” — Macdonald said: “I thought he was DECISIVE with his runs.”

Rusty to powerful. Hesitant to decisive. In seven days.

That’s not a coincidence, twelves. That’s what reps do. And Macdonald told us exactly why: “he’s just going to continue to get better now that he’s stacking weeks and he’s getting healthier.”

ARGUMENT FIVE: the elbow — and the analogy here is genuinely clarifying.

“The Drew Lock thing, that’s concerning too. The fact that he’s got an elbow issue — and he did it in a rainy practice a few weeks ago. And as anybody knows who’s had tennis elbow or anything like that, you know that’s annoying. It can hang around for a quarterback. And the fact that he didn’t practice or was limited tells you it’s not normal.”

“It can hang around for a quarterback.”

That tennis elbow comparison is more useful than it sounds. Tennis elbow isn’t a dramatic injury. It’s a nagging one. It doesn’t stop you from functioning — it just hurts every single time you do the one motion your job requires. For a quarterback, that motion is throwing. And you can’t rest it, because resting it means not practicing.

And his read was that “limited” meant “not normal.” He was right about that too.

Drew Lock played the whole game. Macdonald: “He’s a beast. He just went after it.” And he credited the medical staff for the plan.

But Lock came off the field being evaluated for an OBLIQUE.

Macdonald’s words: “We’re looking at him for an oblique. So we’ll assess it and go from there.” And his dry joke: “apparently something with the midsection of our quarterbacks is an issue right now.”

So the analyst was worried about the elbow. The elbow held. And a brand-new injury showed up instead.

And that’s why Macdonald said this about the week ahead: “I’m assuming we’re probably going to have to make a decision on who we’re going to get ready. Drew’s situation, we’ll have to evaluate that as well. It seems like it’s kind of up in the air right now with Drew.”

The quarterback question is still live going into Las Vegas. That’s the real news.

ARGUMENT SIX: does the offense change with Lock instead of Darnold? And this is where they got sharp.

“I don’t think there’s much of a difference between the Drew Lock and the Sam Darnold offense. That’ll be the same. Milroe certainly a different aspect, where you go — okay, we’ve got to worry about more quarterback run stuff, but probably more limited in the dropback pass game.”

And twelves, Drew Lock said the exact same thing himself last week. Asked whether the offense changes with him: “My easy response would say no. Whatever the coaches feel like is the best fit for us against the scheme we’re going against, I’d like to think they put that out in front of us regardless of who’s playing quarterback.”

Analyst and quarterback, same answer. Lock-for-Darnold is a substitution. Milroe-for-either would be a different offense.

ARGUMENT SEVEN: and this is the most interesting theory in the entire segment.

One of them floated a locker-room reason for naming Lock early instead of keeping it a mystery:

“I’ve had coaches say this before too. Kind of just like — we don’t want to dip our toe in the water. We want the whole team to know where we’re at right now. It’s a big game. We’re not playing games. We don’t need the locker room guessing who’s playing quarterback. We don’t need that gossip. This is what we’ve got this week. Here’s our fifty-three. Let’s go play football. I have a feeling that’s kind of what they came to a little bit.”

“We don’t need the locker room guessing who’s playing quarterback. We don’t need that gossip.”

Twelves, I think that’s exactly right, and it explains something we all watched happen.

Macdonald named Drew Lock as the starter on Wednesday. He could have kept it vague. He could have said “we’ll see how the week goes” and gained a competitive advantage by making Arizona prepare for two quarterbacks.

He didn’t. He said “plan is for Drew to start” immediately.

And the reason is the forty-six other men on that roster. A left tackle preparing for Sunday needs to know whose cadence he’s listening to. A receiver needs to know whose ball he’s catching. And every single person in that building needs to not spend Thursday speculating.

Certainty is worth more than deception. Macdonald chose his own locker room over a small edge against Arizona.

And then he went and won seventeen to three. So I’d say the theory holds.

ARGUMENT EIGHT: the Jalen Milroe scouting report — and this is pure gold, because it’s a real evaluator reading from his own pre-draft notes.

“Let’s go back to the binder on Jalen Milroe two years ago. He was my eighty-sixth player. He was just under six foot two, 217 pounds. He ran 4.4 flat at his pro day. So I used a baseball term, which I rarely do — I said he’s an eighty runner.”

“An eighty runner.”

Let me explain that, because it’s the most flattering thing a scout can say.

The twenty-to-eighty scale comes from baseball. Fifty is average. Eighty is the absolute ceiling — the best in the world at that one thing. Scouts almost never give out an eighty. Giving an eighty means “I cannot imagine better.”

And his follow-up: “Like, you’re not going to find a better runner as a quarterback prospect. He gets the corner on everyone when you watch him on outside runs.”

“He gets the corner on everyone.” That means on designed outside runs, Jalen Milroe beats defenders to the edge. Not sometimes. Everyone.

Now the hard part, and he was equally blunt about it:

“His eyes and feet, they’re not connected. He’s up on his toes. The ball sails. He flashed the ability to drive the ball in the seams, but it is a work in progress as a passer.”

Twelves, I want to unpack “his eyes and feet are not connected,” because it’s the single most important sentence ever said about Jalen Milroe’s development.

Quarterback throwing is a chain. Your eyes find the receiver, and your feet have to arrive at the same answer at the same time. The hitch in your drop, the plant, the hip rotation — all of it should be triggered by what your eyes just decided.

When eyes and feet disconnect, you get exactly what he described: “he’s up on his toes, the ball sails.”

Up on your toes means you’re throwing with your arm instead of your body. And when you throw off your toes without your base under you, the ball comes out high. It sails. It’s a wobbling overthrow — the kind that becomes an interception at this level.

But “he flashed the ability to drive the ball in the seams” is the hope. The seam throw — between the safeties, down the middle — is the hardest high-velocity throw in the sport. If he can make it sometimes, the arm is real.

So the full picture on Jalen Milroe: an eighty-grade runner who gets the edge on everyone, with a passing motion that disconnects under pressure. A work in progress with an elite physical trait.

And NOW CHECK SUNDAY: he took a live snap and operated this offense in a division game. Macdonald: “Jalen Milroe too — operating our offense when he took the snap that he took, and being ready to play in case Drew couldn’t go or couldn’t finish.”

Remember what Drew Lock said: “reps for quarterbacks is better than any tape they can watch.” That snap is worth more to Jalen Milroe than a month of scout team.

“A one-man band.”

“If we can limit him to under two hundred yards.”

That’s a backhanded compliment so large it needs its own zip code. The game plan against Seattle is “hold the best receiver in football under two hundred.”

But the “one-man band” part is a real criticism, and it was fair on Monday night.

ARGUMENT TEN: is there a “Ben Johnson blueprint” to beat Seattle? And the answer is smart and reassuring.

“I don’t think so. I think they’re very uniquely situated with the personnel they have. I feel like we had the same question about Kansas City’s offense after what the Seahawks defense did to them — and it was like, well, the blueprint’s out there for the Chiefs now. And I’m like, I don’t know how many teams can do what the Seahawks can do.”

“I don’t know how many teams can do what the Seahawks can do.”

That’s the right answer and the logic is airtight. A blueprint only works if you have the pieces. Chicago beat Seattle with a specific collection of players Chicago has and nobody else does.

But then he named the part that WILL get copied, and this is the actual warning:

“One thing you will see copycatted is the pressure plan that the Bears had, which was on multiple fronts — which was screens. You saw it with every type of screen. Quick screens out to receivers, delay screens, the tight ends, getting backs involved in the screen. They moved the launch point the whole game. They never let Seattle get comfortable with where Caleb Williams was going to be. Just kept moving him around. And then the tempo stuff.”

Screens, moving launch points, tempo.

And twelves, Julian Love confirmed this from the inside. His words about that game: “that forty-yard screen, that was a killer. Good play call.” And: “that freaking third and nine, that screen play just killed all momentum.”

The analyst said screens are the copycat item. The starting safety said the screen was the play that lost the game. Same conclusion, two different rooms.

So THAT is Seattle’s homework, and it’s specific: you will see screens in Las Vegas on Sunday. Count on it.
