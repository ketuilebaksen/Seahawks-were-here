“There’s blood in the water.”

Seahawks Nation, that’s a national analyst describing what Seattle’s defense is about to do to a rookie quarterback this afternoon.

And here’s the collision that makes this game genuinely absurd:

The Las Vegas Raiders have allowed thirty or more points in SIX STRAIGHT GAMES — which ties an NFL record. They are thirty-second in defense. Dead last. They have allowed the most points in the league this season.

And the Seattle Seahawks have been held under thirty points in FIVE STRAIGHT GAMES.

So one of those streaks dies today.

This is James, welcome back to Seahawks Were Here. Game day. Seattle is five and four, the Raiders are in front of us, and this is the single most “something has to give” matchup on the entire schedule. Let’s go.

First, let me tell you what Seattle actually is on defense, because the numbers are better than you think.

According to this national panel: Seattle has the number one pass defense in the National Football League. Somewhere around a hundred and fifty-something yards allowed per game.

Number one. In the league.

And one of them described this defense like this: “Their defense is just unbelievable.” And another went further: “the best two defenses in football are on complete opposite sides of play style trends. You’ve got Seattle — they’re going to line up in essentially the same shell, the same front, a lot of small bodies, a lot of DBs and light backers, and they’re just going to SUFFOCATE YOU WITH SPEED AND A DOMINANT FRONT.”

Twelves, I want to break that description down, because it’s the most accurate scouting report of Mike Macdonald’s defense I’ve heard.

“Essentially the same shell, the same front.”

That sounds like a criticism. It isn’t. That’s the entire design.

Macdonald’s defense shows you the same picture on almost every snap. Same alignment, same look, same depth. And then it becomes seven or eight different things after the ball is snapped.

That is the hardest thing in football for a quarterback. Because your pre-snap read — the thing you were taught to trust — tells you nothing. The picture at the snap is a lie.

And “a lot of small bodies, a lot of DBs and light backers” is the other half. Seattle is built on speed instead of mass. Smaller, faster players who can cover ground, which is what lets you disguise in the first place — because a fast safety can start in one place and end up somewhere completely different.

And then remember what Macdonald himself told us about how he builds the run fits: “your pass responsibility has to take you to your run fit, and vice versa. You have to make sure your guys aren’t going ‘I got this gap, I got that guy.’ That’s a problem.”

Same direction. No conflicts. That’s why a speed-based defense doesn’t get run over.

NOW — who Seattle is playing, and this is the part that makes this game feel strange.

Fernando Mendoza is making his first career NFL start. And he is the number one overall pick in the draft.

And he got the job because Kirk Cousins — who the panel said was playing well — got traded to the Minnesota Vikings. So the Raiders moved on from a veteran and handed the keys to the rookie.

And one of the analysts, a Raiders guy, said it with his whole chest:

“You know what we’ve got for you, Fernando, as a gift? The Seattle Seahawks.”

Twelves, that’s the joke writing itself. A number one overall pick’s first NFL start, at home, against the number one pass defense in football.

But I want to be fair, because he also said something smart right after: “then I realized there was ANOTHER quarterback that started his career with a pretty good game against the Seattle Seahawks that led to a pretty good run there with the Raiders.”

He’s right to flag it. Rookies have had good days against good defenses. Nothing is predetermined.

And his plea to his own fanbase was genuinely classy: “Raider Nation, let’s have a little bit of grace with this, man. Let’s just be patient. They probably didn’t foresee Kirk playing so well and getting traded. He’s going to be thrust into action. There’s going to be highs and lows — let’s just trust the process.”

Good for him. That’s how you handle a rookie quarterback.

But here is the specific problem Fernando Mendoza has today, and the analysts named it precisely.

“Mendoza just needs to play FASTER than what we saw in the preseason. Even though there was a lot to like in terms of his arm strength and willingness to make aggressive throws, there were also moments — and this goes back to college as well — where he HOLDS ONTO THE FOOTBALL A LITTLE BIT TOO LONG. Takes too many sacks.”

And then: “That is not what you want to do against a Seahawks defense, obviously.”

Twelves, that is the worst possible flaw to bring into this specific building.

Let me explain why, because it connects everything.

Seattle’s defense wins by making you hold the ball. Disguise the coverage so the first read isn’t clear. Make the quarterback hesitate for half a second while he figures out what he’s actually looking at. And then the dominant front gets there.

And the analysts described exactly how that cascade works: “that combination can punish hesitation WITHOUT REQUIRING A SPECTACULAR MISTAKE. Hold the ball, lose yardage, face an obvious passing situation — then the defense gets to dictate the next collision.”

That’s the trap, and it’s so clean.

Seattle doesn’t need a strip sack. They don’t need an interception. They just need you to take a seven-yard sack on second down. Because now it’s third and seventeen, and on third and seventeen Mike Macdonald gets to do whatever he wants.

And remember what Macdonald himself said the plan was: “we’ll focus on us making sure we’re playing our brand of ball and TRY TO APPLY AS MUCH PRESSURE AS WE CAN on their offense.”

That’s it. That’s the whole game plan against a rookie. Make him play fast and make him get to his third read.

AND NOW — the counter-argument, because these analysts were honest enough to give it, and twelves, this is the part Seattle should actually worry about.

“The other thing I’d like to see is him putting Fernando ON THE MOVE. If the Seattle defense — I wouldn’t say has a weakness, but there’s been moments where, whether it’s Brock Purdy extending or Caleb Williams buying time, THEY HAVE EXPOSED SOME OF THE VULNERABILITIES IN COVERAGE.”

Brock Purdy and Caleb Williams. Hold that thought.

Because twelves, go look at Seattle’s two worst defensive performances this season. San Francisco, thirty-two to twenty-nine. Chicago, thirty-four to seventeen.

Brock Purdy and Caleb Williams.

That’s not a coincidence. That is the blueprint.

And here’s WHY it works, and it’s the exact flip side of what makes this defense great.

A disguise-based defense depends on timing. The coverage is designed to hold up for about three seconds — long enough for the rush to arrive. Every rotation, every bracket, every safety sprinting to a spot has a schedule.

And when the quarterback escapes the pocket, the schedule dies.

Cooper Kupp explained this from the offensive side weeks ago, and it’s the same idea: “the play really — the second play starts once he gets out of the pocket.”

The second play. The disguise doesn’t exist on the second play. Nobody’s a safety rotating into a bracket anymore. It’s eleven men scrambling to cover grass.

So if the Raiders move Fernando Mendoza on purpose — designed movement, boot legs, half-field rollouts — they’re not asking a rookie to beat Seattle’s coverage. They’re asking him to avoid it.

And Klint Kubiak, who ran Seattle’s offense last year, knows that better than anybody alive.

Which brings us to the real X-factor, and the panel nailed it.

“One thing he has going for him is Klint Kubiak is his play caller. He not only knows that defense very well — I think he’s done a good job of CREATING FIRST READ OPPORTUNITIES for the quarterback. It’s been Cousins all year long.”

“Creating first read opportunities.”

Twelves, that’s a specific coaching skill and it’s the most valuable one you can have with a young quarterback.

It means designing plays where the first man the quarterback looks at is open. Not “he might be open.” Open, by design — because of a formation, a motion, a leverage advantage you created before the snap.

You take the guesswork out. You give the rookie a clear answer on read one, and then you tell him: throw it there and don’t think.

And if Kubiak has been doing that for Kirk Cousins all season, he already has the plays. He just has to simplify the menu.

And here’s the uncomfortable part: Klint Kubiak knows which of Seattle’s disguises are real and which ones are bluffs. He sat in those meetings.

Which is exactly why Macdonald said what he said this week. A reporter started to ask about overthinking it — “he knows I think this way” — and Macdonald cut him off:

“Sure. Yeah. Yeah, for sure. GET IN YOUR OWN WAY. Yeah.”

He named the trap before anybody could fall into it. Play your brand. Adjust live. Don’t try to win a chess match in your own head.

NOW — the other side of the ball, and the panel’s biggest concern about Seattle is one I share.

“My biggest concern for the Seahawks? Outside of JSN — and I’m talking Tory Horton, I’m talking Cooper Kupp — WHO IS GOING TO STEP UP AND MAKE PLAYS?”

And then the line that got me: “When you look at his target share compared to everybody else, it’s CRIMINAL. It is criminal. Somebody else on the outside has to step up and make a play.”

“It’s criminal.”

And they drew the comparison that should worry you: they brought up what the Rams were doing to Puka Nacua — bracketing him, doubling him, erasing him — and said the same thing could be done to Jaxon Smith-Njigba.

Twelves, this is real. Jaxon Smith-Njigba crossed a THOUSAND receiving yards in nine games. He leads the league in target share. And every defensive coordinator left on this schedule knows exactly who to take away.

But here’s my answer, and I’ve got evidence.

They already tried it. Arizona took him away in the red zone — Macdonald said it in his own words: “to put the ball away in the red zone WHEN THEY TAKE JAX AWAY, to have another option down there is really helpful.”

And AJ Barner caught two touchdowns. With an injured hand.

And Drew Lock’s answer this week was the right one: “they want to double Jax, they’ll double Jax. If they want to take him away from a game, then we have other players that are going to get the ball and make it work. It’s our job to RESPOND.”

And on the progression discipline: “our read is our read. If Jax is number one and he’s covered, we go to number two.”

He’s not going to force it. That’s how you keep zero interceptions on the season.

And my favorite line of the week is still Lock quoting his own receiver: “Jax would tell you that if he’s doubled, he’s still open. That’s kind of been the funny line this year.”

AND NOW — the thing both the panel and I agree is the actual key to this football game.

“If there’s a game for them to get on track running AND throwing the ball, it’s this game.”

And: “the key to this one clearly is getting the run game going — because if you’re going to say we’re going to drop back and throw it, THE FRONT IS TOO GOOD.”

Wait — twelves, hold on. That last part was them talking about the Raiders having to run against Seattle. But flip it, because it works both ways, and it’s even more true for Seattle today.

Las Vegas is thirty-second in defense. Dead last. Six straight games allowing thirty-plus.

If Seattle cannot run the football against the worst defense in the National Football League, with a quarterback on a twenty-throw practice week, then that is a problem no coaching can fix.

And the panel credited the men who should do it: “Zach Charbonnet gave them a little juice last week,” and “Jadarian Price out of the backfield gave them some juice catching the football.”

They’re right. And we’ve got it straight from the people who were there.

Drew Lock on Charbonnet: “when CHARBS does his classic ‘you can’t tackle me’ thing and shoves people to the ground, it’s pretty fun to watch. And that’s kind of like a — oh, THERE it is. That’s what we know him as.” And: “the look in his eye when you come back to the huddle. There’s definitely a feel.”

Macdonald on Price: “he has vision, he has burst.” And Cooper Kupp: “he’s electric. He’s a slash runner. He’s decisive, obviously has great hands.”

And Kupp gave us the math that explains why it matters: “you stay ahead of the six, and you’re at second and six — that’s a big difference than second and ten.”

And Drew Lock gave us the lever itself, and twelves, it applies today more than any game all year:

“Run the ball, run the ball — forcing them to get into single high coverages, three or man, and that opens up the pass game. Then you start hitting the pass game, they’ve got to play shell, and you come back to the run. It’s the classic complementary football between run game and pass game.”

That’s the whole afternoon. Run it until Las Vegas drops a safety down. Then throw it over the top to the best receiver in football.

NOW — the playoff picture, and twelves, you need to hear this because it’s sobering.

The panel laid it out: Seattle is five and four. The Rams are five and four. And neither of them is currently in the playoff picture.

Neither one.

“They’re both over five hundred right now at five and four, but they’re not in the race right now.”

And the honest assessment of what it would take: “I mean, you’re PRAYING ON AN NFC NORTH DOWNFALL, right? How do you not have these three guys in your wild card picture right now?”

Three teams at five and four — Seattle, the Rams, and the Cowboys — all on the outside. Because the NFC North is eating all the wild card spots.

And one of them said: “who would have had the Seahawks, Rams, and Cowboys all out of the playoffs?”

Nobody. In August, nobody would have had that.

But here is the good news, and it’s real: Seattle’s remaining schedule is a genuine gift compared to the Rams’.

Their words: “Seattle’s remaining schedule — this week they face the number one overall pick making his very first start, and then the BYE. Before that run to the finish line. Looking out there: Cowboys, Giants... I mean, getting THIS version of the Eagles, the number one team in the NFC East from last year — what a GIFT relative to what the Rams are going to have to go up against.”

“What a gift.”

And the one circled in red: “that first Rams matchup is on CHRISTMAS. That could be a matchup that decides potentially who ends up challenging for that last wild card spot.”

Christmas Day at Lumen Field against the Rams, for a playoff spot. Mark it now.

But twelves — none of that matters if Seattle doesn’t win today. That’s the whole point. A rookie’s first start on the road is the easiest game left on this schedule, and you have to take it.

And here’s the thing the panel said that I think is the truest sentence in the entire segment:

“For the Seahawks, WINNING MAY NOT BE ENOUGH to quiet the concern. This is an opportunity to show their offense can actually help carry the season.”

That’s exactly right. Seattle is supposed to win this game. The question is HOW.

Because: “Zach Charbonnet is credited with providing a spark — but A SPARK IS NOT A FUNCTIONING ENGINE.”

“A spark is not a functioning engine.” I wish I’d written that line.

And one quick note on the Raiders, because they do have one genuine weapon and Seattle has to account for him.

Brock Bowers. And the panel didn’t hedge at all: “he set the record for most tight end receptions in the first three years — and he missed a couple of games.” Is he the best tight end in football? “Yes. It’s not even close. It’s not even close, bro.”

And: “what’s keeping me from throwing it to Brock Bowers? Clearly not the defense.”

So expect Las Vegas to force-feed him. Which is exactly why Mike Macdonald got asked about Nick Emmanwori on tight ends this week — after Emmanwori erased Trey McBride last Sunday.

Macdonald’s explanation: “Nick has the ability to stay CONNECTED at the top of routes. The best tight ends use physicality to create separation, and Nick’s able to either fight fire with fire, or absorb — we won’t call them push-offs, but I have my own opinion about it.”

And the coaching change behind it: “we didn’t rush Nick as much this week.”

Twelves, I’d bet real money that Nick Emmanwori spends this afternoon attached to Brock Bowers. That might be the most important individual matchup on the field.

Now let me give you my prediction, and I’ll show my work.

Seahawks twenty-four, Raiders thirteen.

And I moved off my earlier number — I had twenty-four to twenty — because the more I looked at Las Vegas’s defense, the more I think that twenty was too generous to a unit that has given up thirty-plus in six straight games.

Here’s the reasoning.

Seattle has the number one pass defense in football facing a quarterback making his first NFL start, who has a documented habit of holding the ball too long. Seattle’s defense is specifically built to punish hesitation. That’s a bad pairing for Las Vegas.

And Seattle’s offense — even a limited one, with Drew Lock on roughly twenty practice throws, elbow tendinitis and a back-side oblique — is facing the thirty-second ranked defense in the league.

And Drew Lock said it plainly this week: “this injury is what it is. It’s going to be ready to go by Sunday, and I’m going to go out and PLAY NORMAL.”

Here’s my hedge, and it’s specific: if Klint Kubiak gets Fernando Mendoza on the move, this gets closer than it should. Brock Purdy and Caleb Williams both proved that extending plays is the way to hurt this defense. If Las Vegas builds half of its game plan around designed movement and quick first reads, that rookie could look a lot better than people expect.

And one more: the Raiders are dangerous on kickoff returns, and Macdonald himself put the value of a kickoff stop at “almost like a point.” Five or six kickoffs, four to six points of hidden margin. Watch that unit.

Two questions, twelves. First: which streak breaks today — the Raiders’ six straight games allowing thirty-plus, or Seattle’s five straight games under thirty? My answer is neither, honestly. I think Seattle scores in the twenties and wins comfortably. But if this offense ever hangs thirty-five on somebody, today is the day it’s available.

And second, the one that actually matters to me: is “a spark is not a functioning engine” fair about Zach Charbonnet? I say it was fair last week and it won’t be fair after today. Eight months off an ACL, two games back, and his own quarterback says the look in his eye is back. Against the worst defense in football, I want twenty-five carries and I want to see what happens.
